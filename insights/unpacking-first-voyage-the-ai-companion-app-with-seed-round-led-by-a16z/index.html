Monica Wang Founder, AGI Villa

In December 2025, First Voyage, an AI companion app, closed a $2.5 million seed round led by a16z Speedrun. It had only been seven months since the launch of its product Momo Self Care in May, and the 2 million total tasks created on the platform confirmed early product-market fit (PMF).

What makes this case compelling is not the funding size, but the three fundamental questions it reveals: What happens when two seemingly contradictory sectors intersect? How can product design shift from “providing functions” to “building relationships”? And how can AI deliver tangible user value without drawing attention to its own technical complexity?

Product Logic: Finding Legitimacy Amid Ethical Dilemmas

AI companion products have long faced an unavoidable critique: the ethical risks of emotional dependency. Replika was hit with FTC complaints over alleged emotional manipulation, and a Stanford research paper noted that some AI companion apps had encouraged self-harm and made suggestive comments to minors. While users genuinely crave companionship, the claim “I want an AI friend” lacks social legitimacy. Investors fear ethical backlash, regulators worry about societal impact, and such products struggle to gain mainstream acceptance.

AI companions possess strong capabilities but lack legitimate use cases — this is the core dilemma of the sector. On the other hand, habit-tracking apps have clear use cases but lack emotional warmth. A common flaw of tools like Forest and Habitica is that users abandon habits not due to insufficient features, but out of loneliness: no one is there to stick with them, nor to care if they quit. A 7-day retention rate below 15% is not a design failure; it reflects unmet emotional needs.

First Voyage’s breakthrough lies in using wellness to legitimize AI companionship. When users say “I’m using Momo to build habits,” the app is framed as a wellness tool with high social acceptability. When they actually feel “Momo cares about me,” it fulfills their emotional need for companionship. The product’s key positioning is described as a “self-care pet” rather than an “AI companion” — wellness is the surface justification, while companionship is the underlying essence.

The brilliance of this design is that it shifts accountability from “oneself” to “Momo.” In traditional habit apps, self-discipline is a personal responsibility; quitting only means “I failed again,” with low psychological cost. As clearly stated in its App Store description: “Take care of your pet by taking care of yourself” — caring for the pet means caring for oneself.

This is far from simple gamification. Points, badges, and streaks are external rewards that can be rationally evaluated. What Momo triggers is emotional accountability — the Tamagotchi effect. The 1990s virtual pet toy sold 82 million units not for its functionality, but because users “couldn’t bear to let it die.” People feel a stronger sense of responsibility toward others than toward themselves, especially when that other being depends on them.

User reviews confirm this logic: “having my own little momo helping me with that is extremely helpful, it helps keep me accountable”. The word “accountable” here refers not to the system, but to Momo. When a product transforms from a tool into a relationship, the psychological cost of churn shifts from “wasting money on another app” to “letting Momo down.”

The category distribution of the 2 million tasks further validates this model. Productivity, spirituality, and mindfulness rank top three, ahead of fitness and studying. The “spirituality” category is nearly absent from traditional habit apps, indicating that Momo attracts not efficiency-seeking users in need of supervision, but wellness-focused users seeking mental support. They come not to be managed, but to be understood.

Founder Besart Çopa has explicitly stated, “We’re glad founders are building wellness, not waifus.” This is not a moral stance, but a strategic choice to define product boundaries. Momo is rated 9+ on the App Store with age verification requirements, and TechCrunch reports that it has implemented safety guardrails for content filtering. These design choices show the team has already addressed risks of over-dependency or misuse. The deeper the emotional connection, the higher the risk of dependency — boundaries must be built from day one.

Business Model: Monetizing the Emotional Economy

First Voyage’s monetization model is straightforward: in-app purchases for virtual goods + subscription memberships. The App Store offers nine tiers of virtual currency, from $4.99 “Handful Of Momo Coins” to $199.99 “Hoard Of Momo Coins,” alongside a $39.99 annual membership and a $59.99 lifetime Momo Gold plan.

The logic behind this pricing structure is clear: users pay not for features, but for relationships.

Virtual goods are essentially vehicles for emotional expression. Users earn coins by completing tasks and buy decorations and items to make Momo more endearing. As the founder emphasized: “users reward Momo with care, affection, and cute accessories” — ornaments are carriers of affection.

This model has already been proven in the gaming industry. The skin economies of Genshin Impact and Honor of Kings boil down to one truth: users pay far more willingly for characters they love than for functional upgrades. When attached to a character, improving its appearance is an emotional choice, not a rational calculation. Momo’s nine pricing tiers — topped at $199.99 — prove that a subset of users is willing to invest significantly in this relationship.

The subscription model follows the same logic: lasting companionship requires ongoing payment. What does the $39.99 annual fee deliver? Not more features, but the promise of sustaining the relationship. The product description notes that Gold members unlock “more of Momo’s personality” — this is not a feature upgrade, but a deepening of the bond.

The monetization efficiency of this emotional economy hinges on one key metric: the depth of users’ emotional investment in the character. The 2 million tasks created prove strong engagement, but conversion depends on how many users truly see Momo as a “being worth caring for.” The founder highlights “pixel-perfect animation” not as a technical showcase, but to build the character’s cuteness and realism — visual design directly shapes emotional attachment.

Funds will be used to develop an Android version, a strategically sound choice: validate the emotional economy model on a single platform first, then scale to a larger user base. iOS users have already shown willingness to pay for virtual characters, while Android boasts a larger user base with different spending habits. Refining unit economics (LTV/CAC ratio) on iOS before expanding to Android with funding is a classic growth path for consumer apps.

Yet the business model faces a long-term challenge: will users still need Momo once they successfully build habits? The paradox of utility apps is that the more effective they are, the less users need them. Momo’s response is to position itself not as a “habit-building tool,” but as a “wellness partner.” Its description emphasizes “uncovering more about your virtual pet's personality, as well as your own needs” — aiming to sustain the relationship beyond habit formation by revealing new layers of Momo’s character and users’ deeper needs.

Will this logic hold over time? The wellness app market generated $880 million in revenue in 2024, a 7.3% drop from 2023, signaling retention as a structural industry challenge. First Voyage believes that turning a product into a relationship will reshape its retention curve compared to traditional wellness apps. This hypothesis, however, still needs time to be proven.

Takeaways for AI Entrepreneurs

First Voyage’s case offers valuable guidance for Chinese entrepreneurs exploring the AI application layer.

Category innovation means identifying structural gaps between two sectors. Instead of competing on features within a single vertical, find the intersection of pain points across two fields. The key questions are: What unmet emotional need do users in Sector A complain about? What legitimate use case is missing from Technology B? If Sector A’s gap aligns with Technology B’s strengths, and Technology B’s dilemma is solved by Sector A’s use case, that intersection is an opportunity.

First Voyage uses wellness to legitimize AI companionship, and AI companionship to add warmth to habit management. This approach rests on a simple truth: mature markets are not devoid of opportunities, but contain systematically overlooked structural problems; new technologies are not panaceas, but require appropriate application scenarios.

The core of relationship-centric design is redefining the user role. Most products cast users as “operators” who use tools to complete tasks. First Voyage positions users as “caregivers” who nurture a virtual being. Different roles mean different sources of accountability: operators are accountable to themselves (and easily quit), while caregivers are accountable to another being (and cannot bear to give up).

Yet deeper relationships demand clearer boundaries. The wellness positioning is not just market segmentation, but a product guardrail — giving the app a clear purpose, rather than a replacement for real life. Anyone building deep emotional connections must design boundaries for those relationships, or risks will spiral out of control.

Productizing AI means hiding technology behind character. Momo does not emphasize “cutting-edge AI,” but presents itself as a “tiny ancient spirit.” Users bond with Momo, not with an “AI feature.” The test is simple: can the product still be clearly understood without the word “AI”?

Technical capabilities must be translated into character traits, and functional limits into consistent personality. Momo only engages in wellness-related conversations, and this restraint gives it identity. A chatbot that can discuss everything has no identity. Technical constraints are not limitations, but tools to define character. The best AI product design lets users feel value, not technical complexity.

The validation metric for the emotional economy is how much users will pay for relationships. Virtual goods are not features, but vessels for emotional expression. A user spending $199.99 on virtual currency is not buying items, but fulfilling the emotional need to “make Momo better.” Subscriptions are not feature unlocks, but promises to “keep the relationship alive.”

The 2 million tasks created prove deep engagement, but a sustainable business model depends on balancing conversion rates and user lifetime value. A seed round does not require flawless financials — only proof that “the direction is right”: users are actively and deeply engaged. The data chosen for fundraising reflects how well a team understands its product’s true value.

In an era flooded with generative AI, what is scarce is no longer the ability to generate content, but the answer to “why users care.”

General large models can produce 10,000 wellness suggestions, but only products that truly understand emotional needs know why users will keep coming back. For most AI application entrepreneurs, the future opportunity may not lie in building smarter models, but in deeply understanding the emotional needs of a vertical audience, building irreplaceable relationships,

产品逻辑：在伦理困境中找到"正当理由"

AI陪伴类产品长期面临一个无法回避的质疑：情感依赖的伦理风险。Replika因情感操纵面临FTC投诉，斯坦福研究报告指出AI陪伴应用曾鼓励自残、对未成年人发表性暗示言论。用户确实需要陪伴，但"我想要个AI朋友"这个理由在社会规范里站不住脚。投资人担心伦理风险，监管担心社会影响，产品很难获得正当性。

AI陪伴有能力但缺场景正当性，这是赛道困境。另一边，习惯管理App有场景但缺情感温度。Forest、Habitica这些工具的共同问题是：用户放弃习惯的真实原因往往不是功能不够，而是孤独感——没人陪你坚持，也没人在乎你放弃。7天留存率不到15%不是产品设计问题，而是情感需求没被满足。

First Voyage的突破点是：用wellness给AI陪伴找正当理由。当用户说"我在用Momo养成习惯"时，这是wellness工具，社会接受度高；当用户实际感受到"Momo在关心我"时，这是情感陪伴，满足心理需求。产品描述里的关键表述是"self-care pet"而非"AI companion"——wellness是表层理由，陪伴是底层本质。

这个设计的精妙之处在于：它把责任从"对自己"转移到"对Momo"。传统习惯App里，自律是对自己的责任，放弃了只是"我又没坚持"，心理成本很低。App Store产品描述写得很清楚："Take care of your pet by taking care of yourself"——照顾宠物的方式就是照顾自己。

这不是简单的游戏化。积分、徽章、连续打卡都是外部奖励，可以被理性计算。Momo激发的是情感责任——Tamagotchi效应。1990年代电子宠物卖出8200万台，不是因为功能强大，而是因为用户"不忍心让它死掉"。人对他者的责任感强于对自己的责任感，尤其当这个他者依赖你的存在时。

用户评价证实了这一点："having my own little momo helping me with that is extremely helpful, it helps keep me accountable"——这里的"accountable（负责）"不是对系统负责，而是对Momo负责。当产品从工具变成关系时，用户流失的心理成本就从"又浪费一个App的钱"变成了"我辜负了Momo"。

200万任务的品类分布进一步验证了这个逻辑。生产力、灵性、正念排前三，而非健身、学习。"灵性（spirituality）"这个品类在传统习惯App里几乎不存在。这说明Momo吸引的不是需要监督工具的效率人群，而是需要精神状态支持的wellness人群。他们来这里不是为了被管理，而是为了被理解。

创始人Besart Çopa明确表态"我们很高兴创业者在做wellness而不是waifus（虚拟妻子）"，这不是道德表态，而是产品边界的战略选择。App Store显示Momo设置了年龄评级9+、需要年龄保证，TechCrunch报道提到已经引入safety guardrails（对话过滤机制）。这些设计说明团队已经遇到过用户过度依赖或不当使用的情况。情感连接越强，依赖风险越高，边界必须从第一天就设计好。

商业模式：情感经济如何变现

First Voyage的变现模式很清晰：虚拟商品内购 + 订阅会员。App Store提供了9档虚拟货币选项，从4.99美元的"Handful Of Momo Coins（一把硬币）"到199.99美元的"Hoard Of Momo Coins（硬币宝藏）"，以及年费39.99美元和终身59.99美元的Momo Gold会员。

这个定价结构背后的逻辑是：用户不是在为功能付费，而是在为关系付费。

虚拟商品的本质是情感表达。用户完成任务获得货币，购买装饰和道具，让Momo变得更可爱。创始人的表述很关键："users reward Momo with care, affection, and cute accessories"——装饰品是affection（情感）的载体。

这套逻辑在游戏行业已经被验证过。原神、王者荣耀的皮肤经济本质就是：用户为喜欢的角色付费，心理阻力远低于为功能付费。当你喜欢一个角色时，让它变好看是情感需求，不是理性计算。Momo的9档定价（最高199.99美元）说明：有一部分用户愿意为这段关系投入可观的金钱。

订阅会员的逻辑是：持续陪伴需要持续付费。年费39.99美元对应的是什么？不是解锁更多功能，而是"让这段关系持续下去"的承诺。产品描述里提到Gold会员可以获得"更多Momo的个性揭示"——这不是功能升级，而是关系深化。

这种情感经济的变现效率取决于一个关键指标：用户对角色的情感投入深度。200万任务创建量证明了使用深度，但付费转化率取决于多少用户真的把Momo当成"需要照顾的存在"。创始人强调"pixel-perfect animation（像素级完美动画）"，这不是技术炫技，而是在构建角色的可爱度和真实感——视觉呈现直接影响情感投入。

融资将用于Android版本开发，这个战略选择也很清晰：先验证单平台的情感经济模型，再扩展用户规模。iOS用户证明了愿意为虚拟角色付费，Android用户基数更大但付费习惯不同。先在iOS跑通单位经济模型（LTV/CAC 比值），再用融资扩展到Android，这是典型的consumer app增长路径。

但这个商业模式也面临一个长期挑战：习惯养成成功后，用户还需要Momo吗？工具型产品的悖论是：产品越有效，用户越不需要你。Momo的应对方式是：不把自己定义为"习惯养成工具"，而是"wellness伙伴"。产品描述强调"uncovering more about your virtual pet's personality, as well as your own needs"——试图通过持续揭示Momo的新特质和用户的深层需求，让这段关系在习惯固化后依然有价值。

这个逻辑能否长期成立？wellness app市场2024年收入8.8亿美元，但相比2023年下降7.3%，说明留存是整个赛道的结构性挑战。First Voyage认为：当产品从工具变成关系时，留存曲线会和传统wellness app不同。但这个假设还需要时间验证。

对AI创业者的启示

First Voyage的案例对于正在探索AI应用层的中国创业者具有重要的参考坐标。

品类创新的本质是找到两个赛道的结构性缺口。不要在单一赛道里卷功能，去找两个赛道的痛点交集。关键问题是：A赛道用户抱怨什么情感需求没被满足？B技术缺少什么正当使用场景？如果A的缺失正好是B的能力，B的困境正好被A的场景解决，交集就是机会。

First Voyage用wellness给AI陪伴正当性，用AI陪伴给习惯管理温度。这个方法的前提是承认：成熟市场不是没有机会，而是有结构性问题被系统性忽视；新技术不是万能钥匙，而是需要找到恰当的应用场景。

关系化设计的核心是改变用户角色。大多数产品让用户扮演"使用者"（我用它完成任务），First Voyage让用户扮演"养育者"（我照顾它成长）。角色不同，责任来源就不同。使用者的责任是对自己（容易放弃），养育者的责任是对他者（不忍心放弃）。

但关系越深，边界越重要。wellness定位不是市场细分，而是产品边界——让产品有清晰的使用目的，而不是成为现实生活的替代品。当你选择构建深度情感关系时，必须同时设计这段关系的边界，否则风险会在不可控的时候爆发。

AI能力的产品化是让技术消失在角色里。Momo不强调"最先进的AI"，而是强调"tiny ancient spirit（微小的远古精灵）"。用户和Momo建立关系，不是和"AI功能"建立关系。检验标准是：去掉"AI"这个词，产品还能被清晰理解吗？

技术能力要被翻译成角色特质，功能边界要变成人设一致性。Momo只做wellness对话，这个克制让它有了性格。如果什么都能聊，它就没有身份。技术约束不是限制，而是角色定义的方式。最好的AI产品化是：让用户感受到价值，而不是感知到技术复杂度。

情感经济的验证指标是用户愿意为关系付多少钱。虚拟商品不是功能，而是情感表达的载体。当用户愿意花199.99美元买虚拟货币时，他不是在买道具，而是在为"让Momo更好"这个情感需求付费。订阅不是功能解锁，而是"让这段关系持续"的承诺。

200万任务创建证明了使用深度，但商业模式成立取决于付费转化率和用户生命周期价值的平衡。种子轮融资不需要完美的财务数据，需要的是"方向对了"的证据——用户真的在用，且用得深。选择什么数据去融资，反映了你对产品价值的理解深度。

在生成式AI泛滥的时代，稀缺的不再是生成内容的能力，而是"用户为什么在乎"的答案。

通用大模型可以生成一万种wellness建议，但只有真正理解情感需求的产品，才知道用户为什么会坚持使用。未来对于大多数AI应用创业者的机会或许不在于造出一个更聪明的模型，而在于深入理解某个垂直人群的情感需求，通过构建不可替代的关系，成为那个用户"舍不得卸载"的产品。
