Monica Wang Founder, AGI Villa

This article is compiled from the 2026 World Artificial Intelligence Conference (WAIC) · Sohu AI Frontier Observation.

How does a country with fewer than ten million people continue to produce AI startups built for the global market? Nitzan and Yoel, the Israeli co-founders of Rumata, unpack their “born global” playbook. Nitzan leads customer strategy and go-to-market, while Yoel began his career at an MLOps company later acquired by JFrog, moved into venture capital, and eventually returned to entrepreneurship. 

At the heart of their vision is a simple belief: the future of software will not be a collection of fragmented tools, but one unified place where people can build and work. They have even coined a term for it: “vibe software.” Their goal is to make the entire cloud infrastructure behind vibe coding invisible, so that non-technical users can build software without having to understand what is happening underneath. 

In this episode, we discuss how startups can build trust when entering overseas markets, create an onboarding process that actually works, and design pricing models that align the interests of the company with those of its customers. We also explore the trade-off between standardized products and enterprise customization, as well as how they view the real opportunities for AI collaboration between China and Israel.

Meet the Founders: A Born-Global Startup from Israel

Monica: A lot of people are interested in this topic — how to go global. Today we have two guests from Israel, Nitzan and Yoel. We'll talk about how to build a born-global startup from Israel. Everyone knows Israel is a remarkable place for innovation and for growing startups, and today we'll hear from the two of you how you do it. Let's start from the beginning — please introduce yourselves and tell the audience what your company does.

Nitzan: Sure. I'm Nitzan, CCO and co-founder of Rumata — Chief Customer Officer. I'm responsible for taking Rumata to its users: customer success, sales, marketing, anything customer-related. My past experience was in marketing and customer success in the global market, from Asia to Europe to North America.

Monica: Thank you. Yoel?

Yoel: A pleasure being here — thank you, Monica, for having us. I'm Yoel, CEO and co-founder of Rumata. I have over a decade of experience in software engineering and machine learning models, from before it became such a buzz with generative AI — ranging from different startups to VCs and everything in between.

Rumata is an AI-driven cloud. It's a unified, all-in-one place for people to go from conception and idea all the way to production. We believe the future of software is not in fragmented tools, but in one unified place that knows the whole lifecycle of software.

Monica: And who's your biggest competitor?

Yoel: Good question. As I said, the current landscape is very fragmented. We try to eliminate the need for people to know many different tools — to give them the flexibility of building software the traditional way, with the power of the traditional cloud, but without all that friction. We want to unify it so everything works easily, in one place. So I don't think we have a single, individual competitor — we think of it as a cohesive, all-in-one place.

Monica: No individual competitor — every founder's dream.

The Non-Technical Co-Founder: Customer-First vs. Capability-First

Monica: A quick opening question. I know you're from a non-technical background, Nitzan — same as me. For me, I always "fight" with my partner, who's the CTO. So what do you think your partner can never do?

Nitzan: It's not that he could never do it — it's the instinct of not first thinking about the technology, but thinking about how the customer will experience it. We can do so many things with technology right now; you can show a lot of capability and flexibility in the software. But you also need to think about not overwhelming the customer — you canshow everything, but you don't want to. That's where we sometimes clash: I want to simplify the system for our customers, and he wants to give them everything they could possibly have.

Monica: Very customer-driven, and problem-driven.

Born Global: Going Global Is Not a Choice, It's the Starting Point

Monica: Israeli companies are known for being very good at going global. Can you give us a concrete sense of what Rumata does on market growth?

Nitzan: I was born global — literally. I was born in Silicon Valley during the dot-com bubble, and I grew up around a lot of Israeli startups that went global from day one. In my experience, going global is not a choice; it's something you start from the very beginning of building the product. That's the mindset we came in with. We wanted to speak with communities of developers and builders from all over the world — people building with AI and building in general — to understand how they build, learn from it, and shape the product to fit their needs regardless of physical location.

Monica: The global market is huge. And normally, for an Israeli startup, the first stop is North America. But you seem to see it differently?

Nitzan: The majority do go to North America first. But I feel there are so many untapped markets — the rest of the globe: Asia, Europe. They need these kinds of tools; they face the same problems. So to focus only on North America is sometimes the wrong decision, because you can reach the other markets too. You just need to change the mindset — instead of only approaching the culture closest to home (North America), reach out to other markets that will benefit from the product no less than North America does.

Why China: "We Use the Chinese Models Because They're the Best"

Monica: I'm very curious — why are you here in China, at WAIC? Are you looking for partners, market, or suppliers? What's your interest here?

Nitzan: China is one of the biggest markets in the world — and it's no accident that we use Chinese models in our platform.

Monica: Really? Which models?

Yoel: All of them — GLM, Qwen, Kimi, everyone.

And you asked earlier what the thing is that your partner can do that you can't. Honestly, that wasn't the question on our minds when we started. Nitzan is simply the best at what she does. As entrepreneurs, we're willing to do everything that needs to be done, but we won't necessarily be the best at it. When you form a founding team, that's the mindset: you have to be ready to get dirty, but you also want the best people around you.

As for China — if I still have to name one thing I can't do as well: understanding cultures the way Nitzan does. Sometimes I think one thing, and the person in front of me thinks another, simply because we come from different places. Nitzan bridges that far better than I do.

Monica: And is that true for China specifically?

Yoel: Not only China. As someone born and raised in Israel my whole life, even speaking to Americans or Europeans — let alone people from Asia, which is sometimes further away — there are differences. Nitzan knows how to bridge them.

And speaking of China: everything on this table from my side — my watch, my phone — is Chinese. Not because it's Chinese, but because it's the best for what I need. There's no denying the power of China in technology. The Chinese models in our platform aren't used because they're Chinese; they're used because they're very good. Denying what's going on in China would be lunacy. You have to be here. You have to meet the people, understand the culture, and learn — and I mean learn fromyou, not only the other way around. We're having this conversation so you can learn from us, but I want to learn from you too.

Nitzan: It's more than that. A lot of people globally know about the "power of China," but they don't look at the powerful people insideChina — tech leaders like you, Monica. You're a four-time founder, and we've learned so much from you in the past couple of days. Meeting the people is such a big part of doing business, of learning, and of treating this world as one global community — being humbled and learning face to face.

What Surprised Them Most About China

Monica: You've been here a few days now. What surprised you most about China?

Yoel: Honestly, almost everything — maybe because I didn't know what I was stepping into. Getting inside, meeting such incredible people with so much hospitality, seeing such amazing technologies — I was blown away, in a good way. It was a pleasure and an honor to be here.

Nitzan: Honestly, what surprised me most was that I felt like I'd walked into a sci-fi movie. I walked through WAIC today and saw a person controlling a robotic hand with his mind. You've heard about this stuff, but seeing it live, right in front of your eyes, is a completely different thing.

And the hospitality of the Chinese — I felt at home immediately. You never know when the next plate of fruit is coming, and then there's another variety, and another. That's how you treat your guests.

From ML Ops to Rumata: Lessons from Startups and VC

Monica: I know you built a startup before that was acquired by JFrog, a well-known Israeli company. Later you were on the VC side. How did those different vantage points change how you want to build Rumata?

Yoel: Just a clarification first: I wasn't an investor — I worked as the technical person inside an investment firm.

So I learned a lot from both sides. From building ML Ops infrastructure, I learned the importance of reliability in production — companies relied on us with their "secret sauce," the heart of their business, and every millisecond mattered. That was a very important lesson.

On the VC side, where I saw many startups, I noticed a recurring pattern: many startups face the same core problems — with different hands, but the same core problems. That's actually what enabled us to build what we build (infrastructure and reliability), and it's where the idea came from — from personal experience, and from seeing others struggle with the same things. Nowadays, writing code with generative AI is easy. But managing all the infrastructure — that's what everyone struggles with. When you sit inside a VC that builds and invests in companies, you see how many of them struggle with exactly that, and you say to yourself: there has to be a simpler way.

Making Infrastructure Invisible: Building for Non-Technical Users

Monica: Even "vibe coding" is still a little hard for non-technical people. And in China there are so many one-person companies, many of them non-technical. How do you build Rumata for non-technical people — can they use it comfortably?

Yoel: For sure. We don't change the infrastructure itself; we change the way you interactwith it. We abstract the infrastructure away — it becomes invisible — because that's a real pain point for a lot of people.

Even when code generation works very well, you still need to manage things: going out and getting API tokens, stitching many platforms together so it all works, deployments, production. Many people don't want to build just to build. They don't want to learn all the DevOps — the infrastructure, connections, integrations. They have to do it only because they want to ship a product and serve their clients. So even for non-technical users, building should be a pleasure — that's the mindset — with the infrastructure abstracted away and invisible. And they can achieve the same complex capabilities and features that technical people can, just by talking to our AI.

Nitzan: It can create everything for them — the front end and back end, which is obvious, but also databases, complex workflows, AI agents — and have it all work seamlessly together.

"Vibe Software": Not Just Coding — the Whole Lifecycle

Monica: You call Rumata "vibe software." That term is a bit new to me. I understand "cloud," and I understand a "vibe coding" product. But what is vibe software? How do you define it?

Nitzan: "Vibe software" is really a marketing term, so people can understand what we do in simple words. Everybody now knows vibe coding — that it tries to code for you. Everybody knows the cloud and the infrastructure behind it. But we needed something that says we deliver the flexibilitywith ease. That's where "vibe software" comes in: we're not only building the code with AI, we're building the entire software. For that, we needed people to understand that software isn't just coding — it's the entire development lifecycle that goes into it. So it's a marketing term to explain and simplify our idea.

Monica: So it can solve every kind of problem — data generation, and so on?

Yoel: We try to be agnostic to the specifics. We don't want to be tied to one cloud provider or one AI model. We want to give anyone the freedom to do whatever they want — but the ability to do it all from one place, so they don't need to go hunting around. If they don't care about the specifics and just want it to work, it will work — it automatically chooses the best piece for your needs. And if they dowant control, they have the freedom to use whatever coding agent or development machine they know, and deploy wherever they want. We stay agnostic to that.

Nitzan: For developers, it gives the freedom to choose the tools they already know and integrate them into Rumata. For non-developers, it's as easy as asking a chat how to get somewhere.

Educating the Market & Building Trust: Boots on the Ground

Monica: As Chief Customer Officer, how do you educate your first users and the market?

Nitzan: Education is number one when you're teaching the market something new — especially globally, because every country has its own behaviors and its own initial assumptions. We plan to solve this with a lot of AI-driven tutorial videos on YouTube. And during the building phase itself, if the AI needs something from you, it lets you know with clear pop-up messages and takes action for you once you consent. For example, if the AI needs compaction, it'll ask: "Would you like to compact the chat now? Would you like to move to a new session?" It should be as intuitive as possible — so while you build, you don't have to think about complexity or how to optimize your token usage. You just write.

Monica: So you'll put courses on YouTube?

Nitzan: Not official yet, but yes — courses, plus full documentation behind Rumata.

Monica: One question: Chinese founders are good at cost reduction and capability, but not as good at building trust with global customers, or at onboarding. That's a big challenge for us. Any advice?

Yoel: Time will be the best answer to this, but here's the core: get boots on the ground. Go places. Go to the people, talk with them, be with them — face to face. That builds a lot of trust, and it lets you understand the pain points up close.

Nitzan: So, boots on the ground is the first thing — yougo, as the founder, and learn the culture of the new market you want to expand into. Second, have somebody literally on the ground — someone who speaks the language and knows the customs, so they can call you out. A lot of the time you'll go with your instinct about how a culture behaves, and it's just not the correct way — that happens more often than not. You need someone who can tell you the minute you do something wrong.

Finding the Right Local Partners

Monica: How do you find the right local partners? Any good ideas?

Yoel: It's easier than you think. Take China — you can't ignore it; we're aware of what's going on here, it's everywhere. Just approaching people works. Your technology is amazing, you already have the proof and the background — so approach people.

Nitzan: When you approach a partner in general, look for one who — at least in the beginning — is notreaching for a thousand customers at the same time. You want someone with a close connection to their customers, because that's how you get the best results and the best feedback. And you should reach out with a clear, cost-effective proposition and an obvious return on investment, because that's what partners care about — increasing their ROI. If you show a clear picture that within a month or two of working with you they'll have reduced their cost, people will meet with you.

Then it's on you to — forgive the phrase — put your foot where your mouth is.

Monica: In Chinese we have a saying about that too — if someone feeds you, you have to give something back.

Nitzan: Exactly. You need to prove yourself. But to get the meeting, as long as you show it'll be beneficial to them, they'll take it. You just need to tweak the messaging. Like any marketing strategy: you can send 100 messages and get zero replies, then change one word and suddenly get 20 replies. So for every 20 messages you send, make a small change and you'll get better results.

Monica: So keep iterating — stay a lean company.

Why Israel Punches Above Its Weight

Monica: I'm very curious about the Israeli tech ecosystem. Why does a country of fewer than 10 million people have so many innovative companies?

Yoel: It's a fascinating question, and I can't claim to know deterministically why. But we try to see our size as an advantage. Because of our size, we sort of haveto innovate — and we have to think globally from the beginning, because we can't go domestic-only.

One thing Israel is particularly good at, in my opinion, is infrastructure and developer tools. The reason: once you think internationally, you get harder problems, and dev tools and infrastructure are international by design — because everyone in the world "speaks tech." That's a universal language. So we try to look at our size not as a deficit but as a positive — like the song, Always Look on the Bright Side of Life. How do we get out of the comfort zone and still succeed even when the odds — quote-unquote, "we're small" — are against us?

Monica: So is Israel good at infrastructure, security, and dev tools because you think those areas are the most promising, or just because you happen to be good at them?

Yoel: Again — we had to think globally, and once you do, you need common ground, a common language. Unlike consumer products, those areas are global by definition. Everyone speaks the same language when we talk about security; everyone speaks the same language about infrastructure — there's compute, there's RAM, there's CPU. Those are globally known terms, with no cultural differences. So we tend to go there.

Competitors as Pace-Setters

Monica: For Chinese founders, we often go to different countries and find our competitor is... also Chinese. In robotics, for instance — at WAIC alone there are hundreds of robotics companies making very similar things. When you go to different markets and see Israeli companies competing with each other in the same area, how do you see that?

Yoel: I'm a runner — I love to run sprints — and there's a phenomenon here. When you run alone, you never break a record; you're never as fast as when you have competitors right next to you. You'll stick to someone to keep your pace. So when you have competitors, look at it that way: you can leverage it to push each other forward. In running, you won't get far without competitors — they're your friends underground, competitors up front. That's okay. Push forward.

Pricing: Aligning Interests Across Build and Deploy

Monica: We face a challenge there on pricing — with so many competitors, how do you set the price? A bit higher, a bit lower? What's your thinking?

Nitzan: In general, pricing isn't about the price itself. Too high, and people are reluctant to buy; too low, and people don't trust you — because if there's already a medium price range in the market, going five dollars above or below won't even get noticed. Big trust comes with a big difference.

Our pricing model is different from our competitors' — because, as we said, there isn't one single competitor; we're both vibe coding andcloud. So we separate pricing into the building phase and the deployment phase. In the building phase, we charge for the infrastructure it takes to build your system — the development machine we spin up for you, the cloud, all the infrastructure behind Rumata that runs it — and you buy tokens separately. We don't earn any money from token usage; we pass it directly to the LLM of your choosing. For all we care, you can bring your own LLM subscription into Rumata.

After you finish developing, you move to the deployment phase. That can either stay at the same cost as the build phase, or — if you scale up your organization — we scale with you, so you can expand your cloud capabilities as your company grows.

Yoel: I'd go even further: our interests are aligned with the customer. We want the building phase to be as shortas possible — we want you to get to production with a functioning application serving your clients as fast as possible. We don't benefit from the LLM or the model at all; we take whatever you give us and pay the LLM provider directly, or you bring your own. We charge only for the infrastructure itself. So we want you in production quickly; then you pay for what you use — where your software runs, your processes, data pipelines, databases, front and back end. If you scale up, no problem — scale with us. We don't want you wasting time in the building phase.

Customer Growth: ICP, Communities, and Design Partners

Monica: I'm curious about your customer-growth strategy. In China, some prefer to land one potential-unicorn customer as a branding image; others support many small, early ones. What's your strategy — your ICP?

Nitzan: Right now our ICP is, essentially, people who have a need. We look at small-to-medium businesses at this moment — though we can expand to enterprises, and enterprises can benefit from Rumata too. But we want the market with the biggest need right now, which is SMBs, and especially more traditional businesses that know they need to improve their operations with AI but don't know how, or are too afraid to begin.

Even six years ago, creating a new website or integrating a new system into your company would cost tens of thousands of dollars. Now you can spend $25 and get a killer application — something that never existed before. But a lot of these companies are a bit too afraid to take the leap, or aren't technical enough. That's exactly the gap we want to bridge.

So we're now going back to the communities we visited beforelaunch — the ones we went to in order to learn how they operate — and offering the product to them, because they already have the need and told us where they suffer in their day-to-day operations. Our mentality for selling Rumata isn't cold-calling people who never saw the need; it's reaching the people who feel the pain and want to benefit — plus reaching global partners. With a global partner, you don't need to convince 100 people to use your product; you need to convince one person that your product will benefit their business and their customers' businesses.

Monica: How do you get that one right person — sometimes it's luck?

Nitzan: It can be luck, but it's not onlyluck — it's persistence. You go to people, ask what they need and how you'll improve their business, and you speak to them not as a client but as a design partner. When you treat partners as design partners and let them shape how you build the product, (a) they'll be more inclined to use you once you reach the point they wanted, and (b) they have a lot of friends too, so you get more and more.

Monica: So the first one is really important.

Nitzan: Exactly. Even if it's less cost-effective for you, find that first design partner who truly resonates with the ICP you want — get that customer profile inside the organization, and expand from there.

Standardize vs. Customize: The Unix-Engine Analogy

Monica: For a software provider, many founders struggle with this: your customers are more like professional/consumer users, but sometimes a giant customer offers to work with you — if you customize the software to their needs. You can get money there, but it costs a lot of time. On the other side, offering standardized software to consumers is simpler and closer to what you originally wanted to build. What's your choice? Many founders struggle with this.

Yoel: Very good question — and it's one reason we don't approach enterprises just yet. It can be a big drag, for the reasons you said: you have to focus on what that specific enterprise wants, and that might not apply to other companies. So we chose not to. That's first.

Second, there's a principle — especially in operating systems. You have the core of the OS, like Unix — that's the engine. On top of it you have many different desktops. The engine is the same, but the interface differs according to the client's needs. That's not a theory I invented; it's one I learned — in Linux you have different desktops, but the engine is the same. So we keep that in mind: build the right engine, and it applies to many use cases; then, no problem, create a different desktop — a different UX experience — based on whatever the client needs.

Nitzan: And specifically in Rumata — this may not apply to everyone — we built the software from day one to be as flexible as possible. Not with enterprise in mind, but with our customers in mind: we want to give them the same experience they had with traditional coding and the cloud, but with the ease of AI. They can integrate other databases, other clouds if they wish, while keeping all the infrastructure and operations in one place. So moving toward enterprise, for us specifically, won't be a significant change — it'll be minor tweaks the customer can do with the coding agent themselves, either by implementing code or by asking the AI to do it for them.

Don't Build to Sell — Build to Serve

Monica: Your previous ML Ops company was acquired by JFrog. How long did it take from day one to acquisition?

Yoel: That varies — there's no one right path. For the company I worked at, it took a few years. Others, nowadays, with AI, exit within 10 months. It really varies. I don't think there's a right formula, and I don't think people should chase being sold. That shouldn't be the goal — the goal is to serve a purpose. You don't build something to sell it or exit it; you build something to serve people. Software is a tool that helps people reach a point — medical, agricultural, education, whatever it may be. Software doesn't stand for itself; it's a tool for humanity to achieve other things. The same goes for selling companies: you don't build to sell, you build to help people.

Monica: In Chinese we'd say that's your "original heart" (初心) for building the product. Do you set KPIs for yourself — one year, two years, three years — where you want Rumata to be?

Nitzan: We do set KPIs, but we like to frame them as: how many customers have we helped, and what is the churn? Vibe-coding products right now have massive churn. But because we're cloud, our goal is for people to scale with us, not scale out of our system. So our biggest KPI isn't just how many people try to build something — it's how many take their project into production.

Monica: What do you mean by "into production" — building a real product online, having, say, 100 users?

Yoel: Good question — how do you define what counts as production? At the bare minimum: people don't leave the platform once they're done building; they stay. They don't pay for building — if they don't build, they don't pay. They pay for the production side, for the infrastructure. That's the key: to provide that so comfortably that people are happy to build, and once they're done, our interests are aligned — you continue with us into production, as much or as little as you want. Start small, we serve you small; want to scale up, easy. You don't need to stress about where to host, or "I'm missing a feature, what do I do" — you control everything from one place. That's our biggest metric, because we want to serve customers from day one until they decide otherwise.

China–Israel AI Collaboration

Monica: What do you see as the biggest opportunity for China–Israel AI collaboration?

Nitzan: Honestly, we already collaborate — without China even realizing it. We enable our customers to use Chinese models in our platform. And the fact that we're here today, starting to collaborate with China and understand the Chinese market, is the beginning of that collaboration for us. I feel the collaboration is more present than a lot of people realize. It's not only us who love the Chinese models — we see developers worldwide using them, no less than other models. It really depends on what you want to achieve and which model fits your needs best; at this point, the country of origin doesn't matter.

Monica: I want you to give China a bit more credit — you're already here, be confident.

Yoel: We don't see the country; we see the capability, the technology.

Monica: That's big. Thank you both so much for staying with us tens of thousands of audiences with us today. Enjoy your stay in China.

Nitzan / Yoel: Thank you very much — great food. Thank you.

一个不到千万人口的国家，为什么持续长出面向全球的 AI 创业公司？两位来自以色列初创企业 Rumata 的联合创始人 Nitzan 与 Yoel——一位负责客户与市场，一位创立的MLOps 公司曾被 JFrog 收购、再从 VC 内部转身再创业——把他们"生而全球（born global）"的方法论摊开来讲：为什么"未来的软件不是碎片化工具，而是一个统一的地方"。他们自己造了个词叫 vibe software，把 vibe coding 之上的整套云基础设施做成"隐形"的，让非技术用户也能构建。本期我们就来谈谈出海如何建立信任、跑通 onboarding，定价如何设计才能让公司与客户的利益一致，标准化产品与为大客户定制之间该怎么选，以及他们如何看待中国与以色列之间真实的 AI 合作机会。

Yoel Nisanov

Yoel Nisanov 是Rumata的联合创始人，来自以色列的企业家和技术构建者，拥有超过十年在机器学习、大数据和人工智能基础设施领域的经验。

在他的职业生涯中，他一直专注于让先进技术更易于获取，打造产品和平台，帮助开发者和组织更有效地采用新兴技术。他曾任职于多家初创公司以及以色列一家领先的风险投资公司，并构建了一个被JFrog收购的MLOps平台。

Rumata 是一个由AI驱动的云平台（Vibe Software），它能够像传统云一样强大地构建、部署和扩展软件应用，同时又具备Vibe编码界面的易用性。

Nitzan Mantel

从 MLOps 到 Rumata：创业公司与 VC 带来的经验

Yoel：先澄清一下。我当时并不是投资人，而是在一家投资机构里负责技术相关工作。

所以我确实从两个不同的角度学到了很多东西。在构建 MLOps 基础设施时，我学到的一个非常重要的事情，就是 Production 环境中的可靠性有多么重要。客户把他们的“Secret Sauce”，也就是业务最核心的东西放在我们这里，每一毫秒都很重要。这是一个非常重要的经验。

在 VC 那边，我看了很多创业公司。我发现一个反复出现的模式：很多创业公司虽然表面上做的事情不同，但面对的核心问题其实非常相似，只是由不同的人、以不同形式面对同样的问题。这其实也是为什么我们后来能够构建现在的产品，也就是基础设施和可靠性。Rumata 的想法一方面来自我自己的个人经历，另一方面也来自我看到那么多人重复面对相同的困难。现在有了生成式 AI，写代码已经变得非常容易，但真正管理所有基础设施，仍然是所有人都很痛苦的地方。当你坐在一家同时孵化和投资创业公司的 VC 机构里，看着一家公司又一家公司在完全相同的问题上挣扎时，你自然会想：一定应该存在一种更简单的方式。

让基础设施“消失”：为非技术用户打造产品

Monica：即使是“Vibe Coding”，对非技术背景的人来说仍然有一点难。而且中国现在有大量一人公司，其中很多创始人并没有技术背景。你们如何让 Rumata 真正适合非技术用户？他们能很舒服地使用吗？

Yoel：当然可以。我们并没有改变基础设施本身，而是改变了用户与基础设施互动的方式。我们把基础设施抽象掉，让它变得“不可见”，因为对于很多人来说，这本身就是一个真正的痛点。

即使代码生成已经非常好用，用户仍然需要管理很多事情：比如去申请 API Token，把很多不同平台连接起来，让它们真正能够协同工作，还要做部署、进入 Production 等。很多人并不是为了“开发本身”而开发。他们并不想学习所有 DevOps 知识，不想研究基础设施、各种连接和集成。他们之所以不得不做这些，只是因为他们最终想上线一个产品、服务自己的客户。所以即使对于非技术用户来说，Build 产品也应该是一件愉快的事情——这是我们的核心思维方式——基础设施应该被抽象掉，变成不可见的。用户只需要和我们的 AI 对话，就能够完成和技术人员同样复杂的能力与功能。

Nitzan：它可以为你创建一切——前端和后端当然是最基础的，还包括数据库、复杂工作流、AI Agent 等，而且这一切能够无缝连接、协同工作。

“Vibe Software”：不只是写代码，而是整个软件生命周期

Monica：你们把 Rumata 称为“Vibe Software”。这个词对我来说有点新。我理解 Cloud，也理解 Vibe Coding 产品。但什么是 Vibe Software？你们如何定义它？

Nitzan：“Vibe Software”其实是一个 Marketing Term，它的目的，是让用户可以用简单的语言理解我们到底在做什么。现在大家都知道 Vibe Coding，知道它的目标是替你写代码。大家也知道 Cloud，以及背后的基础设施。但我们需要一个词来表达：我们既提供灵活性，又提供简单易用的体验。所以就有了“Vibe Software”。我们并不只是使用 AI 来写代码，而是在使用 AI 构建整个软件。因此，我们需要让大家理解，Software 并不等于 Coding，软件包含完整的开发生命周期。所以 Vibe Software 是一个 Marketing Term，用来更简单地解释我们的理念。

Monica：所以它可以解决各种问题，包括数据生成之类的？

Yoel：我们希望尽量保持 Agnostic，也就是不被某个具体技术绑定。我们不希望绑定某一个 Cloud Provider，也不希望绑定某一个 AI 模型。我们想给任何人自由，让他们做任何想做的事情，但与此同时，他们可以在一个地方完成所有事情，而不需要到处寻找工具。如果用户根本不在乎底层到底用什么，只希望它能够工作，那它就会自动工作，并根据你的需求选择最合适的部分。如果用户确实希望获得控制权，也完全可以。他们可以继续使用自己熟悉的 Coding Agent 或开发环境，也可以部署到任何想部署的地方。在这些问题上，我们保持中立和开放。

Nitzan：对于开发者来说，它给予的是自由。你可以继续选择自己熟悉的工具，然后把这些工具集成进 Rumata。对于非开发者而言，使用方式则简单得像在聊天窗口里问一句“我要怎么到那个地方？”

教育市场与建立信任：真正到当地去

Monica：作为 Chief Customer Officer，你如何教育第一批用户以及整个市场？

Nitzan：当你需要向市场介绍一个新东西时，Education 是第一位的，尤其是在全球市场，因为每个国家都有自己的行为习惯，也都有自己的初始认知。我们计划通过大量由 AI 驱动的 YouTube 教程视频来解决这个问题。另外，在产品实际 Build 的过程中，如果 AI 需要用户做什么，它会通过非常清楚的 Pop-up 提示用户，并且在获得你的同意以后帮你执行操作。比如，如果 AI 判断需要进行 Compaction，它会问你：“你现在要压缩这段对话吗？你希望开启一个新的 Session 吗？”整个体验应该尽可能直觉化。这样用户在 Build 的过程中，就完全不需要考虑复杂性，也不用自己思考怎么优化 Token 使用。你只需要写就好了。

Monica：所以你们会在 YouTube 上放课程？

Nitzan：现在还没有正式上线，但会的。除了课程，也会有完整的 Rumata Documentation。

Monica：有一个问题。中国创业者通常很擅长降低成本和做产品能力，但在和全球客户建立信任，以及 Onboarding 方面往往没有那么强。这是我们一个很大的挑战。你们有什么建议？

Yoel：最终，时间可能会给出最好的答案。但最核心的一点是：Get boots on the ground。真正去当地。去见那些人，和他们聊，和他们待在一起，面对面交流。这样会建立非常多信任，同时，你也能够非常近距离地理解他们真正的痛点。

Nitzan：所以第一件事情，就是 Boots on the ground。创始人本人要去。你要真正去了解自己想进入的新市场文化。第二，在当地真正找到一个人。这个人要会说当地语言，也了解当地习俗，而且要敢于直接指出你的问题。很多时候，你会凭自己的直觉去判断另一个文化中的人会怎么做，但你的直觉经常是错的。这种情况实际上比你想象中更频繁。所以你需要一个人，在你做错事情的第一时间就直接告诉你。

找到正确的本地合作伙伴

Monica：怎么才能找到合适的本地合作伙伴？有没有什么比较好的方法？

Yoel：其实比你想象中简单。拿中国来说，你根本无法忽视中国。我们知道这里正在发生什么，因为中国已经无处不在。直接去接触人就可以。你们的技术已经非常优秀，也有足够多的成绩和背景证明自己，所以就直接主动联系。

Nitzan：一般来说，当你寻找合作伙伴时，尤其是在刚开始阶段，最好找那种没有同时试图触达一千个客户的人。你希望找到一个和自己客户关系非常紧密的合作伙伴，因为这样你才能拿到最好的结果，也能得到最好的反馈。同时，你需要用一个非常清楚、Cost-effective 的方案去接触合作伙伴，并且让 ROI 一目了然，因为合作伙伴最关心的，就是提升自己的 ROI。如果你能够非常清楚地告诉对方，和你合作一个月或者两个月以后，他们的成本就会下降，那很多人都会愿意见你。

接下来就看你自己能不能——原谅我用这个说法——Put your foot where your mouth is。

Monica：中文里也有类似的说法。别人给了你东西，你就必须给出相应的回报。

Nitzan：对，就是这样。你必须证明自己。但要拿到第一次 Meeting，只要你能够非常清楚地证明，这件事情对他们是有价值的，他们通常都会愿意见你。接下来只需要不断调整你的 Messaging。这和任何 Marketing Strategy 都一样。你可能发了 100 条消息，一条回复都没有，但只要改其中一个词，突然就有 20 个人回复你。所以每发 20 条消息，就做一个小调整，结果会越来越好。

Monica：所以要不断迭代，始终保持 Lean Company 的状态。

为什么以色列总能创造超出自身规模的科技影响力？

Monica：我一直对以色列的科技生态非常好奇。为什么一个人口不到 1,000 万的国家，却有这么多创新公司？

Yoel：这是一个非常有意思的问题。我不能说自己能够非常确定地解释为什么。但我们会努力把“规模小”看成一种优势。正因为国家很小，我们某种程度上不得不创新，同时，我们也必须从一开始就思考全球市场，因为我们无法只服务国内市场。

我个人认为，以色列有几个特别擅长的领域，包括基础设施和开发者工具。原因在于，当你从一开始就用国际视角思考问题时，你会遇到更难的问题，而 Dev Tools 和 Infrastructure 天生就是国际化的，因为全世界的人都“说技术语言”。这是一种 Universal Language。所以我们会努力不把规模小看成劣势，而把它理解为一种积极因素。就像那首歌《Always Look on the Bright Side of Life》。当所谓“我们很小”这样看起来不利的条件摆在面前时，我们如何跳出舒适区，仍然成功？

Monica：那么以色列之所以擅长基础设施、安全和 Dev Tools，是因为你们认为这些领域最有前景，还是只是因为你们刚好比较擅长？

Yoel：还是刚才那个逻辑。我们必须全球化思考。一旦开始全球化，就需要寻找一种 Common Ground，一种共同语言。和消费类产品不同，这些领域本身就是全球性的。当我们讨论 Security 时，全世界使用的是相同的语言。讨论 Infrastructure 时也是一样。Compute、RAM、CPU，这些都是全球通用的概念，没有什么文化差异。所以我们自然更容易进入这些领域。

竞争对手，也是帮你提速的人

Monica：对中国创始人来说，我们经常到了不同国家以后发现，我们的竞争对手……还是中国公司。比如机器人。光是 WAIC 上，就可能有几百家机器人公司在做非常相似的事情。当你去不同市场，也看到以色列公司在同一个领域彼此竞争时，你怎么看待这种现象？

Yoel：我喜欢跑步，而且特别喜欢跑短距离冲刺。跑步里有一个现象。如果你一个人跑，通常很难打破纪录。当身边有竞争对手的时候，你往往会跑得更快。你会盯着某个人，跟住他的节奏。所以当你有竞争对手时，也可以这样理解。你可以利用竞争推动彼此前进。在跑步里，没有竞争对手，你很难真正跑得远。他们在背后是你的朋友，在前面是你的竞争者。这完全没问题。继续往前冲就好。

定价：让 Build 与 Deploy 阶段的利益保持一致

Monica：但竞争这么多，也会带来一个挑战，就是 Pricing。面对这么多竞争对手，你们怎么定价？稍微高一点，还是稍微低一点？你们怎么思考这个问题？

Nitzan：一般来说，Pricing 并不只是价格本身的问题。价格太高，人们会不愿意买；但价格太低，人们也可能不信任你。因为如果整个市场已经形成一个中间价格区间，你比别人贵 5 美元或便宜 5 美元，很多人根本不会注意到。真正影响信任的，是明显的差异。

我们的 Pricing Model 和竞争对手不太一样，因为就像刚才说的，我们没有一个单独的竞争对手，我们既是 Vibe Coding，也是 Cloud。所以我们把价格分成 Build 阶段和 Deployment 阶段。在 Build 阶段，我们只对构建系统所需要的基础设施收费，包括我们为你启动的 Development Machine、Cloud，以及 Rumata 背后所有运行相关的 Infrastructure。Token 则单独购买。我们不会从 Token 使用中赚任何钱，这部分费用会直接支付给你自己选择的 LLM。对我们来说，你甚至完全可以把自己的 LLM Subscription 带进 Rumata。

完成开发以后，就进入 Deployment 阶段。这个阶段的成本可以和 Build 阶段保持一致。如果你的 Organization 开始规模化，我们也会跟着你一起 Scale。随着公司的成长，你可以不断扩展自己的 Cloud 能力。

Yoel：我甚至会再往前说一步。我们和客户的利益其实是完全一致的。我们希望 Build 阶段越短越好，希望你尽可能快地进入 Production，让一个真正能工作的 Application 开始服务你的客户。我们不会从 LLM 或 Model 本身获得任何收益。你使用什么，我们就直接把费用支付给对应的 LLM Provider，或者你直接带自己的 Subscription 进来。我们只对基础设施本身收费。所以我们希望你尽快进入 Production。进入 Production 后，你根据真正使用的东西付费——你的软件运行在哪里、Processes、Data Pipelines、Databases、Front End、Back End 等。如果你要 Scale，没有问题，和我们一起 Scale。我们不希望你在 Build 阶段浪费时间。

客户增长：ICP、社区与 Design Partner

Monica：我很好奇你们的客户增长策略。在中国，有些公司会希望先拿下一家潜在独角兽客户，把它当作 Branding Case；另外一些公司则会支持很多规模比较小、比较早期的客户。你们的策略是什么？你们的 ICP 是谁？

Nitzan：目前我们的 ICP，简单来说，就是“真正有需求的人”。现阶段，我们主要关注中小企业。当然，我们未来也可以拓展到 Enterprise，而且 Enterprise 同样可以从 Rumata 中获益。但我们现在更希望优先服务需求最强烈的市场，也就是 SMB，特别是比较传统的企业。这些企业知道自己需要通过 AI 改善运营，但不知道应该怎么做，或者因为害怕而迟迟不敢开始。

即使在六年前，企业想做一个新网站，或者把一套新系统集成到公司里，可能要花几万美元。而现在，你可能只需要花 25 美元，就能做出一个非常厉害的 Application。这是以前完全不存在的事情。但很多公司还是有一点害怕跨出这一步，或者本身缺乏技术能力。这正是我们希望弥合的 Gap。

所以现在，我们正在重新回到 Launch 前接触过的那些社区。当时我们进入这些社区，是为了学习他们怎么工作。现在我们开始把产品提供给他们，因为他们已经有真实需求，而且他们此前已经告诉过我们，自己日常运营中到底哪里最痛苦。我们销售 Rumata 的思路，并不是去 Cold Call 那些从来没有意识到自己存在需求的人，而是找到真正已经感受到痛苦、也希望从产品中获益的人。同时，我们也会接触全球合作伙伴。如果你找到一个 Global Partner，就不需要说服 100 个人分别使用你的产品，你只需要说服一个人，让他相信你的产品能够改善他的业务，也能够改善他的客户的业务。

Monica：那怎么才能找到最关键的那一个人？有时候是不是纯粹靠运气？

Nitzan：有时候确实有运气因素，但绝不仅仅是运气，更多的是 Persistence。你不断去找人，问他们需要什么，以及你如何改善他们的业务。你不要把他们仅仅当作 Client，而应该把他们当作 Design Partner。当你把合作伙伴真正当作 Design Partner，让他们参与塑造你的产品时，第一，当产品最终发展到他们想要的状态，他们会更愿意真正使用你的产品；第二，他们也有很多朋友，于是你会不断获得更多客户。

Monica：所以第一个客户真的非常重要。

Nitzan：完全正确。即使从短期成本上看并不一定最划算，你也应该找到第一个真正与目标 ICP 高度匹配的 Design Partner。把那个 Customer Profile 真正带进你的 Organization，然后从这里开始扩展。

标准化还是定制化：Unix Engine 的类比

Monica：对软件公司来说，很多创始人都会面对一个问题。你们的客户更多可能是专业用户或者消费级用户，但有时候，突然来了一家很大的客户，说愿意和你合作，但前提是你必须按照他的需求 Customize 软件。这样可以赚到钱，但会消耗大量时间。另一边，是继续给消费者提供 Standardized Software，这件事情更简单，也更接近你一开始真正想做的产品。你们会怎么选择？很多创业者都会在这里纠结。

Yoel：非常好的问题，而且这也是为什么我们暂时还没有重点进入 Enterprise 市场。它确实可能成为非常大的拖累。正如你刚才说的，你必须把大量精力集中在某一家 Enterprise 的具体需求上，但这些需求可能对其他客户完全没有价值。所以我们的选择是暂时不做。这是第一点。

第二，有一个原则，在 Operating System 里尤其常见。你有一个 OS 的核心，比如 Unix，它就是 Engine。在这个 Engine 上面，你可以有很多不同的 Desktop。底层 Engine 是一样的，但 Interface 可以根据不同客户需求而变化。这不是我自己发明的理论，是我学来的。比如 Linux 有很多不同的 Desktop，但底层 Engine 一样。所以我们一直记住这个原则：先构建一个正确的 Engine，让它能够适配大量不同 Use Case。在此基础上完全可以根据客户需求，做不同的 Desktop，也就是不同的 UX Experience。

Nitzan：另外，具体到 Rumata，这一点可能不是所有公司都适用。我们从第一天开始，就把软件设计成尽可能 Flexible。这并不是因为当时就想着 Enterprise，而是因为我们从客户需求出发。我们希望让用户既拥有传统 Coding 和 Cloud 那种自由和能力，又拥有 AI 带来的简单体验。如果用户愿意，他们可以集成其他 Database，也可以连接其他 Cloud，同时继续在一个地方管理全部 Infrastructure 和 Operations。所以对 Rumata 来说，未来进入 Enterprise 市场并不需要特别大的改变，可能只需要一些很小的调整。而且客户甚至可以自己通过 Coding Agent 完成这些调整，要么直接写代码，要么直接让 AI 帮他们完成。

不要为了出售公司而创业，而是为了服务用户

Monica：你之前参与的那家 MLOps 公司后来被 JFrog 收购。从第一天到最终被收购，大概用了多久？

Yoel：每家公司都不同，没有唯一正确的路径。我参与的那家公司用了几年。现在在 AI 时代，也有一些公司 10 个月就 Exit 了。差异非常大。我不认为存在一个正确公式，而且我认为，人们也不应该把“被收购”本身当作追逐的目标。这不应该成为创业的目标。真正的目标应该是 Serve a purpose。你不是为了卖掉公司或者 Exit 才去 Build 一样东西，你应该为了服务人而构建产品。Software 本质上只是一种 Tool，它帮助人类到达某个目标，这个目标可能在医疗、农业、教育，或者其他任何领域。Software 并不是为了 Software 自身而存在的，它只是帮助人类完成其他事情的工具。卖公司也是一样，你不应该为了卖掉它而构建它，你应该为了真正帮助别人而构建它。

Monica：中文里，我们可能会说这就是你打造产品的“初心”。你们会给自己设 KPI 吗？比如一年、两年、三年后，希望 Rumata 发展到哪里？

Nitzan：我们确实会设 KPI。但我们更喜欢这样定义：我们帮助了多少客户？Churn 是多少？现在很多 Vibe Coding 产品的 Churn 都非常高。但因为我们同时是 Cloud，所以我们的目标是让客户和我们一起 Scale，而不是 Scale 到一定程度以后就离开我们的系统。所以我们最大的 KPI 并不仅仅是多少人尝试用 Rumata Build 一个东西，真正重要的是：有多少人最终把自己的 Project 带进了 Production。

Monica：你说的“进入 Production”具体是什么意思？是把一个真正的产品放到线上，比如已经有 100 个用户？

Yoel：很好的问题。到底怎样才叫 Production？最基本的一个标准就是：用户在完成 Build 以后，并不会离开这个平台，而是继续留下来。他们不会因为 Build 本身付费。如果不 Build，就不会为 Build 付钱。真正付费的是 Production 这一边，是 Infrastructure。这才是关键。我们希望把这个过程做得足够舒服，让用户很开心地完成 Build。完成以后，我们双方利益完全一致。你继续和我们一起进入 Production。你想小规模使用，就从小规模开始，我们就按照小规模服务你。你想 Scale Up，也非常简单。你不需要焦虑：“到底应该把产品 Host 在哪里？”“我缺了一个 Feature，现在怎么办？”所有东西都可以在一个地方控制。这是我们最大的 Metric，因为我们希望从客户第一天开始，一直服务到他自己决定不再继续为止。

中以 AI 合作机会

Monica：你们认为，中国和以色列在 AI 领域最大的合作机会是什么？

Nitzan：说实话，我们其实已经在合作了，甚至中国自己可能都没有意识到。因为我们的平台已经允许客户使用中国模型。而今天我们来到这里，开始和中国合作，也开始真正理解中国市场。对我们来说，这就是合作的开始。我觉得真实存在的合作，比很多人意识到的更加广泛。不只是我们喜欢中国模型，我们看到全世界的开发者都在使用中国模型，使用程度并不比其他模型低。最终还是取决于：你到底想实现什么目标？哪一个 Model 最符合你的需求？发展到今天，模型来自哪个国家已经没有那么重要了。

Monica：我希望你们能多给中国一点 Credit。人都已经来到这里了，要更有自信一点。

Yoel：我们看到的不是国家。我们看到的是 Capability，是 Technology。

Monica：这句话很重要。非常感谢你们两位今天和我们数万名观众一起进行了这么长时间的交流。希望你们享受接下来在中国的行程。

Nitzan / Yoel：非常感谢。还有非常好吃的食物。谢谢。
