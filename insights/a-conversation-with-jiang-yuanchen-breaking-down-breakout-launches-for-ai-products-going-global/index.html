An AI product launches. Its video gets hundreds of thousands of views, and the comments look lively. Yet it generates only a dozen registrations. Why?

After managing more than 40 international product launches, Momentum founder Jiang Yuanchen has found that many AI teams think a launch means “making a video and asking a few influencers to share it.” But the factors that determine the outcome often come before launch day: Has product-market fit been validated? Who is most willing to pay? Can the website, registration process, product experience, and payment journey handle a sudden influx of traffic?

In this episode, we break down the process from preparation through launch day and the following two weeks: how to know whether a product is ready to launch, how to choose between a founder video, motion graphics, and a commercial, how to develop positioning and select channels, and how to prevent a high-profile launch from becoming a fleeting burst of fireworks.

If you are preparing an AI product for international expansion, an initial go-to-market campaign, or its first overseas launch, this conversation will help you rethink the process. A launch is a coordinated growth campaign requiring the product, content, distribution, and operations teams to work together.

Introduction

First, a principle: a launch should amplify validated product-market fit, rather than serve as the experiment that establishes it.

Many teams hope a major launch will “show us how the market responds.” But if the product hasn’t reached enough early users, and the team hasn’t identified the people and use cases with the highest willingness to pay, the team and production studio have little to rely on beyond ideas discussed in a meeting room.

The video may look beautiful, and influencers may deliver hundreds of thousands of views. But if visitors don’t register or pay, traffic quickly disappears at the top of the funnel.

Second, positioning may determine 40% to 50% of a launch’s success.

Someone scrolling quickly through X won’t spend several minutes understanding a feature list. The opening seconds of the video and the first sentence of the post must immediately explain what the product is, what problem it solves, and why it matters to that person.

Helena originally wanted to position itself as an “AI CMO.” But many of its target users were CMOs, who might find that label threatening. The team ultimately changed the positioning to “The First Autonomous AI Marketer,” shifting from replacing the CMO to helping marketing teams complete work automatically.

Third, the first hour after publication is the most important window of the entire launch.

A mature influencer campaign doesn’t have 300 people copy the same sentence at the same moment. It uses a “1–3–6” structure: approximately 10% from the founder’s core network, 30% from highly relevant influencers whose audiences match the ideal customer profile, and 60% to broaden reach and sustain attention.

The founder’s friends, investors, board members, and industry partners create genuine engagement in the first 20 to 30 minutes. Relevant influencers then explain the product’s value, followed by broader-reach creators who introduce it to larger audiences.

Fourth, a launch video should ideally communicate one central creative idea and one or two key selling points.

Technical founders often want to include every feature they spent months building. The audience finishes the video thinking the product is impressive, but doesn’t remember what it does.

A video isn’t a product manual. Its job is to make the right people click and take action.

Fifth, a launch creates a spike; ongoing operations build the growth curve.

The two weeks after launch usually remain a favorable window. Teams should keep repurposing assets, replying to comments, publishing use-case content, building the founder’s public presence, deploying influencers in stages, and bringing user feedback back into the product.

If launch day is a fireworks display and all marketing stops the next morning, even substantial attention can quickly fade to nothing.

Jiang Yuanchen: Hello, everyone. I’m Jiang Yuanchen, founder of Momentum and the creative studio LucidEdge.

Our main business is helping AI software and hardware products launch internationally.

A complete launch usually includes a launch or demo video, supported by influencer promotion, PR, Product Hunt, media coverage, and other distribution activities.

We started in commercial production. What we do now is somewhat like a traditional advertising agency for the AI era.

We can make a talking-head video featuring the founder. We can also create a larger production with actors and a director—a full TV commercial, or TVC.

If the founder doesn’t want to appear on camera, or the team wants to emphasize aesthetics and artistic expression, we can use motion graphics alone, like the Kimi K3 launch video people saw a few days ago.

Those are our three main formats. We have been doing this for nearly two years.

Why Founder-Led Videos Often Convert Best

Monica: Which of those three formats works best?

Jiang Yuanchen: For conversion, having the founder appear on camera usually works best.

Relatively few Chinese teams use that approach. But across the 30 or 40 projects we have managed, founder videos generally perform better, both in direct conversion and in business results when combined with influencer promotion.

For example, we worked on Blockit. Within six hours of launch, it generated 1,214 registrations.

Its product and conversion journey were already well developed. Around one-sixth of those users entered their credit card details immediately and became paying customers.

Normally, organic traffic brought it around 10 registrations and one paying customer a day.

I wouldn’t necessarily say launch-day business increased 300 times, but 100 to 200 times its usual level is a reasonable estimate.

Monica: Why do some founders prefer not to appear on camera?

Jiang Yuanchen: First, building a founder’s public profile over the long term requires considerable investment.

Many founders have purely technical backgrounds and want to focus on the product rather than continually invest time in a personal brand.

Others are introverted and uncomfortable in front of a camera. Some teams choose a member who is more willing to present instead.

Monica: In those situations, do you still encourage the founder to appear?

Jiang Yuanchen: We judge that through the initial conversations with the client.

During the creative pitch and positioning consultation, the first issue is positioning, particularly the script. We usually spend one or two weeks developing the narrative.

Only after settling the narrative and product positioning do we choose the most appropriate format.

If the business objective is to acquire registrations quickly and use those users’ feedback to improve unfinished parts of the product, we usually recommend a founder-led approach.

If the team wants stronger brand aesthetics and already has some market visibility—for example, an established following on X—we lean toward content that upgrades its brand presentation.

The same logic applies to a TVC.

For a software product launching for the first time with no existing market presence, we generally don’t recommend a TVC unless the budget is sufficient and the team wants richer storytelling and brand depth, rather than prioritizing short-term registrations.

Overall, for a first launch, if the founder is willing, we usually recommend a founder-led talking-head video first.

“The Launch Doesn’t End When the Video Is Finished”

Monica: Do you continue helping with operations or a series of founder-branding activities afterward, or do you focus only on the launch?

Jiang Yuanchen: We usually focus on the launch itself.

But we have seen clients execute a beautiful launch and then stop investing.

They don’t cut the launch video into shorter content or continue social media activity, so traffic quickly falls back.

They have created momentum but fail to sustain or amplify it. That is a real missed opportunity.

Monica: How many launches have you done so far?

Jiang Yuanchen: Including this month, around 42.

Monica: Of those 40-plus launches, how many would you consider genuinely successful?

Jiang Yuanchen: Around 27 or 28.

Some of the others, particularly projects from our early days, didn’t produce ideal business results.

Our work opens the top of the funnel. But why do many AI products fail to handle the traffic or convert it after launch?

Some campaigns have attractive distribution numbers—hundreds of thousands of views—but when we review results with the team, actual conversions are very low.

A common reason is that the product wasn’t tested with early users before launch.

The team simply wanted a louder first step in its go-to-market strategy, or rushed to publish.

Without early-user validation, the best use case, or a clear ideal customer profile, we lack real evidence too. We can only work from the assumptions on both sides, which makes mistakes much more likely.

We generally don’t work that way now.

The product should first be validated by early users, with higher-converting use cases identified and clarity about who is most willing to pay. Then we have greater confidence in taking on the project and planning the next steps together.

For very early products still in development, we usually won’t immediately undertake a large-scale launch, though we offer whatever advice and assistance we can.

There are three core questions.

First, has product-market fit been validated? Do the product’s features genuinely match commercial demand?

Second, what are the best ideal customer profile and use cases? Which users are most willing to pay?

Third, is the conversion journey smooth?

That journey needs systematic, continuous improvement, from social content to the website, landing page, freemium model, ongoing payment, and retention.

Many products launch before those pieces are ready because they want attention or speed.

We encountered that in projects late last year and the year before. With more experience, we now discuss it thoroughly with clients in advance.

Otherwise, the market sees the product and PR goals are achieved, but the business doesn’t grow. We feel we haven’t truly helped the client.

After 40-Plus Launches, What Counts as Success?

Monica: Do you have a clear benchmark? What would make you call a launch successful?

Jiang Yuanchen: We don’t stop working immediately on launch day. We continue reviewing data with the client.

After several hours, how many clicks, registrations, and payments have there been?

Metrics differ by category, so we use common market benchmarks as a reference.

If a video gets hundreds of thousands of views but fewer than 10 conversions, that is clearly weak.

Blockit was a strong example. Initial clicks were in the tens of thousands, leading to 1,214 registrations within six hours, followed by around one-sixth becoming paying users immediately.

We consider results like that healthy.

Of course, nobody can guarantee a breakout hit. What we can do is try to keep performance consistently above a healthy baseline.

Whether it truly takes off depends more on the product and the maturity of the conversion journey.

An agency can’t fully control those factors unless it becomes more deeply involved in the product and business, like a consultancy.

Why Turn a Calendar into a Chessboard?

Monica: Could you break down the Blockit case? From the first contact, how long did it take, and what did you do?

Jiang Yuanchen: It is a good example of our complete process.

The founder was previously a partner at Sequoia and later started his own company.

The product automates calendar management and is particularly suited to investors and founders.

It helps users organize things such as picking up children, board meetings, and sales meetings. Over time, it learns their habits, even which café they prefer on Monday mornings.

The ambition is to become part of the user’s entire personal operating system and plan their schedule intelligently.

After receiving the brief, we prepared creative proposals, much like a traditional advertising agency.

The creative director usually develops four or five big ideas.

The chosen concept came partly from the client, and we refined it: a calendar as a chessboard.

That is how the video opens.

Moving one piece affects the entire board, while some commitments can’t move at all, such as a board meeting or taking a child to school.

Users need to arrange their schedules to make their whole lives work better, just as chess players consider which moves create the best overall position.

After agreeing on the concept, we developed the full script over roughly two weeks.

The filming itself was quick.

We brought the founder to a suitable location, set up the cameras, and worked with the production team. Principal photography took around half a day.

Most of the remaining work was motion graphics, color grading, and editing.

From preparation to delivery, the project took a little over 20 days—close to a month.

Monica: Where is the founder based?

Jiang Yuanchen: In the Bay Area.

He was a Sequoia partner before starting the company.

A couple of days ago, I mentioned this case to another prospective client, who suddenly told me the founder had once been his client. It really is a small world.

Monica: Do you have local filming partners?

Jiang Yuanchen: Yes. We have production partners in several regions, mainly Shanghai and Shenzhen in China, along with regular partners overseas.

Local teams handle filming. We mainly handle the creative development, script, and some post-production.

Why Did One Launch Take Four Months?

Monica: After the video is finished, do you also participate in promotion on Product Hunt, X, and other channels?

Jiang Yuanchen: When we align on business goals early on, some clients make it clear that they need substantial visibility—perhaps several million impressions.

To make that more predictable, we usually use influencer promotion as a core method. Influencers are now a relatively reliable accelerator.

Our packages are fairly standardized, generally around 15K to 20K. Larger campaigns may reach approximately 25K. We design the specific plan around the target impressions.

The video can be used widely: on the website, X, LinkedIn, as the launch video on Product Hunt, or directly in paid ads.

If the client needs it, we also propose Product Hunt and PR plans.

Monica: From preparing a month in advance to monitoring conversion afterward, how long do you usually stay involved?

Jiang Yuanchen: Our longest engagement lasted around four months.

It began with a relatively large TVC project.

The initial budget was over RMB 300,000, but additional content brought the final amount above RMB 400,000.

Scheduling, post-production, color grading, and multiple revision rounds took more than two months.

Then came PR, media outreach, Product Hunt, and influencer coordination. From the first conversation to launch, it was nearly three months.

Afterward, we continued checking in weekly to understand business progress.

We don’t usually manage long-term operations ourselves, but if a client needs that, we work with partners or agencies run by people we know.

That helps prevent a situation where the client’s marketing or operations team can’t handle the traffic after launch.

Monica: When clients want an expensive, large-scale production, where does most of the budget go?

Jiang Yuanchen: Hardware clients are more likely to invest in large productions, especially for Kickstarter.

A TVC has become almost standard and is one of their core communication assets.

Shenzhen has a fairly mature industry around this.

Software companies usually begin considering TVCs around the pre-Series A or Series A stage. At seed stage, the expense can be difficult.

A polished production with a complex script and detailed execution may cost RMB 600,000 or 700,000.

AI Can Generate Ideas. Why Can’t It Replace Motion Designers Yet?

Monica: Where do you use AI in your launch workflow?

Jiang Yuanchen: In scripting and early creative development.

We have essentially embedded AI into a traditional advertising agency workflow.

The creative director and assistants think independently about client needs, but also use AI to generate ideas.

They may select three to five directions from 100 or 200 ideas.

Doing all of that mentally used to be exhausting. AI improves efficiency significantly, potentially improves quality, reduces costs, and increases client satisfaction.

We also use AI in the video itself.

If a client can’t afford certain live-action shots, or we think a section would work better generated by AI, we create those segments and blend them into live footage or motion design.

Those are the main uses now.

I would like to automate as much as possible, but client communication, team management, and collaboration account for around 30% to 40% of our time. Those are difficult to automate fully.

Monica: Which models do you mainly use for video generation?

Jiang Yuanchen: We have many overseas team members, particularly European motion designers, who previously used Veo 3 frequently.

As Seedance improved, they began using it too. But Veo 3 was more established in their workflow, perhaps mainly out of habit.

We continue testing new models.

For motion graphics, people often discuss Remotion and HeyGen’s Hyperframe.

We have tested incorporating them into our workflow, but currently they mostly produce draft-level motion graphics.

Getting something attractive enough for commercial delivery is still difficult with fully automated tools.

Usually, we still use Adobe After Effects and create it manually, frame by frame, which takes considerable time.

My estimate is that we may need another year or two.

Recent tests also showed that, especially with motion graphics, a scene may take considerable effort to generate, only for a client’s revision request to require regenerating it.

Sometimes manual adjustments are faster. So we still rely mainly on human production.

Monica: Are most of your motion designers in Europe?

Jiang Yuanchen: Yes.

Europe has a mature motion design industry and training system. Some people start learning in high school.

Producing a high-quality launch video generally requires five or six years of industry experience.

Why Did Helena Abandon the “AI CMO” Positioning?

Jiang Yuanchen: Helena launched on a Monday morning, received 3.6 million views across channels, and produced strong business results.

It received more than 9,000 demo requests, and website traffic increased tenfold.

The first element was timing.

Service providers like us maintain schedules to avoid launching multiple projects on the same day and competing for attention.

We generally decide in advance whether to launch this week or next.

A reliable standard window is Tuesday between 8 and 10 a.m. Pacific Time.

If we know a competitor will launch that day, we may move to Monday or Wednesday.

Second is positioning.

During the first one or two weeks, we discuss the brief, script, and positioning carefully with the client.

Helena is an AI go-to-market tool.

Initially, the team wanted to follow a much-discussed product called Okara and use “AI CMO.”

That concept resonates with independent developers and technical founding teams because they often lack professional marketers.

But Helena’s actual features made it a go-to-market tool for CMOs—effectively their junior colleague or assistant.

Positioning it as an AI CMO could make its target users think it was intended to replace them.

The team therefore rebranded it as an “Autonomous AI Marketer,” moving from a potentially threatening label to something more acceptable to the target audience.

In my experience, around 40% to 50% of a launch’s success depends on how positioning is communicated.

People scroll quickly on X. The opening seconds and post copy must immediately explain what the product is and what problem it solves.

Imagine a New York founder getting up, drinking coffee, and checking X.

Can the product address a persistent pain point strongly enough to make them visit the website and register?

Positioning has a major influence.

The filming itself is more systematic. Once the script is settled, production and motion graphics can be relatively standardized.

In the First Hour, How Should 300 Influencers Participate?

Jiang Yuanchen: Another important step is driving distribution after publication.

Influencer promotion is a mature approach.

To reach millions of views, you might use 100 or 200 creators, or more.

One easily overlooked resource is the founder’s own network.

Overseas founders often have an advantage here. They may know industry leaders, shareholders, board members, fellow founders, and investors.

Having those people engage during the first 20 to 30 minutes helps organic reach and introduces the content to larger audiences.

If we include them in the influencer system and have 300 distribution participants, we generally use a “1–3–6” ratio.

The “1” is 30 people from the founder’s network, who participate first.

After half an hour comes the “3”: 90 relevant creators, including major and mid-sized accounts whose audiences closely match the ideal customer profile. They quote-post or comment.

The remaining “6,” or 180 creators, expand the audience toward the end of the first hour and keep attention going over the following week.

Their audiences don’t need to match as precisely as those of the first 90.

Their main purpose is broader reach and helping the post move into a larger distribution pool.

After that initial push, distribution relies mainly on X’s recommendation system.

Helena and many of our current launch campaigns use a similar approach.

Monica: What is the ideal posting schedule?

Jiang Yuanchen: Launch day, particularly the first hour, matters most.

The goal is to build momentum.

Ideally, most core influencers participate within that hour, creating a concentrated push.

Afterward, scheduling depends on the product team’s needs.

If the team has follow-up activity, we may reserve some creators for the second, third, or fourth day.

Overall, the first hour and the first few hours are the golden window. Concentrating most activity on launch day usually works better.

Monica: Should the first wave of influencer posts be consistent or personalized?

Jiang Yuanchen: Personalized.

One reason we emphasize the timing window is to work with the platform’s organic distribution.

Some influencers use entertaining quote-posts.

When Helena launched, one creator used a sad scene from The Godfather with the caption “Marketing Agency after this launch,” suggesting the product might take some agency work.

That quote-post itself received many views.

X encourages interesting content, including content with some controversy.

Cluely previously released a video about using AI to assist with dating. Intense arguments in the comments continually signaled attention to the platform, and it ultimately received 16 million views.

But if the campaign prioritizes business conversion and user acquisition, we still focus first on communicating product value accurately, rather than letting the content drift just to attract views.

“The Biggest Danger Is Looking Sophisticated Without Explaining What the Product Does”

Monica: Launch videos vary greatly.

The chessboard example is closer to a creative commercial. But many technical founders want to show the complete workflow and emphasize technical capability and a developer aesthetic.

What do you recommend?

Jiang Yuanchen: Different clients have different needs.

If a technical founder wants a serious introduction, we try to translate product value into market-facing language and build the script around it.

A standard format is the founder explaining the product clearly, supported by motion graphics and product footage.

Teams making their first launch video often encounter the same issue: they have many features and use cases and want to include everything.

That easily becomes a presentation for themselves rather than the audience.

People on X don’t have enough attention to absorb all that information.

We now prefer one or two central selling points per video. Two is already the upper limit.

Ideally, as with Blockit, there is one central creative idea and one or two key scenarios.

Sometimes a full demonstration isn’t necessary. A few clear sentences from the founder are enough.

Avoid too many transitions and messages.

The worst result is that viewers think the product looks sophisticated and polished but don’t remember what it does or why it matters to them.

Without Product-Market Fit, Why Can More Traffic Mean More Waste?

Monica: Why do some launches get strong traffic, influencer participation, and hundreds of thousands or millions of views, yet disappointing business results?

Jiang Yuanchen: First is product-market fit.

Unless the only goal is to seize a timing opportunity and achieve PR without considering registrations or conversion, the preparation needs serious work.

Product and operations teams should use waitlists, outreach, user interviews, and observation in Reddit and Discord communities to establish PMF.

A solid result gives a large campaign a stronger foundation.

If PMF isn’t established, we also find ourselves asking during production: Will anyone actually pay for this positioning?

Second, get enough early-user feedback before the formal launch.

Through outreach, offline events, friends, or investor introductions, find an initial group—perhaps around 100 users.

Early products inevitably need feature changes and adjustments to landing-page messaging. Those should reflect real feedback.

Reddit communities can help online. If a founder is good at building in public, their public presence can also be a strong acquisition channel.

Third, refine the conversion journey before launch: landing page, sign-up, aha moment, payment, and retention.

Ideally, interview those early users and run A/B tests until conversion reaches a level the team considers acceptable.

Benchmarks differ by category.

But getting those steps to a reasonable standard before launch provides much greater confidence in the outcome.

Blockit’s 1,214 registrations within six hours, with around one-sixth paying immediately, were possible because the team had already spent months refining PMF, early-user validation, and conversion.

“A Launch Is a Coordinated Campaign”

Jiang Yuanchen: PMF, early users, and the payment journey are work the product team should ideally complete before launch.

That preparation makes an enormous difference.

Only then comes launch execution.

A launch is a campaign, almost a coordinated battle, with launch day particularly important.

An ideal schedule is to complete those three foundations, then spend around a month planning the launch.

Positioning still comes first.

Helena’s shift from AI CMO to “The First Autonomous AI Marketer” may have determined 40% to 50% of the result, including whether users remembered the product and felt it addressed their needs.

Then come channel selection and influencer strategy.

Different products fit different channels.

AI productivity tools, especially B2B products, usually focus on X and LinkedIn.

Consumer lifestyle agents may be better suited to Instagram and TikTok.

For technically oriented products aimed at independent developers, GitHub can be a strong PR and growth channel.

Product Hunt may drive fewer direct conversions than before, but it remains valuable for exposure and PR.

A well-executed campaign can generate millions of impressions, and media outlets look there for stories.

There is no universally best channel, only the best fit for the product.

We generally use the “1–3–6” influencer structure and concentrate the main traffic push on launch day.

If the product team needs continued activity, we reserve some creators for the following week.

“Don’t Let a Launch Become a Fireworks Display”

Monica: Do you usually choose either Product Hunt or X, or use both?

Jiang Yuanchen: It depends on the budget and product fit.

For a highly technical product, developing a serious presence on GitHub may produce better conversion than investing in Product Hunt.

The key is aligning with the client’s business goals.

The final piece is sustaining the traffic afterward.

Influencers, video, and PR generate a large spike, but you shouldn’t simply let it disappear.

There is usually another two-week window to reinforce positioning, build recognition, and find new growth opportunities.

A common approach is repurposing assets.

If the main video performs well, cut it into shorter content and create posts around individual scenarios.

Publish separately the points the founder wanted to communicate but couldn’t include in the main video.

Keep some influencers for later in the first week, and maintain comment replies and community activity.

Beyond that comes longer-term social media work.

Use the attention to build the founder’s public presence. Consumer products can test paid advertising. Reddit can become a long-term channel.

The official brand account and social content also need ongoing attention.

We see a launch as a concentrated push that starts a growth flywheel and serves the product’s long-term development.

If there is no follow-up, it becomes a fireworks display. Once the traffic disappears, new users stop coming.

The full journey should extend from launch to conversion and retention, then build lasting content and word of mouth that support further growth.

If the First Launch Fails, Does Renaming and Relaunching Help?

Monica: Some products launch, underperform, and then launch again later, sometimes with a new name. Does that work?

Jiang Yuanchen: If the launches aren’t too far apart, several consecutive spikes can still help.

At least for PR, they repeatedly remind the market that the product exists.

But long-term acquisition and sustained growth still require the ongoing work we discussed.

I remember Aha Creator having a good launch.

Around July last year, I think the product was called HeadAI.

Its positioning was clear: “Your AI Marketing Manager.”

Few products used that positioning at the time, so it established the concept in people’s minds.

Combined with a series of promotional activities, I thought it was a successful launch.

Why “Replacing a Role” Remains an Effective Conversion Story

Monica: We see roughly three types of positioning for AI products.

First, replacing a role, such as AI HR.

Second, efficiency—how much faster or more productive it is.

Third, an entirely new category, such as “the world’s first” product of a particular kind.

How do these compare in exposure, conversion, and user acceptance?

Jiang Yuanchen: Based on our experience, “replacing a role” tends to generate relatively stable business because it addresses a concrete need.

An AI CMO aimed at developers or technical teams responds to a real problem.

They don’t want to spend heavily hiring an expensive marketing team that takes time to integrate.

If a capable SaaS tool helps do that work, the cost-saving value is clear, so conversion tends to be relatively high.

Creating a new category, such as “The World’s First,” often has stronger PR impact and is more likely to go viral.

People see something new, and distribution numbers may look excellent.

But conversion may be less direct and tangible than with the first approach.

From the user’s perspective, they first need to understand what they are seeing on X.

If “The World’s First” is followed by a string of difficult concepts, they may find it interesting without knowing why it matters to them, then quickly scroll past.

If the Product Looks Almost Identical to Competitors, What New Story Can You Tell?

Monica: Suppose a product looks almost identical to competitors, even down to the interface. How do you find selling points and a narrative?

Jiang Yuanchen: That is common now, particularly in crowded categories.

Sometimes the differences really are little more than interface colors and branding.

Positioning then requires greater care.

We may need two or three weeks, or longer.

Our creative director provides positioning consultation and becomes deeply involved, like a traditional consultancy.

We have a package covering several modules: brand identity, the website user journey, the impression the company wants to make, Q3 and Q4 business goals, competitors, and desired differentiation.

Helena faced competition from Okara and other AI go-to-market tools.

Finding a new route into the market required deep product understanding and sometimes several meetings a week with the founder.

Small tests may also be necessary.

Before launch, the team can publish content testing different aesthetic and positioning directions, using user-generated content or smaller creators to observe which approach resonates.

Small tests help avoid large mistakes.

As Software Gets More Crowded, Is Hardware the Next Opportunity?

Jiang Yuanchen: I have recently worked with some hardware products.

If demand is well established, the supply chain is strong, and the category is novel enough, hardware may be easier to make stand out.

Software is genuinely crowded.

Some clients told us in follow-up conversations that many similar products appeared immediately after their launch because software’s barriers to entry are generally lower.

Hardware could be a good direction, but demand must be solid.

Spending heavily to finish a product and only then discovering negative market feedback can cause major losses.

My work focuses on marketing, so I pay particular attention to AI tools that help with SEO, advertising, and marketing.

DTC brands and Chinese cross-border sellers actively try such tools and often have relatively high willingness to pay.

As building AI SaaS becomes easier, competition will increasingly shift toward distribution, positioning, and marketing.

It is somewhat like competition in Chinese e-commerce: how do you actually sell the product, explain its benefits, and reach real users?

Personally, I am more optimistic about AI tools that help users market their products.

Helena, for example, helps users make money. That proposition is generally easy for the market to accept.

How Does a 23-Person Launch Studio Operate?

Jiang Yuanchen: Someone asked about team structure.

Including contractors, we have around 23 people.

The creative director is central, supported by two creative assistants.

Those three handle the scripts and ideas for all videos.

The creative director also leads early positioning consultations.

He spent five or six years at an American advertising agency and has focused on AI product positioning and launches over the past two or three years.

Professional teams handle filming and motion graphics.

We have long-term production partners in Shanghai, Shenzhen, and the Bay Area, which makes coordination efficient and delivery more reliable.

We also work with partners specializing in post-launch growth.

Someone asked whether I am optimistic about agents.

I don’t think I have enough experience to judge like an investor. I’m not Zhu Xiaohu, so I shouldn’t casually declare which sectors I favor.

Reddit, meanwhile, is already a highly specialized field.

Whether on China’s Zhihu or overseas Reddit, professional teams have established methods for improving marketing results and managing campaign ROI.

As platform enforcement becomes stricter, this work will depend even more on specialists.

Marketing itself is highly specialized, with increasingly detailed responsibilities across channels.

Does a Launch Video Have a Fixed Formula?

Monica: Is there a standard creative framework? For example, what should happen in the first three, five, or 10 seconds, and in the final five or 10 seconds?

Jiang Yuanchen: We have five frequently used formats internally, though the specific methods are part of our internal process.

Overall, the client’s commercial goals determine the approach.

If the campaign isn’t aiming for a huge viral hit and instead wants initial users plus some visibility, a common structure is problem-solving: start with the pain point, introduce product features, and finish with a call to action.

That is familiar to many people.

一个判断：Launch 不是用来验证 PMF的，而是用来放大已经得到验证的 PMF。很多团队希望借一场大规模发布“看看市场反应”。但如果产品此前没有接触过足够多的种子用户，没有找到付费意愿最高的人群和场景，团队和制作方能依靠的就只剩下会议室里的想象。视频可以拍得很漂亮，达人也可以带来几十万播放，但用户进来之后不注册、不付费，流量只会在漏斗的第一层迅速流失。

第二，产品定位可能决定一场 Launch 成败的 40% 到 50%。用户在 X 上快速滑动时，不会花几分钟理解你的功能列表。视频最开始几秒和帖子第一句话，必须让他立刻明白：这是什么、解决什么问题、为什么和我有关。Helena 原本想把自己定义为“AI CMO”，但真正的目标用户中有很多 CMO，这个名字反而会让他们感到被威胁。最终，团队将定位改为“The First Autonomous AI Marketer”，从“取代 CMO”变成“帮助市场团队自动完成工作”。

第三，发布后的第一个小时，是整场 Launch 最重要的时间窗口。成熟的达人推广不是让 300 个人在同一时刻复制同一句话，而是按“1—3—6”的结构分层：约 10% 来自创始人自己的核心关系网络，约 30% 是与 ICP 高度匹配的精准达人，剩下 60% 用来扩大覆盖和延长热度。创始人的朋友、投资人、董事和行业伙伴先在前 20 分钟到半小时内形成真实互动，精准达人随后解释产品价值，泛流量达人再把内容推向更大的流量池。

第四，一支 Launch Video 最好只讲一个核心创意，以及一到两个核心卖点。技术型创始人经常希望把几个月开发出来的所有功能都塞进一条视频里，结果用户看完只觉得“很厉害”，却完全没记住产品到底是做什么的。视频不是产品说明书。它的任务，是让最正确的那批人产生点击和行动。

第五，Launch 只是一次脉冲，后面的运营才是增长曲线。发布后的两周通常仍然处在红利窗口。团队需要继续拆解素材、回复评论、发布场景内容、运营创始人 IP、分批使用达人，并把这一波用户反馈重新带回产品。如果发布当天像放了一场烟花，第二天所有运营都停止，那么再大的声量也很快会归零。

做过 40 多场发布，什么才算真正成功？


Monica：你有没有一条比较明确的判断标准？达到什么程度，你会认为一场 Launch 成功了？

姜元晨：现在发布当天，我们不会立刻结束服务，而是会持续与客户核对数据。例如发布若干小时后，产生了多少点击、多少注册、多少付费。不同品类的指标并不完全相同，我们会参考市场上较为常见的业务数据来判断。如果视频获得了几十万次播放，最终却只有不到十个转化，这显然是非常低的结果。刚才提到的 Blockit 就属于表现非常好的案例。最初进入的点击流量有数万，6 个小时内带来了 1,214 个注册，之后又有约六分之一直接转为付费用户。现在我们会把类似结果视为比较健康的指标。当然，爆款很难保证，我们能够做的是尽可能让项目稳定在一条健康基准线上。至于能否真正爆发，更依赖产品本身，以及后续转化路径是否已经打磨成熟。这不是乙方能够完全干预的部分，除非我们像咨询公司一样，更深地介入产品和业务。

一张日程表，为什么被讲成一盘棋？


Monica：能不能具体拆解一下 Blockit 这个案例？从最初接触产品开始，整个 Lead Time 有多长，分别做了哪些工作？

姜元晨：这个案例基本能够代表我们的完整 Process。创始人曾经是红杉合伙人，后来出来创业。产品是一款日程自动化管理工具，特别适合投资人和创业者使用。它可以帮助用户管理哪天接孩子、哪天开 Board Meeting、哪天和客户开 Sales Meeting，也会逐步了解用户的生活习惯，甚至知道你每周一早上最喜欢去哪家咖啡厅。它希望深入用户的整个生活操作系统，帮助用户智能规划日程。客户找到我们以后，我们首先根据 Brief 完成创意提案，类似传统广告公司的 Idea 提报。创意总监通常会提出四到五个 Big Idea。这个案例最终采用的创意，一部分也来自客户，我们在此基础上进行了完善：把日程表比作一盘棋。片子开头就是这个场景，每移动一颗棋子都会影响全局，有些事项则根本无法移动，例如 Board Meeting 或送孩子上学。用户需要思考如何安排这些日程，才能让整个生活达到最优状态，就像下棋时要判断移动哪些棋子，才能获得最好的全局结果。创意确定以后，我们再延伸成完整脚本，大约用了两周。拍摄本身反而很快，我们请创始人到合适的场地，把机位架好，配合摄影团队，大约半天就完成了主体拍摄。之后主要是后期动效、调色和剪辑。整个项目从前期到交付，大约用了 20 多天，接近一个月。

Monica：创始人常驻在哪里？

姜元晨：在湾区。他以前是红杉合伙人，后来出来创业。前两天我接触另一位客户，谈到这个案例时，对方突然告诉我，这位创始人以前就是他的客户。确实会让人感到世界很小。

Monica：你们在当地也有合作的拍摄团队吗？

姜元晨：有。我们在多个地区都有合作的拍摄团队，国内主要在上海和深圳，海外也有固定合作方。具体执行由当地团队完成，我们自己主要负责前期创意、脚本和部分后期工作。

一场 Launch，为什么跟了四个月？


Monica：视频完成并上线以后，Product Hunt、X 等渠道上的运营推广，你们也会参与吗？

姜元晨：在前期对齐商业需求时，如果客户明确提出这一波必须做出较大声量，例如希望达到数百万次 Impression，为了提高确定性，我们通常会把达人推广作为核心手段。达人现在相当于一个比较稳定的加速器。我们的 Package 也相对固定，通常在 15K 到 20K；如果规模更大，可能会做到 25K 左右，再根据目标 Impression 设计具体方案。视频的应用场景很广，除了官网、X 和 LinkedIn，也可以作为 Product Hunt 的首发视频，甚至直接用于广告投放。如果客户有需求，我们也会提出 Product Hunt 和 PR 方案。

Monica：从提前一个月开始准备，到发布后观察转化，你们通常会跟进多久？

姜元晨：我们跟进时间最长的客户大约持续了四个月。前期制作的是一支 TVC，属于较大的项目，最初预算为三十多万元，后续不断增加内容，最后做到了四十多万元。制作周期本身很长，包括排期、后期、调色和多轮修改，仅这一部分就用了两个多月。之后还要推进 PR、媒体联络、Product Hunt 和达人安排，从第一次沟通到正式 Launch，接近三个月。发布以后，我们又继续跟进，每隔一周与客户联系一次，了解业务进展。我们现在通常不负责长期运营，但如果客户有需要，也会和合作伙伴或朋友的 Agency 一起继续承接，避免一场 Launch 结束以后，产品自身的 Marketing Team 或运营团队无法有效接住后续流量。

Monica：客户希望制作高成本、大制作视频时，预算通常主要花在哪里？

姜元晨：目前硬件客户投入大制作的情况更多，尤其是准备上线 Kickstarter 的产品，TVC 基本已经成为标配，也是最核心的传播资产之一。深圳在这一领域已经形成比较成熟的产业。软件公司通常到了 Pre-A 或 A 轮阶段，才会开始考虑 TVC；如果仍处于种子轮，这笔投入会比较有压力。要做到真正有质感，而且脚本和细节较为复杂，预算可能达到六七十万元。

AI 能做创意，但为什么还替代不了动效师？


Monica：在你的 Launch 工作流中，哪些环节在使用 AI？

姜元晨：脚本写作和前期创意都会使用。我们有点像把 AI 嵌入传统广告公司的工作流程。创意总监和助理团队除了根据客户需求独立思考，也会利用 AI 辅助生成创意。从一两百个 Idea 中筛选出三到五个方向，过去完全依靠人脑会非常辛苦，现在效率提高了很多，质量反而可能更好，成本也有所下降，客户满意度会更高。视频本身也会用到 AI。如果客户无法承担某些实拍成本，或者我们判断某段内容用 AI 生成效果更好，就会直接生成部分片段，再融合进实拍或 Motion Design 中。目前主要应用在这些环节。我也希望尽量实现自动化，但客户沟通、团队管理和成员协作大约占工作时间的 30% 到 40%，这些很难完全自动化。

Monica：如果使用 AI 生成视频，你们目前主要用哪些模型？

姜元晨：团队里有不少海外成员，尤其是欧洲的动效师，他们以前使用 Veo3 比较多。Seedance 质量提升以后也会用到一些，之前成熟工作流里Veo3使用得更多，可能主要是团队习惯。最近出现的一些新模型，我们也在持续尝试。动效方面，大家经常讨论 Remotion 和HeyGen的Hyperframe，我们此前也测试过如何将它们融入工作流程，但现阶段更多只能生成草稿级别的动效。要真正达到好看、可以商业交付的水平，仍然很难完全依靠自动化工具，通常还是要用 Adobe After Effects 一帧一帧手工制作，时间成本很高。我判断可能还需要一到两年。最近实际测试以后发现，尤其是动效，一幕内容好不容易生成出来，客户只要提出修改，往往就需要重新生成。相比之下，手工调整有时反而更快，所以目前我们还是以人工制作为主。

Monica：你的动效师主要在欧洲吗？

姜元晨：对。欧洲有比较成熟的 Motion Design 产业和培训体系，有些人从高中阶段就开始学习。一般要达到高质量 Launch Video 的制作水平，通常需要五六年的行业经验。

Helena 为什么放弃了“AI CMO”这个定位？


姜元晨：Helena 在一个周一早晨发布，全网获得了 360 万次播放，业务结果也很好，收到了 9,000 多个 Demo 申请，官网流量真正增长到了原来的十倍。它的打法中，第一点是时间窗口。我们这些服务方内部会有类似的时间表，尽量避免多个项目集中在同一天发布、相互争抢流量，通常会提前规划在本周还是下周上线。比较稳定的常规发布时间，是太平洋时间周二早上 8 点到 10 点。如果提前知道当天会有竞品发布，我们会调整到周一或周三。第二点是定位。前期一到两周，我们会和客户仔细讨论 Brief、脚本和产品定位。Helena 属于比较常见的 AI Go-to-Market 工具。最初团队想参考一个很受关注的产品 Okara，主打“AI CMO”。这个概念很容易击中独立开发者和技术背景创始人团队的需求，因为他们的团队里通常缺少专业的市场运营人员。但结合 Helena 的实际 Feature，它本身是一款给 CMO 使用的 Go-to-Market 工具，相当于 CMO 的 Junior 和助理。如果定位成 AI CMO，目标用户很容易认为它是来取代自己的。因此那边重新进行了 Rebrand，将它定义为 Autonomous AI Marketer，也就是自动化 AI 营销助手，从一个可能令人产生防备的“AI CMO”，切换到更容易被目标用户接受的方向。以我的经验来看，一场 Launch 是否成功，大约有 40% 到 50% 取决于产品如何表达定位。X 上用户的浏览速度非常快，视频前几秒以及帖子文案所传达的定位，必须让人迅速理解这个产品是什么、能够解决什么问题。比如一位纽约创业者早上起床、喝咖啡、刷 X 时，产品能否恰好击中他长期存在的痛点，并促使他进入官网完成注册？定位的影响非常大。相比之下，拍摄本身更偏工程化，脚本确定以后，拍摄和后期动效都可以相对标准化。

发布第一个小时，300 个达人该怎么出场？


姜元晨：接下来一个重要环节，是视频发布后的推流。现在比较成熟的方式，通常是通过达人推广。如果希望做到百万量级，可能会使用一两百位，甚至更多达人。一个容易被忽略的点，是 Founder 自己的 Network。海外创始人在这方面通常更有优势，他可能认识行业大佬、股东、董事、创业者朋友和投资人。在发布后的前 20 分钟到半小时，由创始人自己的 Network 先参与传播，对后续 Organic 流量以及进入更大流量池非常有帮助。如果把这些人也纳入达人体系，假设一共有 300 个传播节点，我们通常采用“1—3—6”的配比。“1”是 30 位 Founder 自己的 Network，最先参与；半小时后进入“3”，也就是 90 位更头部或中腰部、与 ICP 高度匹配的精准博主，由他们进行 Quote 或 Comment；剩下的“6”，也就是 180 位，负责进入更大的流量池，在发布一小时末尾以及后续一周持续加热。他们的受众画像不需要像前面 90 位那么精准，可以更泛一些，主要目的是扩大覆盖，帮助帖子进入下一个更大的流量池。完成这一轮推动以后，后续就主要依赖 X 自身的推荐机制。Helena 以及我们自己现在操盘的一些 Launch Campaign，基本都采用类似打法。

Monica：达人的发布节奏，最理想的安排是什么？

姜元晨：发布当天，尤其是第一个小时最重要，核心是先把 Momentum 建立起来。最好在发布后一小时内，让大部分核心达人完成传播，集中形成势能。后续还要根据产品方的需求来安排。如果团队还有后续运营动作，我们会把一部分达人放在第二、第三或第四天，持续进行加热。总体而言，第一个小时以及当天最初几个小时是黄金窗口，大部分达人集中在当天效果更好。

Monica：第一轮达人发布的内容，是保持一致更好，还是个性化更好？

姜元晨：个性化更好。我们之所以强调窗口期，很大一部分原因也是为了更好地匹配平台的 Organic 机制。有些达人会用更有趣的方式来做 Quote。Helena 发布时，有一位达人使用了《教父》里的悲伤画面，配文是“Marketing Agency after this launch”，意思是这个产品一发布，可能会抢走一部分 Agency 的工作。那条 Quote 本身也获得了很高的播放量。X 平台会鼓励有趣、甚至带有一定争议的内容。比如之前 Cluely发布了一支用 AI 辅助约会的片子，评论区争议非常激烈，不断向平台传递“很多人正在关注”的信号，最终获得了 1,600 万次播放。当然，如果项目更偏业务转化，目标是获得用户，我们仍然会优先准确传达产品价值，而不会单纯为了流量让内容过度发散。

“最危险的，是看完觉得高级，却不知道产品有什么用”


Monica：现在很多 Launch Video 的风格差异很大。像棋盘的案例，更偏广告片和创意表达；但很多技术型创始人更希望完整展示产品工作流，突出极客感和技术能力。你们通常会怎么建议？

姜元晨：不同客户的需求不一样。如果技术型创始人希望用比较严肃的方式介绍产品，我们会尽可能用市场化语言表达产品价值，并以此为核心设计脚本和拍摄。比较标准的形式，是由创始人直接出镜，清楚讲解产品功能，再配合动效和产品画面。第一次做 Launch 或广告片的团队，往往会遇到同一个问题：产品有很多功能和场景，因此希望在一支片子里全部讲完。这很容易陷入自说自话。尤其在 X 上，用户没有足够多的注意力来消化所有信息。我们现在更倾向于让一支片子只传达一到两个核心卖点，两个已经是上限。最理想的状态，像 Blockit 一样，围绕一个核心创意，配合一两个核心场景；有时甚至不需要完整演示，由创始人用几句话讲清楚就足够。要尽量避免切换太多、表达太多。最糟糕的结果，是用户看完后觉得产品很高级、很有质感，却完全没有记住它到底做什么、对自己有什么价值。

PMF 没跑通，为什么流量越大越可能浪费？


Monica：为什么很多 Launch 做出去以后，流量数据很好，达人也参与了，获得了几十万甚至数百万次播放，但业务结果仍不理想？

姜元晨：第一是 PMF。除非只是为了快速抢占时间窗口、纯粹完成 PR，不考虑注册和转化，否则前期一定要下足功夫。产品团队和运营团队需要配合，通过 Waitlist、Outreach、用户访谈，以及在 Reddit、Discord 社群中观察用户，先把 PMF 做扎实。获得一个比较 Solid 的结果以后，再开展大规模 Campaign 会更有把握。假如 PMF 尚未成立，我们在制作过程中也会不断怀疑：这个定位发布出去，真的会有人付费吗？第二，在正式 Launch 前，需要获得足够多的种子用户反馈和验证。无论是通过 Outreach、线下活动、朋友介绍还是投资人介绍，都应该先找到一批种子用户，可能是 100 位左右。产品上线初期一定会存在需要调整的功能和 Landing Page 表达，这些都要根据真实用户反馈来修改。线上可以寻找 Reddit 社区；如果创始人擅长 Building in Public，Founder IP 也是很好的获客渠道。第三，在正式 Launch 前，要打磨好后续转化路径，包括 Landing Page、Sign-up、Aha Moment、付费和留存机制。最好通过前面这批种子用户进行访谈和不同版本的 A/B Test，达到团队内部认可的转化水平。不同品类的指标并不相同，但只有把前面这些环节打磨到一定程度，再进行 Launch，业务结果才更有保障。Blockit 能够在发布 6 小时后获得 1,214 个注册，并让约六分之一直接付费，就是因为团队此前几个月已经把 PMF、种子用户和转化路径打磨得非常顺畅。

“Launch 不是上线，而是一场集中作战”


姜元晨：前面提到的 PMF、种子用户和付费转化链路，是 Launch 之前产品团队最好先完成的工作。这些准备到位以后，对 Launch 的帮助非常大。下一步才是具体如何执行 Launch。Launch 的确像一场 Campaign，也像一场战役，发布当天尤其重要。比较理想的节奏，是在前面三项工作完成以后，再用一个月左右正式筹划。第一步仍然是定位。Helena 从 AI CMO 调整到 The First Autonomous AI Marketer，这个定位变化可能决定了整场 Launch 40% 到 50% 的结果，影响用户能否记住它、能否被它击中。接下来是渠道选择和达人打法。不同类型的产品适合不同渠道。AI 生产力工具，尤其是 To B 产品，通常以 X 和 LinkedIn 为主要阵地；To C 的生活类 Agent，主阵地可能是 Instagram 和 TikTok；面向独立开发者、技术属性更强的产品，GitHub 也可能是很好的 PR 和增长渠道。Product Hunt 目前对直接转化的推动可能不如过去，但在曝光和 PR 方面仍然很有价值，打的好的话可以带来数百万级曝光，一些媒体也会在那里寻找素材。没有绝对最好的渠道，只有最适合产品的渠道。达人推广通常采用“1—3—6”的结构，发布日尽可能把主要流量推出来；如果产品方后续还需要持续运营，再把一部分达人分配到接下来一周持续加热。

“别让一次发布变成一场烟花”


Monica：在你们过去的案例中，Product Hunt 和 X 通常只选其中一个，还是会同时使用？

姜元晨：要根据客户预算和产品适配度判断。有些产品技术属性较强，投入 Product Hunt 可能还不如认真经营 GitHub，转化率反而更高。核心还是与客户团队对齐商业目标。最后一部分，是发布以后如何继续运营这波流量。Launch 会通过达人、视频和 PR 渠道带来一次很高的流量脉冲，但发布后不能任由它自然消失。通常还有大约两周的红利窗口，可以继续强化产品定位和市场心智，也可以寻找新的增长点。常规做法包括素材二次分发：如果主片表现很好，就继续拆成短内容，围绕不同场景制作小型 Post，把创始人原本希望表达、但没有放进主片的内容分开发布。也可以保留一部分达人，在第一周后面的几天继续推广，同时做好评论回复和社区运营。再往后，是更长期的社媒运营：借助这一波声量继续经营创始人 IP；To C 产品可以配合投放测试；Reddit 可以作为长期渠道；品牌官号和社媒内容也要持续运营。我们更倾向于把 Launch 视为一次集中推动的增长飞轮，让它真正服务于产品的长期发展，而不是把所有希望都压在发布当天。如果 Launch 结束后完全没有后续动作，就像放了一场烟花，流量消失以后便不会再有新的用户进入。完整链路应该从 Launch 延伸到转化与留存，再沉淀内容和口碑，最终带动后续增长。

第一次没打响，换个名字再发一次有用吗？


Monica：有些产品第一次发布以后，效果没有达到预期，过一段时间又重新发布，或者改一个名字再发布。你认为这种方式有效吗？

姜元晨：如果几次发布的间隔不太长，连续制造几次脉冲，仍然会有一定效果。至少从 PR 声量看，它能够让市场反复知道这个产品的存在。但如果目标是长期获取用户、形成持续增长，仍然要配合刚才提到的长线运营。我记得 Aha Creator 的 Launch 做得比较好。大约是去年 7 月，当时产品好像叫 HeadAI，那一轮定位非常准确，主打“你的 AI Marketing Manager”。当时使用这个定位的产品还不多，它直接把这一概念植入市场心智，再配合一系列宣发动作，我认为那次 Launch 很成功。

“替代一个岗位”，为什么仍是最容易转化的叙事？


Monica： 当前AI 产品的定位，我们观察下来大致有三类：第一类是替代岗位，例如 AI HR；第二类是强调效率，比原来的方式快多少倍、提升多少效率；第三类是全新的物种，例如“全球首个某某产品”。从曝光、转化和用户接受度来看，这三类定位分别有什么特点？

姜元晨：根据目前的经验，“替代岗位”这一类定位通常能够带来比较稳定的业务量，因为它直接切中实际需求。尤其是面向开发者或技术背景团队的 AI CMO，它回应了一个很现实的痛点：团队不希望花很多钱招聘一个成本高、还需要长时间磨合的运营团队，如果有一个能力很强的 SaaS 工具可以辅助完成这些工作，节省成本的价值非常明确，因此转化率通常也比较高。至于创造一个新类目，例如 The World’s First，PR 效果往往更强，也更容易 Go Viral。用户看到一个全新的物种，传播数据可能很好看。但在具体转化上，它未必像第一类定位那么直接、那么可感。换到用户视角，他在 X 上刷到这条内容，首先需要理解你说的到底是什么。如果 The World’s First 后面跟着一串让人难以理解的概念，他可能只会觉得产品很有意思，却不知道与自己有什么关系，很快就会划走。

产品和竞品几乎一样，还能讲出什么新故事？


Monica：如果你接到一款产品，它和竞品看起来几乎一模一样，甚至连 UI 都很接近。在这种情况下，你们如何寻找卖点和叙事？

姜元晨：这种情况现在很常见，尤其是在一些非常拥挤的赛道里，产品之间可能真的只剩下 UI 颜色和品牌形象上的区别。这个时候，定位就需要更加谨慎。前期可能要投入两到三周，甚至更长时间。我们的创意总监会提供创意定位咨询，像传统咨询公司一样深度介入。我们有一套 Package，会围绕多个模块提问：品牌形象、网站 User Journey、希望向市场呈现怎样的感受、Q3 和 Q4 的商业目标、竞争对手，以及希望建立哪些差异化。像 Helena 当时已经面临 Okara 和其他 AI Go-to-Market 工具的竞争，如何重新切入市场，就要求我们深入产品本身，甚至一周与创始人开多次会议，逐步找到突破口。有时还需要进行小规模测试。正式 Launch 前，可以先由团队发布一些审美和定位方向的内容，通过 UGC 或小型 KOC，做简单测试，观察市场对哪一种趋势反应更好，用小测试尽量避免大错误。

软件越来越拥挤，下一波机会会在硬件吗？


姜元晨：最近我接触了一些硬件产品。如果产品与市场需求高度匹配，后端供应链能力强，而且品类本身足够新颖，我认为硬件可能更容易打出效果。软件市场现在确实比较拥挤。有客户在回访时提到，自己的产品刚刚 Launch，市场上立刻出现了大量类似产品，因为软件的进入门槛通常没有硬件那么高。硬件可能是一个比较好的方向，但前提是市场需求必须足够 Solid，不能投入大量资金完成产品以后，才发现市场验证非常消极，那样同样会造成很大损失。我的工作更多集中在 Marketing 领域，因此也比较关注能够帮助用户做 SEO、投流和营销的 AI 工具。尤其是 DTC 和国内跨境卖家，会主动尝试这类工具，付费意愿也比较高。随着 AI SaaS 的产品构建门槛下降，大家竞争的重点会越来越转向 Distribution、定位和营销，有点像进入国内电商竞争阶段：关键是如何真正把产品卖出去，如何讲清楚卖点，并触达真实用户。因此我个人更看好能够帮助用户完成营销的 AI 工具。像 Helena 这类产品，本身就属于“帮助用户赚钱”的工具，这个概念通常很容易被市场接受。

一家 Launch Studio 的 23 人团队如何运转？


姜元晨：有人问团队构成和分工。如果把 Contractor 计算在内，我们现在大约有 23 个人。创意总监是核心，他下面有两位负责辅助创意的伙伴，三个人主要负责所有视频的脚本和创意。前期定位咨询也由创意总监负责。他曾经在美国广告公司工作五六年，最近两三年主要从事 AI 产品定位咨询和 Launch 操盘，因此是前期工作的主力。拍摄和动效则由专业团队完成。摄影方面，我们在上海、深圳和湾区都有长期合作的 Production Team，对接效率比较高，交付效果也更有保障。我们还与一些专门负责后续增长运营的 Partner 合作。有人问我是否看好 Agent，我认为自己还没有足够资历像投资人一样判断，也不是朱啸虎，不能轻易说自己看好什么方向。至于 Reddit，它已经是一项专业性很强的工作。无论国内的知乎还是海外的 Reddit，都有非常专业的团队，掌握成熟的运营方法，能够把营销效果和整套 Campaign 的 ROI 控制得更好。随着平台管理越来越严格，这类工作会更加依赖专业团队。营销本身就是一个高度专业化、渠道分工越来越细的领域。

Launch Video 真的有固定公式吗？


Monica：Launch Video 是否存在相对固定的创意框架？例如前三秒、五秒、十秒分别讲什么，最后五秒、十秒又讲什么？

姜元晨：我们内部有五种经常使用的内容形式，具体方法可能比较内部化。总体上还是根据产品方的商业目标来决定。如果这一波不追求特别大的爆发，只希望切实获得第一批用户，同时建立一定市场声量，比较常见的是 Problem-Solving 结构：从痛点切入，接着介绍产品 Feature，最后落到 CTA，这是大家比较熟悉的结构。如果目标是纯粹 Go Viral，创意就会更天马行空，也很难形成固定模板。我最近也一直思考，如何让 Agency 的工作流更加自动化、更容易扩张，但在前期对齐客户需求时，每家公司的问题都不同：有的希望这一波尽可能打爆；有的更看重用户获取；有的不需要很大的传播，只希望把产品完整、清楚地介绍出来。不同目标对应完全不同的打法和内容结构，因此仍然很依赖创意总监的判断、审美和经验。

中国团队和海外 Studio，差别究竟在哪里？


Monica：美国团队做 Launch 和中国团队做 Launch，在思维方式和叙事手法上有哪些差异？还是说现在已经越来越相似？

姜元晨：我对中国团队的 Launch 了解还不算多。平时经常联系和研究的同行基本都是海外团队，国内专门做这件事的团队似乎还不算很多。我的团队大部分成员也是海外背景，因此我们主要处在海外的行业圈层里。不同公司的风格差异很大。虽然大家会互相分享方法论、共同探讨，但我们本质上都像 Studio 或广告创意公司，每家都有自己的风格。有的非常天马行空，像 Kimi K3 那类视觉表现很强的片子；有的则更偏务实，完整介绍产品，更强调增长效果。我目前接触到的国内团队，很多原来服务时尚、电商或其他行业，后来开始承接 AI 客户，整体打法可能更务实。但在审美、艺术性和品牌感方面，海外偏艺术表达的 Studio 往往更有优势。

Monica：你的创意总监是中国人吗？

姜元晨：不是。他的背景比较特别，会讲五种语言，祖籍巴西，在瑞士长大，后来又到美国工作。

从欧美到日本，小语种市场值得做吗？


Monica：你做过小语种市场的 Launch 吗？

姜元晨：目前没有，基本还是以欧美市场为主。我们接触的 AI 产品，大部分也优先进入欧美市场，因此迪拜、中东、中亚等地区暂时还没有做过。日本市场会涉及一些，因为 AI SaaS 出海除了欧美，日本也是一个比较理想的市场。我此前看到过一组数据，日本用户一旦认可产品，付费留存率往往很高，尤其是与 To B 工作流深度契合的产品，留存率甚至可能达到 90%。因此除了欧美市场，我们也会做一些日本渠道推广。其他语言区目前涉及不多。

Monica：你和创意总监是怎么认识的？

姜元晨：是我主动 Outreach 找到他的。我长期关注他的社媒 Profile，看他分享的内容，觉得这个人很有能力，于是主动联系。最开始我们只通过一个项目进行简单合作，后来逐步深入，最终成为团队成员。

Monica：这个经历很值得大家借鉴。

姜元晨：社媒确实是一个非常 Solid 的渠道，无论是招聘、寻找客户，还是建立其他合作关系。国内很多工厂做外贸，现在也会通过 Facebook、Instagram 获取客户。社媒的价值很大，当然我自己也还在持续学习。

什么样的产品，Launch 之后更容易接住增长？


Monica：在你过去做过的产品里，有没有一开始你并不看好，结果发布效果反而很不错的案例？

姜元晨：这个问题有些尴尬。如果我们判断产品暂时不适合做 Launch，一般会比较委婉地表示需要再考虑，或者暂时不接这个项目。因为我们对每个项目的投入都很重，双方如果不匹配，勉强合作的效果也不会好。我们可能会陪伴一段时间，给出一些力所能及的建议，长期跟进，等产品和时机更成熟以后再开始合作。至于哪一类产品更容易成功，我也不敢给出绝对结论。根据目前的经验，一些 To B 产品的表现比较 Solid，主要集中在生产力场景：要么帮助客户实现增长，要么帮助客户降低成本。这类产品通常更容易销售，业务表现也更稳定。生活类产品则更依赖目标人群和市场打法。我们目前服务的客户，通常是 8 到 30 人的团队，处于 Pre-A 到 A 轮，产品本身已经经过一定验证。

“只有一张产品图，也要开始拍 Kickstarter 视频”


Monica：你接过准备上线 Kickstarter、但 Demo 还没有完成，只提供一张产品照片或效果图的项目吗？

姜元晨：确实接过。这类项目投入会非常大。最痛苦的情况是，片子已经制作到一半，产品突然发生重大修改，所有内容都需要重新制作。动效师可能连续熬夜到凌晨四五点，再完成新一轮调整。成熟产品有成熟产品的优势，特别早期的项目在制作上确实更辛苦。不过能够陪伴一款产品从最初概念走到落地，再正式推向市场，带来的成就感也很强。

Launch 阶段，钱该花在 KOL 还是 Google Ads？


Monica：有同学问，Google 投流和社媒 KOL 推广，哪一部分的支出更高？

姜元晨：在我们目前的项目中，社媒 KOL 推广的支出通常更高。我们的核心业务是广告制作和 Launch Video，本身就处在社媒传播生态里。视频完成以后，最自然的延伸场景就是达人推广，再将内容放到官网和 YouTube 等渠道。因此我们现在使用 KOL 的比例更高，而且行业打法也已经比较成熟，各家 Agency 在这一领域都有相对稳定的方法。一般做 Launch 时，都会安排 KOL 推广。Google Ads 更多用于后续长期运营，客户需要持续监测 CPM、CPA，并按照完整的广告投放方式逐步优化。仅就 Launch 这一阶段而言，我们通常不会负责 Google 投流，除非客户需要一套完整的全案服务。

“我不想什么都自己做，更愿意和伙伴一起做”


姜元晨：扩展业务范围这个问题比较敏感。我现阶段还是希望先把 Launch 做好。当然，我们也会从客户场景出发，提供一些力所能及、真正能够帮助到客户的服务。目前通常是与不同 Partner 联合完成，因为每家公司都有自己的专长。如果一家 Agency 同时承接太多工作，效果未必最好。如果扩展业务能够满足客户需求、真正为客户创造价值，我们会认真考虑，但不希望通过正面竞争去争夺别人的业务，更愿意通过合作共同创造价值。这个话题之所以敏感，是因为有时一旦公开表示准备进入某个领域，同行可能就会产生防备。其实我希望未来做得很大，尽可能把营销变成 Fully Autonomous 的系统。目前仍在探索最合适的切入渠道。投流本身的自动化程度可以比较高，但我们最早起家的创意业务，涉及创意判断和客户沟通，能够自动化的部分确实很有限。

Monica：所以未来有可能做成一个“Launch 版奈飞”吗？

姜元晨：我考虑过类似方向。不过长期来看，我可能更希望回归实业，例如帮助国内商家实现海外自动化增长。我有一个更长远的理想，是推动整条产业链实现自动化，当然这可能需要二三十年。我希望从营销切入，因为整条产业链最末端的订单来源，基本掌握在 DTC 品牌和大型卖家手中。无论从代运营还是 AI 自动化代运营切入，只要进入他们的营销和运营环节，就能够了解整个产业中的订单如何产生，再逐步向上游延伸。例如连接供应商和工厂，把它们纳入同一套自动化系统，类似把商家的 ERP、工厂的 MES 和供应链的 WMS 全部打通。长远目标是这样，但现阶段我们仍然处在最末端的一个小环节，先把 Creative Agency 做好。

从营销自动化到“大同社会”，创业者的终极理想


Monica：如果这一整套都实现了，人类可能只需要等待产品被生产出来。

姜元晨：我的理想，是实现一种可以称为“大同社会”的状态。去年我经常思考，自己的人生究竟希望完成什么。最后我想到，最希望实现的目标，是让地球上的人都不再为基本的吃穿发愁，尽可能缓解生存压力。结合当前科技，我认为可以通过经济自动化逐步实现：把整条产业链从原料、加工、生产、运输到市场销售，其中的人力成本尽可能减少或优化。比如一个鼠标售价 55 元，背后整条产业链的人工成本可能占 30 到 40 元，包括商家运营、广告投手、运输快递、工厂生产、财务法务和原料开采。如果整条产业链中的人工环节都能通过 AI 和机器人替代，最终产品的成本可能只需要 5 元，剩余部分主要是能源和各环节利润。可以先从基础物资开始，完成一条产业链以后，再推进下一条。当然，这已经超出了今天的话题。

Monica：你想打造的可能是一个硅基世界。

姜元晨：大家也经常讨论：如果 AI 和机器人完成了整条产业链中的所有工作，人类以后做什么？假如真的能够实现一个美好的社会，所有生产活动都由机器人和 AI 完成，人类不再为基本生存发愁，就可以更自由地探索自己的兴趣。喜欢弹钢琴的人可以弹钢琴，喜欢画画的人可以画画，喜欢科学研究的人可以长期研究，喜欢艺术的人可以持续探索艺术，回到人的精神与创造本身。我是这样设想的。这个方向确实和黑湖有一些相似，黑湖主要做工厂 MES 系统。我创业时也考虑过是否进入这个方向，但投入太大，以我当时的资源很难承担，可能需要大规模融资。

Monica：这个设想很有可能会实现，而且时间可能比你想象得更短，那时候也许会进入 UBI 阶段。

姜元晨：对，这确实是我认真思考过的方向。如果一生能够推动这件事实现，会让我觉得这一趟很有意义。也不知道为什么，我会给自己设定这样一个 Mission。现在能做的，就是一步步向前推进。有时我也会怀疑，现在所做的广告创意业务，与这个目标是否距离太远。但我还是希望先把一个真实业务跑起来，建立商业 Sense，再考虑下一阶段。

“见客户前故意熬夜，只为了让自己看起来成熟一点”


Monica：你是哪一年出生的？

从 T-30 到 T+30，一场标准 Launch 怎么排？


Monica：最后请你快速复盘一下，从 T-30 到 T+30，一场标准 Launch 的完整时间表应该怎样安排？

姜元晨：在正式进入 T-30 之前，团队应尽可能先把 PMF 跑通。最好已经服务过 100 位种子用户；如果时间和精力有限，至少也应该有 50 位，完成一轮反馈和访谈，找出最佳场景和 ICP。与此同时，还要把后续付费机制设计并验证好，达到比较理想的指标。进入 T-30 的第一天，开始确定产品定位。前期场景和 ICP 已经明确以后，定位也会更容易。定位确定后，再根据定位、场景、ICP 和商业目标，延伸出视频创意策略和脚本，最好在 7 到 10 天内完成。如果需要拍摄，前期准备完成后，可以用一到两天完成拍摄；如果不需要实拍，就直接进入 Motion Design 后期，通常需要大约 20 天。开始制作视频的同时，可以同步筹备 Product Hunt、PR 媒体、达人推广和其他渠道。如果时间充裕，有一个多月甚至两个月，可以先把视频准备好，再推进 PR 和 Product Hunt，因为 Product Hunt 最好在拿到视频以后再联系 Hunter。如果时间非常紧，就采用并行方式推进。视频准备完成后，配合其他渠道正式 Launch。发布当天，第一个小时是黄金窗口，执行“1—3—6”。如果没有 Founder 自己的 Network，就直接从“3”和“6”开始。根据产品后续增长需求，保留一部分达人，在后续持续加热。发布日最重要。如果产品团队前期已经把 PMF、种子用户和转化路径做好，我们又能够准确定位，视频创意和制作流程也已经比较工程化，那么这一波发布通常不会出现太大偏差，也更有机会获得理想的业务数据。我们的标准服务到这里基本结束。如果客户还需要后续增长运营，我们会继续介入，分析这一轮带来了多少注册用户，并对各环节进行埋点：从社媒帖子、达人帖子，到官网、Landing Page、Sign-up，再到付费，监测每个节点的转化率，持续优化。同时配合 Reddit、Founder IP、品牌官号、社媒内容、持续投流和达人推广等后续动作。这样从产品验证、前期准备，到 Launch 当天集中爆发，再到发布后的持续运营，才能形成一条比较完整、健康的增长曲线。

“Launch Video 是我的海博翻译社”


姜元晨：还有一位老师问，为什么我最开始选择做 Launch Video，这与后面提到的理想似乎反差很大。我自己也会思考这个问题。如果做一个类比，可能有点像马云当年的海博翻译社。同时，Launch Video 与我最初想切入的环节也有一定关联，因为它本身就是从营销进入。围绕 Video，我们现在已经与 Partner 一起延伸出很多配套服务，例如达人推广、SEO、Reddit 和投流。这些业务已经触及刚才所说 Mission 的第一个环节。它也算是一份立身之业，让我积累商业 Sense。未来希望实现的 Mission 也主要面向 To B，因此现阶段可以先积累 To B 经验和团队运营能力。创业确实不容易，需要考虑账期、财务、团队管理、客户预期、下一步发展、市场运营、获客、转化和交付，整个过程像一场商业实战训练。当然，我自己也很热爱 Launch Video。希望把LucidEdge做成这个行业里具有代表性的 Agency，至少像“海博翻译社”一样成为一个标杆。它本身也是一项艺术性很强的工作。真正投入以后，我发现自己确实喜欢这一行。尤其在线下喊出“Action”、正式开机的那一刻，我会非常兴奋；或者内部团队完成一支片子，最终看到它的视觉效果非常好，也会产生很强的成就感和喜悦，觉得这份工作本身很快乐。未来再一步一步向那个 Mission 靠近。
