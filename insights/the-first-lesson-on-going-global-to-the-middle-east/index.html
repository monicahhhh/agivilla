A Structured Understanding of the Real Middle East Market

Monica

Gao Song has been in the Middle East for ten years. As someone deeply integrated into the local culture, including its humanities, politics, and economy, what kind of place and market is the Middle East in your eyes?

Gao Song

The Middle East market has only recently come into focus. In the past, it was mainly associated with two things: oil and war. Oil, primarily in the Gulf countries, led to their heavy reliance on it and created many wealthy individuals. Since 2022, we've seen more and more Chinese entrepreneurs, as well as companies from other parts of the world, and high-net-worth individuals, choosing the Middle East as a new destination for global expansion. There are both external and internal reasons for this, but overall, it's a huge market.

When we talk about the Middle East, we often use the MENA concept, which stands for Middle East and North Africa. The Middle East sometimes even includes Morocco, Tunisia, Algeria, Somalia, and Djibouti. So, when defining the Middle East, whether culturally, linguistically (Arabic), or geographically, I believe there isn't a single, unified concept. If we say we're going to the Middle East, which Middle East exactly are we referring to? This concept is broad and complex, and we need to understand which specific sub-market we intend to enter.

Monica

Over the past decade, including your time at Tencent, you've encountered many Chinese companies expanding overseas. What changes have you observed in the trend and enthusiasm of Chinese companies going to the Middle East over the last ten years or recent years?

Gao Song

Before 2020, I believe there was very little participation from private enterprises in going global to the Middle East. If there was private enterprise involvement, it was mainly traditional foreign trade business owners, perhaps engaging in trade or providing local services like opening restaurants or supermarkets. Another group was large state-owned enterprises going to the Middle East. When discussing domestic companies now seeking new business growth opportunities in the Middle East, the market can be roughly divided into several regions.

First, there are the GCC countries (Gulf Cooperation Council). Then there are large population markets like Egypt and Iraq. There's also the Levant region (Syria, Lebanon), and some relatively closed regions like Algeria.

Regionally, the biggest hotspot currently is still the GCC, the Gulf countries. The Gulf countries are also the primary focus for most tech companies expanding overseas. First, their total GDP is high, per capita ARPU is very high, and willingness to pay is strong. In the Islamic world, people's desires are often suppressed due to religious doctrines and the historical lack of modernization in their countries, leading many local customers or users to be highly receptive to new things, and their willingness to pay is also quite strong.

As you just mentioned, within the GCC, there are a few key markets to focus on. The first is the UAE (United Arab Emirates), which can be considered the first stop for almost all Chinese enterprises expanding into the Middle East and even Africa. The UAE, especially Dubai and Abu Dhabi, positions itself as a free port. This place is highly urbanized and globalized, and living there feels pretty much the same as living in Shanghai or Shenzhen in China.

In Dubai, 90% of the population are foreigners, with only 10% being true locals. These foreigners come from over 200 nationalities, from Asia to Africa, America, and Oceania, and they coexist harmoniously. The UAE has a famous landmark called the Abrahamic Family House, where a church, a synagogue, and a mosque are built side-by-side in the same complex. This country has always positioned itself as inclusive, open, and egalitarian.

Naturally, all applications, including payment capabilities and population, are at a global level, akin to a global village. When it comes to innovation, 5G, digital government services, AI cloud services, cybersecurity, smart cities, SaaS, Fintech, and Web3 are all very mature markets in the UAE.

Many years ago, there was Souq.com, a relatively large local e-commerce website in the UAE, which was later acquired by Amazon. There's also Noon, which all e-commerce enthusiasts know, and it still ranks among the top one or two in the Middle East. Additionally, the UAE has many vertical tech companies, such as Tabby, a payment company offering online and offline installment payments and buy-now-pay-later services. Another is Qlub, which offers software for skipping queues and paying at offline restaurants, incorporating local payment habits like tipping or splitting bills into the payment page, making it highly localized.

On a larger scale, there's the G42 Group, which is currently the leading AI and technology enterprise in the UAE. This is the overall concept of the UAE.

Another key GCC market to focus on is Saudi Arabia. With the Vision 2030 launched by Mohammed bin Salman Al Saud after he came to power, regardless of its outcomes, it has certainly attracted significant global attention. Therefore, many who talk about expanding into the Middle East now believe that Saudi Arabia's 36 million people represent a potential goldmine. The society possesses vast wealth, but at the lifestyle, application, and B2B enterprise levels, it has not been fully modernized. This is currently the biggest prize everyone wants to seize when expanding overseas.

However, the costs and barriers are also higher than many imagine. Saudi Arabia's overall modernization heavily relies on the Saudi government, and the government and state-owned enterprises exert very strong control over the entire industry chain. When doing business in Saudi Arabia, there's a distinct feeling: expanding in the UAE involves normal market competition, normal global competition, relying on product strength. But in Saudi Arabia, you need many prerequisites to enter certain projects, requiring introductions or partnerships with reputable state-owned or local enterprises to gain access to the entire industry chain and immense opportunities.

Thus, many successful bidders for good projects in Saudi Arabia are large state-owned enterprises or central enterprises, operating top-down. Nevertheless, Saudi Arabia's sheer size makes it a market worth paying close attention to. There's a saying, though I'm not sure how accurate it is, that many people say: Saudi Arabia is essentially the UAE of ten, fifteen, or twenty years ago. If it maintains its current open policies, given its size, in ten or twenty years, it will be many times the size of the UAE.

Currently, in Saudi Arabia, we see strong promotion of industries such as AI digitalization, gaming cloud services, data industry, data centers, and Fintech. These two markets are the main and most noteworthy areas within the Gulf countries.

Besides the large region of the Gulf countries, two other markets are worth noting for Chinese enterprises: Egypt and Iraq.

The Egyptian market has existed for many years and is a common topic in Middle East expansion. Egypt's characteristics include a very large population of 120 million, and the overall population is very young, with a median age definitely below 35. However, there's a significant problem: due to the overall state of governance in Egypt, the per capita GDP and overall economy have been unstable for many years, and the country's infrastructure development is also poor.

Flying from Dubai to Egypt feels like going from a tier-one city to an eighteen-tier city, especially Cairo. I lived in Cairo for over a year, in the affluent Maadi district, which was basically where all Chinese companies gathered when they came to Cairo. But even in this so-called affluent area, there were many landfills nearby, roads were poorly maintained, there were no lane markings, cars were battered, driving was very undisciplined, and traffic lights were rarely seen. The entire city gave a very chaotic and complex impression.

However, despite all this, internet penetration is still very high. With its massive population base, e-commerce, mobile payments, and the gaming industry are very popular in a giant population market like Egypt. When developing software to boost DAU, the first stop for cold starts was usually Egypt, because the population is quick to activate, costs are low, and labor costs in Egypt are also very low. These are Egypt's advantages. But the problem is that ARPU is very low, meaning people's ability to pay is low.

Furthermore, the network infrastructure is not highly developed; to this day, many Egyptians cannot constantly connect to the internet using data. They might listen to music in the car by downloading it at home via Wi-Fi beforehand. This seems incredible in China but is indeed a reality there, due to their low income and relatively high infrastructure costs. Internet usage habits are strong, but it's not always possible to use or access it.

Another market is Iraq. Iraq is slowly emerging from the shadow of war. I revisited Iraq in September 2024, specifically Baghdad and Erbil, which are currently relatively stable. Iraq has over 45 million people and abundant oil. Similar to Egypt, it's a large population market, but Iraq's purchasing power is particularly strong.

Currently, Iraq has a critical issue with payments, due to problems with dollar settlements, which makes it difficult for many goods to enter and for people to purchase external items. Political infrastructure is relatively weak, and there are still political and security risks in the northern Kurdish region.

These are generally the cities and countries currently most focused on for Middle East expansion. Other places, such as the Levant region—Syria, Jordan, Palestine—are still frequently affected by political and security issues, making business operations difficult. Moreover, the government's transparency and friendliness to foreign businesses are not as stable as in the Gulf region or other areas.

Then there are markets like Tunisia and Morocco, which lean more towards requiring a European mindset for business. Tunisia and Morocco are both gateways from Europe into Africa; Morocco is sometimes called Europe's backyard, and both were once French colonies. In these countries, the common languages are French and Arabic, along with local Berber. Doing business in these markets requires an entirely different approach. While the Gulf market might focus more on modernization and accommodating different nationalities and users, if you go to Morocco or Tunisia, the market and users are heavily skewed towards European styles, especially French user habits. They are more familiar with Europe, more familiar with French, and more familiar with European ways of doing things, only leaning towards Arabs or Islam on some religious or traditional levels. Many distinctions need to be made here; it's not a monolithic situation at all.

So, I think we can broadly divide this region into these categories.

The Global View of the Middle East: Why the UAE is the First Choice

Monica

What position do you think the UAE holds in the entire Middle East? And from the perspective of the American tech scene and some dollar funds, where do they place the UAE now?

Gao Song

Why we recommend the UAE might be counter-intuitive for many. Dubai has almost no oil. When its relatives in neighboring areas discovered oil early on, Dubai had none, forcing it to find another path to survival. The then-ruler, Sheikh Rashid bin Saeed Al Maktoum, was very farsighted, directly defining Dubai as a regional hub or transit point. From the 1960s and 1970s, it began to position itself as a springboard to radiate to surrounding countries. It started early with port construction, free zone development, tax reforms, and a friendly attitude towards foreign businesses. Initially, it attracted many Iranian merchants because Iran is across the sea. Later, as the world gradually focused on the Middle East, Dubai's status became prominent, and even the entire UAE became the gateway to the Middle East.

Now, why is the world looking at the UAE? First, compared to all other surrounding Middle Eastern countries, the UAE's government infrastructure is top-notch, comparable to Shenzhen's government in many aspects. Just as Shenzhen handles many government services through mini-programs or WeChat official accounts, in Dubai and the UAE, the government has its own dedicated apps. The Dubai Police have their app, and the electricity and water authority also has its app, all allowing online operations. Many things, including property transactions, have been fully digitized.

In terms of ease of use, it's more user-friendly. Let me tell you a fun fact: in 2019, in Egypt, I went to the immigration office to apply for a work residency visa. Anyone who has worked in Egypt knows that obtaining a residency visa there used to be very difficult. Basically, every Tuesday morning at 9 AM, you had to go to the immigration office, where there was no queue; everyone would swarm into a small door, and whoever pushed to the front got in first. Once inside, you'd see the government service hall, where all personal archives were still stored in paper files. There were many people, and efficiency might have been an issue; you'd often see files flying on the ground or in the air. This reflects a common situation in the Middle East: achieving digitalization is a rare feat, unlike the excellent conditions we have in China. So, many countries have deterred foreign enterprises due to insufficient transparency and digitalization, leading to inefficiency. For businesses, efficiency is life; every week of delay means burning a lot of money.

In this regard, the UAE is ahead of the entire Middle East.

Furthermore, the UAE implements a so-called zero-tax policy. Qualified companies in free zones are not subject to corporate income tax. Foreign capital can hold 100% ownership of all companies in Dubai's free zones and UAE free zones. Profits and capital can flow freely internationally without any restrictions or foreign exchange controls. This is very friendly to Europeans, Southeast Asians, and Chinese entrepreneurs.

Moreover, the UAE integrated into the Western system very early on. In the UAE, especially in Dubai, two types of laws are applied. Dubai has a specialized free zone called DIFC, the Dubai International Financial Centre, which operates under complete common law, Anglo-American law, seamlessly aligning with Western enterprises. Therefore, Western banks, brokerages, and other large multinational groups choose DIFC as their first stop because, legally, it fully integrates with their legal ecosystem. Other areas of Dubai follow its own civil law.

From a legal and business environment perspective, only 10% of the UAE's population are locals; the rest are all foreigners. For businesses, talent acquisition is a huge advantage. It's easy to recruit talent from various countries worldwide, and the recruitment difficulty is relatively low, with people moving between companies. The professional community is essentially concentrated in Abu Dhabi and Dubai.

Many people, when they think of Dubai or the Middle East, wonder about war or scams. In fact, I've experienced it firsthand: Dubai and the UAE are extremely safe, almost identical to China. A woman can walk alone in downtown Dubai at night without any problems; it's very safe. Many residents in Dubai don't even lock their doors; their homes don't need to be locked, and no one will break in to steal. If you leave your phone, laptop, or bag in a mall, it will likely still be there three hours later. This is because the UAE has strict visa controls; people who come to the UAE are mostly expatriate workers or investors. Before 2022, it was rare to see elderly people or children; the UAE was a city for workers and investors.

From a safety perspective, it's very suitable for people from different countries to relocate. Even for Europeans, they are willing to go to the UAE because it's safer than Europe, with no theft, robbery, or vandalism, and there's no personal income tax – it's zero. In the first half of 2025, a large number of British, French, and Italian people moved from Europe to the UAE. There were even articles in British newspapers urging people not to go to the UAE and to stay in the UK. This is because taxes can be saved by 30-40%, which is equivalent to a 50% salary increase.

Furthermore, there's plenty of sunshine here, and the cost of living is slightly lower than in Europe, and it's safer than Europe. So, from all angles, people are more willing to choose this place. From income to safety and all other aspects, there's a huge wave of interest here.

The US is also making many bets on the Middle East now. The US might be our biggest competitor in the future AI or tech industry, but currently, at least at the government level, the US has a significant presence throughout the Middle East. For example, Microsoft has invested approximately $15 billion in Saudi Arabia and the UAE for data centers and cloud service infrastructure. They even made an equity investment in the UAE state-owned tech group G42.

Last year, when Trump visited the Middle East with an AMD delegation (including Elon Musk, NVIDIA, Qualcomm, Oracle, etc.), they also engaged in extensive AI cooperation, including the export of high-end GPUs to Saudi Arabia and the UAE. At the same time, Trump's StarGate program includes a StarGate UAE plan, aiming to build one of the largest overseas computing centers for the US, located in the UAE, which is an AI data center. We can see many entrepreneurs from Silicon Valley moving to Abu Dhabi and Dubai to secure investments and start businesses; these are behaviors we can observe, and they have been active for a long time.

Monica

I'd like to echo what Gao Song just said. Before going to the Middle East, many friends would worry about safety. But honestly, my biggest feeling in the Middle East was that it's truly, incredibly safe. As Gao Song said, you don't see any homeless people on the streets. In contrast, in all the cities I've visited, Los Angeles had the most homeless people; when I went for a night run there, many homeless people were on the streets. But I didn't see a single homeless person in the Middle East. At night, it's basically brightly lit everywhere, especially in Dubai.

Dubai feels very much like Shenzhen; late at night, there are still many young people active in various malls, bars, and shopping centers, and the overall pace is very fast. You can also see people trying out many new apps, and there are many small, active startup ecosystems.

Another very significant feeling is its internationalization. Before going, I knew it was an immigrant city, but the degree of internationalization far exceeded my imagination. The actual expatriate population, as far as the eye could see, was probably over 90%. The cost of hiring international employees was not as high as imagined either. Some local blue-collar workers, such as those from India and Pakistan, earn around 3,000 to 4,000 RMB per month. These individuals find it relatively easy to reside and obtain relevant work visas locally.

Another point that struck me deeply was the penetration of English in these Middle Eastern countries. Although Arabic is the native language, English is spoken very authentically. So, the degree of internationalization and smoothness is excellent, which is very conducive to establishing businesses and recruiting international employees locally. Abu Dhabi has established an artificial intelligence university, which also offers an advantage in recruiting students with AI backgrounds.

When visiting the Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence (MBZUAI), I was also deeply impressed by one thing: many newly admitted undergraduates are descendants of the royal family, which shows the core class's strong determination for young people to develop in the direction of artificial intelligence.

Where are the Opportunities in the Middle East?

Monica

Gao Song, could you elaborate on where the local opportunities lie? Whether it's artificial intelligence or SaaS, there are different ecosystems for ToB and ToC, and the scenarios and supply chains they rely on will also differ. What are your thoughts on business opportunities and space?

Gao Song

Here, I'd like to first summarize why it's now essential to embark on Middle East expansion—it's reached a "land grab" moment. The closest free ports to China are Hong Kong or Singapore. Hong Kong goes without saying. Singapore is very elite and mature; due to its limited land area, it's a very complete or already saturated market, welcoming only the elite of the elite.

However, in the UAE, you'll find a strong pioneering spirit, making it particularly suitable for entrepreneurs or startups to venture into. The opportunities and costs here are very favorable.

Another point is why Chinese companies should go global. Firstly, it's certainly due to the pressures in the domestic market, forcing them to look for opportunities abroad. These external opportunities can simultaneously offer capital, labor, and market factors. If you compare Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and Latin America, you'll find that the overall cost of entering the Middle East is very low.

There's also the geopolitical issue that must be considered going forward, especially for the AI industry, which is very sensitive. Traditional large markets like the US and Europe will easily be affected by political factors in the future, thereby impacting the entire operation of businesses.

However, the Middle East's positioning is that of a "weather vane"—it doesn't lean on either side, practicing "equidistance diplomacy," maintaining equal distance from both China and the US. It hopes that its market will have both China and the US, wishing for these two energies to form a balanced state. Therefore, it's a naturally suitable environment for us to try some overseas expansion or to establish entities there for tax planning, etc.

Furthermore, the Middle East has now launched national policies, explicitly stating its demand for AI. As mentioned earlier, MBZUAI, the artificial intelligence university, and the UAE has a national AI strategy for 2031. Saudi Arabia aims to become the third largest AI powerhouse after China and the US. While these might be ambitious claims, the attitude is certainly there.

Having discussed these attitudes, let's look at specific entry points. First, there are government or national project-driven enterprises. Without a doubt, these might have already secured orders from the UAE or Saudi Arabia. Such companies generally rely on major domestic players, implementing top-down, high-level cooperation to localize in Saudi Arabia or the Middle East and secure projects. This is an entry point from China moving upwards.

Another approach is to make bottom-up attempts locally, such as providing high-end services or customized B2B solutions. Especially in the UAE and Saudi Arabia, there are many gaps in consumer upgrades. The entire spectrum of clothing, food, housing, transportation, and entertainment is several generations behind China's current level.

Imagine, for example, clothing: there's no live-streaming e-commerce here. For food, delivery services are just starting to see Meituan enter and compete with local delivery companies using Chinese power. For housing, the real estate market in the UAE and Saudi Arabia is booming, potentially for another decade. All housing-related aspects, including some AI companies in the housing sector, are starting to expand into the Middle East, such as those doing smart property management or AI-driven real estate peripheral services. There are also loan and insurance industries; in every niche, you'll find very few competitors.

For transportation, like ride-hailing, it's currently still Careem and Uber, which are the same company. There are also navigation, vehicle sales, and used cars. Chinese automobiles are just starting to make a name for themselves in the Middle East market. You might not imagine it, but besides BYD, the best-selling Chinese car in the Middle East is Jetour. The Jetour Shanhai and Jetour T2 are selling extremely well in the UAE, and BYD has now entered the entire UAE taxi industry. Electric vehicles have made significant progress and changes in the Middle East over the past three years or so. In recent months, more and more domestic brands like Jishi and possibly Huawei will soon follow.

As for entertainment, there's even more. From May to October, the outdoor temperature in the entire Gulf region is basically around 50 degrees Celsius. 50 degrees means absolutely no outdoor activities, so there are countless opportunities for indoor activities. Even in Dubai, a very mature Middle Eastern city, there are only a few large shopping malls: The Dubai Mall, Mall of the Emirates, Dubai Hills Mall, and Marina Mall—no more than 6 in total.

Dubai's population, and the UAE's population, which was around 3 million in 2024, has now surged to 4.2 million. This rapid population growth will bring a series of demands for peripheral living services. In every vertical segment and field, I believe that no matter what industry you are in, if you visit Dubai, you will find very few competitors. Here, if you're doing AI or software, it's either Indians or Americans.

Everyone knows the style of Indians; in terms of application and practice, they are certainly not as sophisticated or authentic as what Chinese people do. Western services or products are often made according to traditional Western product logic, unable to be as meticulous, close to users' lives, or even as intensely competitive as in China. Moreover, Western pricing is generally higher than domestic pricing. So, there are still many competitive advantages in this area.

Furthermore, besides the UAE's need for consumption upgrades, Arabs have a strong need for identity recognition. Identity recognition is a broad concept. Why do I say they need identity recognition? They have always been wealthy, but they have never been seriously and solemnly treated on the world stage. Since 9/11, the powerful Western propaganda machine has portrayed all Arabs as terrorists. Perhaps even now, if a Muslim woman wearing a black veil appears on a Chinese street, it might still draw strange looks. But if you truly interact with Muslims, you'll find most are generous and kind, especially those in the Gulf who are not short of money.

So they always feel that despite being so rich, living a luxury lifestyle, driving Range Rovers, and living in villas, when they study in the UK or travel in Europe, they feel that Arabs don't receive the respect they deserve, leading to a strong sense of entitlement, a "give me my freaking respect" feeling. Therefore, creating products that foster identity recognition, localization, community building, and suitability for national conditions has consistently shown very good results.

Taking the gaming industry as an example, many years ago, I heard a legend: a dozen Arabic-speaking university students started a company to create a game, an RTS game, and three Arab big brothers supported the entire company. This is very real; the game is called "Sultan's Revenge."

This is a classic RTS (real-time strategy) game, with typical RTS gameplay found on the market. Its biggest innovation is incorporating Arab historical backgrounds, art, history, characters, and the Arabic language, making it feel very familiar to Arabs. What do Arabs usually play? Especially in Saudi Arabia, almost every well-off family has a PS5. On PS5, they play Call of Duty, where Americans kill Arabs. How can that make them feel good?

Suddenly, "Sultan's Revenge" appeared on the market, allowing players to embody great historical figures like Saladin or other Arab heroes, representing Arabs in conquering the world. This is a rarity; it truly makes them feel respected by the company. The characteristic of Arabs is that they care more about whether they are respected. Some domestic entrepreneurs or those doing business might not care so much, as long as everyone gets their profit. But for Arabs, respect is paramount. Even if a deal is excellent, if they feel disrespected or find something displeasing, the matter will be delayed. They need to become friends and feel fully respected before proceeding.

This kind of identity recognition, even during my time at major companies, I found that even industry-leading companies couldn't do localization well. Localization is a broad concept. On one hand, the most basic is Arabic adaptation. Arabic is written from right to left, and letters have ligatures. People not working on the front lines don't know this; if you use Photoshop to create images with Arabic text, you need to specifically re-adjust language options, otherwise, the Arabic text will be garbled.

Often, you see many smaller companies on the market, for the sake of saving money or convenience, directly using Google Translate, and then outputting garbled Arabic text in an image. They proudly release it, thinking they have catered to the Arab audience. In reality, people see the garbled text and the translationese, and clearly realize it's a perfunctory, dismissive effort.

Furthermore, when it comes to cultural adaptation, especially in places like Egypt and Saudi Arabia, there are many strict rules regarding Arab taboos. In games, nudity in skins, certain actions, and even alcohol and pork are not allowed to appear, or even be subtly hinted at. Any suspicious tendency will lead to a ban. We often saw Chinese live-streaming apps being banned in the Middle East because many things considered not suggestive in China were completely inappropriate there. How much skin women show is a very sensitive issue.

Moreover, Arabs are family-oriented and have a strong tribal identity. Even now, Saudi Arabia and the UAE are essentially tribal systems, and emirates are fundamentally tribes. The emir, as the leader of the emirate, must collaborate with several major local families when implementing policies or reforms throughout the emirate.

The entire management style is not democratic, nor is it very modern; it's a very feudal system, but that is the national reality. Therefore, it is essential to consider the need for identity recognition related to family, tribe, and group.

Furthermore, from a perspective of dignity, Arabs very much desire to have a dignified experience. What specific forms can this dignified experience take? One can research this. In terms of monetization, Chinese live-streaming companies have reached an unparalleled level. Various avatar frames can be divided into 10 levels, and spoken language can have colorful fonts, with each color representing a level. Gifts also have a strong sense of level and rarity, creating a dignified experience for them.

These are what I've seen to be quite successful in high-end services. I've already talked a lot about vertical scenarios; domestic companies can localize their existing mature technologies and implement them locally. The demand and market potential are enormous.

What are the Most Common Pitfalls for Companies Expanding into the Middle East?

Monica

That's very insightful. We've discussed many opportunities; in your opinion, what are the most common pitfalls for Chinese startups expanding into the Middle East?

Gao Song

The most common pitfall, and indeed the most important one, is the need to transform one's mindset. Perhaps even now, many people contact me with the first sentence: "I produce such-and-such a product; can you find me a customer?" This is a very common mindset for many companies going global, which can be called the traditional trade 1.0 mindset for overseas expansion. They have a product and want to know if they can find customers to directly ship or transport the product to. This mindset is the first thing that needs to change; it simply won't work in the Middle East.

The Middle East doesn't lack products; Indians have been importing for decades, from Europe, America, Asia, and various other places. From a product perspective, there's no need for another competitor or supplier. The days of simply being a supplier will quickly become a thing of the past. Firstly, profit margins are very low, and competition is very high. What needs to be done is to escape this competition.

What needs to be done now is manufacturing upgrading or overseas expansion upgrading, breaking away from the vicious cycle. Everyone drives down prices, which drives down costs, which can lead to shoddy manufacturing and an increasingly poor international image for "Made in China." We need to break out of this cycle and pursue brand internationalization.

Brand internationalization has been widely discussed in the West, but in the Middle East, there's an inherent advantage for it. The Middle East is very keen for us to localize things together with them. If you partner with local companies or families, and integrate yourself as part of the UAE or Saudi Arabian lineage, this logic works very well for expanding into the Middle East. By becoming a Middle Eastern company, or a hybrid Middle Eastern company, Arabs will be very supportive.

First, they will feel that this is a local UAE company. If you bind with a family or cooperate with a local family, the family's inherent local influence and trusted endorsement are very strong. Arabs or locals will prioritize choosing the name of a local family. So, the first point is to change your mindset, abandon the traditional trade-oriented overseas expansion mindset, and embrace a brand-oriented, deeply localized overseas expansion mindset.

If you think about it, the cost isn't that high. If you engage in traditional trade-oriented overseas expansion, competing on price and profit margins, you'll eventually find it exhausting. You might save money, but you'll expend more energy, and the final experience will be very poor. However, if you pursue local overseas expansion and build a local brand, profit margins will increase, and your local influence will grow. These are all strong intangible benefits and advantages.

That's the first pitfall.

The second pitfall is a lack of understanding of local culture, which is a common deficiency for most people in China. After all, very few people truly understand the Middle East, and few have actually been there. Today happens to be the first day of Ramadan in the Middle East, coincidentally; observing the moon determines the start, and yesterday we saw the moon, so today is the first day. When Ramadan is mentioned, people might say they don't eat or drink, and wonder if society comes to a halt, or if they can also rest. In fact, quite the opposite; many companies see their performance surge during Ramadan. This is because Ramadan has strong family, gathering, and cultural attributes, offering many opportunities for engagement.

从迪拜飞到埃及，有一种从一线城市飞到十八线城市的感觉，尤其是开罗。我当时在开罗住了一年多，当时还住在富人区马阿迪，这个地方基本上是之前所有中企去开罗都会聚集的地方。但即使是所谓的富人区，旁边也有很多垃圾场，路修得也不好，没有行车线，路上车都是坑坑洼洼的，开车非常不规矩，很少见到交通信号灯。整个城市给人一种非常混乱、非常复杂的感觉。

但是好在即使如此，互联网渗透率还是非常高。凭借庞大的人口基数，做电商、移动支付、游戏行业在埃及这种巨型人口市场非常吃香。当时做一些冲DAU的软件，做冷启动第一站一般都会选择埃及，因为人口启动快、成本低，埃及人工成本也非常低，这是埃及的优势。但问题是ARPU很低，人的付费能力很低。

再加上网络基建水平也不高，至今仍然有很多埃及人没有办法时时刻刻用数据联络上网络。他们可能在车上听音乐，是提前在家里WiFi把音乐下载好，再带到车上去听。这个事情在国内来讲很不可思议，但确实存在，因为他们收入低，基建成本也比较高。互联网使用习惯很强，但不是每时每刻都能使用、都能接入的。

另外一个市场是伊拉克。伊拉克目前属于慢慢从战后阴霾中走出，我2024年9月重新去了一次伊拉克，当时是在巴格达、埃尔比勒这些城市，它们目前都属于比较稳定的状态。伊拉克有超过4500多万人口，石油非常多。跟埃及相似点是都是大型人口市场，但伊拉克付费能力还特别强。

目前来讲伊拉克有个很关键的问题是支付问题，因为跟美元的结汇还有一些问题，导致很多物资无法进来，很难购买外部的东西。政治基础设施比较薄弱，北边库区还有一些政治风险和安全风险。

以上这几个基本上是目前出海中东大家最关注的一些城市和国家。其他地方比如沙姆地区，叙利亚、约旦、巴勒斯坦地区，还是会时常受到政治影响、安全影响，对于开展业务有一定难度。再加上本身政府透明体系、对外商友好程度，不会像海湾地区或其他地区那么稳定。

再有一些市场，突尼斯和摩洛哥这些市场，更偏向于需要用欧洲的思维来做。突尼斯和摩洛哥都是欧洲进入非洲的入口，有时候讲摩洛哥是整个欧洲的后花园，曾经都是法国的殖民地。在这些国家通用语言是法语和阿拉伯语，还有本地柏柏尔语。做这些国家的市场是完全另外一种思维。做海湾市场可能更多要考虑现代化、兼顾不同国籍、不同用户。如果跑到摩洛哥、突尼斯这些国家，面对的市场、用户更多严重偏向欧洲风格，尤其是法国的用户习惯。他们对欧洲更熟悉，对法语更熟悉，对欧洲行为方式更熟悉，只在一些宗教或比较传统的层面上更偏向阿拉伯人或伊斯兰教。这里要做很多区分，完全不是铁板一块的状态。

所以我觉得大体上可以把这个区域分为这几个。

我们为什么推荐阿联酋，可能对很多人是反常识。迪拜这个地方几乎没有石油，迪拜在很早周边亲戚们都挖出石油以后，它没有石油，所以强迫它必须要找到另外一条生存路线。当时的酋长谢赫·拉希德·本·赛义德·阿勒马克图姆（Sheikh Rashid bin Saeed Al Maktoum）非常有远见，直接定义自己作为地区的枢纽，或者是地区的中转站。从1960、1970年代开始，就把自己的定位去做辐射周边国家的跳板。很早就开始做港口建设、自由区建设、税制改革和对外商友好的态度。最开始吸引了很多伊朗商人，因为伊朗在海对面。后面整个世界慢慢关注中东的时候，迪拜的地位就凸显了，甚至整个阿联酋成为了中东的门户。

现在说为什么全球都在看阿联酋？首先相比所有周边其他中东国家，阿联酋目前的政务建设非常一流，在很多地方可以媲美深圳政府。像深圳做很多政务，通过小程序或微信公众号就可以办理。同样在迪拜、阿联酋，政府都有自己的专门APP，迪拜警察有自己的APP，水电局也有自己的APP，都可以完成线上操作。包括房产买卖等很多东西都已经完全实现信息化了。

在使用门槛上来讲更友好。给大家讲一个趣事，2019年在埃及，去移民局办理工作居留签证。相信去过埃及工作的出海人都知道，埃及居留签证之前是很难办下来的。基本上每个周二早上9点就要过去移民局，没有排队，一窝蜂大家往很小的门挤进去，谁挤到前面谁就跑到前面。挤进去以后可以看到办证政务大厅，后面全部还是用纸质的储存个人档案。人又多，办事效率可能又有问题，经常会看到档案飞在地上或飞在空中。这反映了一个很常见的中东现状：实现信息化是很难得的事情，不像国内现在有这么好的条件。所以很多国家已经劝退了很多外资企业，透明度不够，信息化程度不够，导致效率不够。对于企业来讲效率就是生命，每拖一个礼拜，企业要烧很多钱。

在这一点上，阿联酋走在整个中东的前面。

再有阿联酋实行所谓零税，对自由区的合格企业不收企业所得税，外资可以100%控股所有在迪拜自由区、阿联酋自由区的企业，利润和资本可以自由在国际之间来往流动，不会做任何限制，不会管控外汇。这一点对欧洲人、东南亚、中国企业家非常友好。

再有阿联酋很早就接入到欧美体系。在阿联酋尤其在迪拜，实行两种法律。迪拜有一个专门的自由区叫DIFC，就是国际金融自由区，实行的是完整的common law，英美法，完全无缝对接欧美企业。所以看到欧美的银行、券商等大型跨国集团，首站都会选择DIFC，因为从法律上来讲完全融合了它们的法律生态。其他地区迪拜实行自己的大陆法。

从法律和商业环境上来讲，阿联酋只有10%的本地人，剩下全都是外国人。对于企业来讲，人才招聘是很大的优势。在这里很容易招到全世界不同国家的人才，招聘难度比较低，大家彼此之间流动。圈子基本上固定在阿布扎比和迪拜这两个城市里面。

再有很多人提到迪拜、中东，会想到会不会有战争，或者会不会有诈骗。其实之前去也有亲身体验，整个迪拜、阿联酋非常安全，跟国内基本上毫无二致。女生晚上一个人在迪拜市中心走完全没有任何问题，非常安全。很多在迪拜住的人都不锁门，家里不需要锁门，也没有人会进去偷东西。假如在商场把手机、电脑或书包丢在那里，可能三个小时后回来，一定还会在那里。因为阿联酋对于签证管控很严格，来到阿联酋的人基本上都是外派务工人员或投资人。2022年之前都很难见到老人小孩，阿联酋就是一个打工人和投资人的城市。

从安全的角度上来讲，非常适合不同国家迁入。甚至对于欧洲人来讲，他们愿意去阿联酋就是因为比欧洲更安全，没有偷、没有抢、没有打砸，而且还没有个人所得税，这里是零。2025年上半年，有大量的英国人、法国人、意大利人从欧洲跑到阿联酋。甚至在英国报纸上刊登让大家不要再去UAE了，留在UK。因为税首先可能省30%-40%，相当于涨薪一半了。

再有这边阳光比较充足，生活成本也比欧洲稍微低一点，也比欧洲安全。所以从各个角度上来讲，大家更愿意选择这里。从收入、安全包括方方面面，这里是一个很大的热潮。

美国对中东现在也有很多押注。美国可能是我们未来AI或科技行业最大的竞争对手，但目前看到至少在政府层面上，美国已经在整个中东有大量布局。像是微软在沙特、阿联酋都有大量差不多150亿美元做数据中心，还有做云服务基础设施的投资。甚至对阿联酋国有公司G42科技集团进行股权投资。

在这里我想先总结一下，为什么说现在已经到了必须要去做中东出海的阶段，已经到了所谓占位的时间点。离中国最近的自由港是香港或新加坡，香港就不用说了，新加坡是非常精英、非常成熟，因为整个国土面积的限制，是非常完整或已经竞争很饱和的地方，它欢迎精英中的精英。

玩这方面就更多了。中东人从5月开始一直到10月，在整个海湾地区基本上都是室外保持50度左右。50度也就意味着完全零室外活动，在室内可以做的文章就太多了。哪怕讲迪拜是非常成熟的中东城市，迪拜比较大型的购物中心也就那么几个，The Dubai Mall、Mall of Emirates、Dubai Hills Mall、Marina Mall，加起来不超过6个。

印度人的风格大家是知道的，有很多在应用实践层面上肯定不如中国人做得完善、真实。欧美服务或产品往往是按照传统欧美做产品的逻辑去做，没有办法做到像中国这么细致、贴近用户生活，甚至去做内卷的地步。而且欧美定价方式一般来讲肯定比国内定价方式要高。所以在这块竞争优势还是很多的。

突然市场上来了一个《苏丹的复仇》，要扮演历史上伟大的萨拉丁或阿拉伯英雄，代表阿拉伯去征战世界。这是稀缺，真正的发现这个公司尊重我。阿拉伯人的特点是更在乎有没有尊重他。国内一些企业家或做事，可能不需要管那么多，只要大家把利益拿到就可以了。但对于阿拉伯来讲，一定要获得足够有的尊重。哪怕deal是很好的deal，但觉得不尊重，或者哪块看不顺眼了，这事就得等等，得聊成朋友，能充分感受到尊重才行。

这种身份认同，哪怕是我在大厂期间，还是会发现行业代表顶尖水平的一些公司，都没有办法很好地做本地化。本地化是很宽泛的概念。一方面最基本的阿拉伯语适配，阿拉伯语从右往左写，字母有连符。这个事情不在一线做的人都不知道，用PS做图要做阿拉伯语，都有专门重新调配语言选项，否则阿拉伯语打出来是乱码。

再有讲文化适配，尤其是埃及、沙特这些地方对于阿拉伯禁忌讲究很多。游戏里面皮肤的裸露、一些动作行为，甚至酒精、猪肉这些东西都不可以出现，甚至不可以擦边呈现。但凡有怀疑倾向都会被封杀。之前经常看到国内直播软件去到中东就被封杀，国内很多觉得不擦边的东西，放到那里完全不合适。女生露多少皮肤，这些事情都很敏感。

再有阿拉伯人都是以家庭为单位，有很强的部落意识。哪怕现在沙特、阿联酋都是类似部落制，酋长国本质还是部落。酋长作为酋长国领导人，在整个酋长国推行政策或实行改革的时候，都要联合本地几大家族一起做事。

整个管理方式不是民主，甚至不是很现代，是非常封建的方式，但这就是国情。所以必须要考虑到对于家庭、部落、团体身份认同的需求。

再有从尊贵的角度上来讲，阿拉伯人非常希望自己能够获得尊贵的体验。这种尊贵体验具体可以体现在哪些？可以研究，在变现这方面，国内直播公司已经做到登峰造极的地步。各种头像框可以分10级，发语言可以分五颜六色的字，每一种字都是级别的代表，发的礼物都有很强的级别和稀有感，给他们制造尊贵的体验。

这些是我看到的在高端服务这块比较成功的。垂直场景刚刚已经讲了很多，国内公司都可以把国内现有的一些成熟技术去做好本地化，在本地落地。需求和市场的潜力都是非常大的。

中东不缺产品，进口几十年前印度人就在做进口了，从欧洲、美洲、亚洲等各个地方进口。从产品角度上来讲，不需要多一个竞争者或供应商。继续再去做供应商的日子很快就会一去不复返。第一点利润率非常低，竞争非常高，要做的其实是逃避，逃开这种竞争。

现在要做的是制造业升级或出海升级，摆脱恶性循环。大家彼此压价格，压价格去压成本，压成本可能导致粗制滥造，导致Made in China在国际形象越来越差。要跳脱出这个循环，做品牌出海。

品牌出海在欧美已经有很多人讲怎么去做，在中东还有一个天然做品牌出海的优势。中东很希望我们去跟它一起落地一些事情，如果去跟本地一些公司或家族一起落地，把自己也成为阿联酋或沙特血脉融入到自己的一部分，这个逻辑在中东出海就非常行得通。把自己已经成为了一个中东企业，或混血的中东企业，中东人会非常支持。

首先他们会觉得这是阿联酋本地自己的公司，如果绑定一个家族或跟本地家族合作，家族天然在本地的影响力和信任背书就非常强。阿拉伯人或本地人都会优先选择本地家族的名字。所以第一点一定要改变思路，抛弃传统贸易型出海思维，一定要做深耕本地落地的品牌出海思维。

其实去想的话，成本没有那么高。做传统贸易型出海，卷价格、卷利润率，最后会发现做得很累，可能在钱上面省了，但在精力上面耗费的东西更多，最后体验也非常差。但如果自己去做本地出海、本地品牌，利润率会变高，在本地影响力增大，这些都是很强的隐形收益和好处。

这是第一个坑。

第二个坑是对于本地文化的了解，这是国内大部分人的欠缺。毕竟真的很少人了解中东，很少人去到中东。今天正好也是中东斋月的第一天，非常巧，看定斋月是要看月亮，昨天看了月亮发现今天是第一天。提到斋月大家会说他们不吃饭、不喝水，是不是整个社会就停了，是不是也可以休息了？其实恰恰相反，很多公司业务只有在斋月的时候业绩反而冲得特别高。因为斋月有很强的家庭属性、聚会属性、文化属性，在这上面可以做的文章非常多。

现在去到阿联酋，会发现所有品牌都在搞一个叫iftar的东西。iftar是阿拉伯语，意思就是早餐。在封斋过程中，从早上太阳升起以后就不吃不喝，一直到晚上黄昏太阳落下，可以吃一天的第一顿饭，那个时候可能是晚上六七点，但这是第一天的第一顿饭，也叫早餐，所以叫iftar，吃早餐，早餐会。

华为包括很多ToC企业都会去做自己品牌的iftar，甚至会在清真寺做一些慈善活动。这些都是最好的去宣传自己品牌的时候，也是所有穆斯林一天最放松、最快乐的时候。这个时候去做品牌宣传、品牌推广，都是非常有效的。

因为讲尊重的事情所以想到之前见到的一个很糟糕的事情，在埃及的中国管理者的风格在国内很常见，经常骂下属，你怎么这么笨，不想干有的人是干，很多这种话。在埃及或阿拉伯其实是行不通的，他们会觉得非常不被尊重。当时亲眼看到中国管理者跟埃及员工打架，这是很让人难过的事情。这位中国管理者真的讨厌阿拉伯人吗，其实也没有。很多管理层刀子嘴豆腐心，或一瞬间着急了，没有真的意思说你就是笨蛋、很蠢，但说者无意听者有心，很多事情不可以跨过那条线。

这个事情真的导致当时这位中国管理者和埃及员工直接打起来了，埃及员工一巴掌就呼上去了。这个事情真的会发生，在中国员工还敢打老板、打经理是不可能的。所以对本地文化的一些理解，包括有些国内男老板去到中东，看到穆斯林美女，商务礼仪上握手，但其实不可以握，男生不可以握女生的手。哪怕在商务场合，男老板也不可以握对面女生的手，除非人家主动伸手。一般来讲把手放在胸口给人家点点头，致意一下就可以了。很多这些小细节大家都是不了解的。

这些是中东的文化，包括家族文化在中东也是很盛行的。往往在一些比较大的赛道，其实都是有山头的，某些家族都是垄断了这个东西。比如在阿联酋看一些汽车，可能就是Al-Futtaim家族。阿联酋所有的商超，大部分家乐福也都是属于Al-Futtaim家族的。其他一些家族，比较大的Al Habtoor家族、Al Ghurair家族、Sajwani家族，都在自己的行业已经有非常强大的影响力了。所以如何进入到一个行业，如何与这些巨头竞争、合作，其实都是一种艺术。

这是第三个点了。还有一个，真正想好要出海的时候，满足了第一点，要做本地化、品牌出海、本地深耕的想法没有问题。那么接下来是怎么把市场调研做好。刚刚也讲整个中东市场不是铁板一块的，进入阿联酋市场也只是进入一个门户而已。哪怕主体设立在阿联酋，要在埃及做生意，要在非洲其他国家做生意，还是要研究本地的习惯。不同国家、不同市场差异非常大。

在阿联酋，是很国际化的，会有很强烈竞争的自由竞争环境。这个地方客户群非常多样，人口不多1000多万，但可能要应付至少做四五种语言，竞争者是来自欧美、印度、中国、日本其他国家。竞争对手、客户群都不一样。如果跑到埃及、摩洛哥、伊拉克去做，虽然人口那么多，但用户画像高度相似，就不需要考虑做很多种语言、做很多种用户不同习惯。但这个时候会发现支付能力差很多，企业商务习惯又很不一样。所以要想好，产品或服务更适用于哪一种，哪个国家、哪种行业、哪种习惯。这些可能是要去避免的一些坑。

高嵩
