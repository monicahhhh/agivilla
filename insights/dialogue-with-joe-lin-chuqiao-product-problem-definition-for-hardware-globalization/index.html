Product Problem Definition Methodology

Monica

Thank you for sharing, Joe.

Product problem definition has always been a core focus for me. There's a saying: "If you have an hour to build a product, spend 55 minutes defining the problem and 5 minutes solving it." Many teams with limited entrepreneurial experience often don't invest enough in the problem definition stage. From my discussions with Joe, I've learned that defining problems for hardware products also needs to be thoroughly thought through from the beginning. I'd like to ask Joe, what methodologies do you use when communicating product problem definition with startup teams, or how do you guide them to review?

Joe

When I engage in any product-related collaboration, I always start with a core question: Why do users need your brand? This question is worth spending 10 minutes, 20 minutes, or even half a year to a year for the team to ponder – what value can the brand's existence bring to users? This value includes both traditional physical and functional values, as well as the increasingly important emotional value.

With the global supply chain becoming increasingly perfect, product homogenization is severe. Why would users choose you over a competitor? Just as GoPro action cameras already existed early on, Insta360 still managed to rise; after Jackery, EcoFlow still broke through in the portable power station field. There are also brands like yoose, Govee, and Bambu Lab, whose success stems from precisely capturing a core point of user demand.

Before sharing methodologies, everyone must first clearly understand this core question. Once clear, we can then break down specific product definition methodologies. Next, I will analyze this by combining 6 case studies, and also incorporate our own entrepreneurial experience – how we seized a core point to secure our first order, achieve cash flow recovery, and sustain it to this day.

Monica

Okay, today we will dissect 6 highly representative products, and the content promises to be very exciting. Without further ado, let's dive straight into the case studies. Joe, which product do you think is suitable to start with?

Joe

I've categorized the 6 cases into three types, expanding on the "Demand - Pain Point - Experience" three-stage methodology: The first category includes portable power station brands Jackery and EcoFlow; the second category features Govee LED strip lights and Bambu Lab 3D printers; and the third category covers yoose shavers and Insta360 panoramic action cameras.

Among these brands, by comparing cases from different regions and markets, everyone can gain a clearer understanding of the core logic of product definition.

Returning to the methodology itself, the core of product definition is to determine its stage of user value: demand stage, pain point stage, or experience stage. Any product (including software) is inseparable from these three stages. Once the stage is clear, the direction of product definition becomes much clearer.

Case Study One: Jackery vs EcoFlow

Joe

First, let's share the first set of cases – Jackery and EcoFlow.

The originator of outdoor power stations is the American brand Goal Zero, but Jackery was able to catch up and surpass it primarily by accurately identifying a demand gap in the overseas market. At that time, Jackery already had a considerable presence in the domestic power bank market, but industry competition was fierce. To find a second growth curve, the team turned its attention overseas.

Around 2015-2016, Jackery launched its first overseas product: a 300-watt, 0.5 kWh outdoor power station, precisely targeting the fishing user group. These users needed to power oxygen supply equipment for fish and other small appliances. Traditional diesel generators were noisy and easily scared fish away, while Jackery's product was quiet and had sufficient power, perfectly meeting this underserved demand, making it a typical demand-stage product. The product design was engineering-oriented, and while its appearance wasn't outstanding, it had almost no competitors between 2015-2019. By accurately capturing demand, Jackery's global market share grew from over $100 million to $700-800 million.

EcoFlow, founded in 2017, seized the opportunity in the pain point stage. In 2019, EcoFlow launched its first product, with the core innovation being the adoption of bidirectional inverter technology, which compressed charging time from Jackery's 6 hours to 90-100 minutes.

By this time, the outdoor power station user base had gradually matured, but slow charging had become a core pain point – users who spontaneously decided to go camping or fishing had to wait a long time for charging, severely impacting their experience. EcoFlow positioned "fast charging" as its core selling point, solving this pain point through technological innovation.

After the global pandemic outbreak in 2020, long-distance travel was restricted, and short-distance activities like outdoor camping surged, significantly increasing user demand for outdoor power stations. From late 2020 to 2021, Japan introduced a policy to popularize emergency equipment, making outdoor power stations essential. Under the combination of multiple factors, EcoFlow's sales experienced explosive growth.

As the market developed, user demand for product appearance gradually increased. Jackery's early products featured an engineering design with a black casing and orange vents, but later they also launched products with softer color schemes, gradually transitioning from the demand stage to the experience stage.

In product definition, demand and pain points sometimes coexist. For example, while current outdoor power stations have solved the charging speed issue, their excessive weight is unfriendly to female users, and there is still room for optimization in terms of volume, noise, and waterproof performance. For the aging Japanese market, modular and miniaturized designs might become new breakthroughs.

Monica

Judging from Jackery and EcoFlow products, their exterior designs are mostly square boxes. I'd like to ask, from a product design perspective, what is the future extension space for such products? Will they move towards a lighter, more portable direction? Is the current square design limited by internal configurations?

Joe

This is a very critical question. The core of design is user-centric thinking, and user-centric thinking needs to be dynamically adjusted in conjunction with the product's demand, pain point, and experience stages.

Early Jackery was in the demand stage, and the market was a growth market. As long as the core demand could be met, a relatively crude appearance didn't deter users from buying. EcoFlow, on the other hand, was in the pain point stage, emphasizing a "black technology, high-end" positioning, hence adopting black and silver as primary colors to enhance technological feel, differentiating itself from Jackery's "low capacity, high cost-effectiveness."

Later, BLUETTI emerged, further segmenting the market with "large capacity, high cost-effectiveness" as its selling point. When the market enters the experience stage, product appearance and detailed innovation become key differentiators. For example, some brands try to incorporate retractable tabletops into outdoor power stations, or design smaller, softer-colored products specifically for female users.

The core reason for the square design is internal structural limitations: the bottom houses the battery, and the top contains the inverter and control panel. A square structure maximizes space utilization and ensures capacity and performance. Even so, different brands still differentiate themselves through detailed design.

For example, in the design of high-power products above 1500 watts, EcoFlow introduced a dual-handle model for the Texas market in the US. Local users are mostly male, and the dual-handle design better conveys a sense of strength, fitting their usage scenarios and aesthetic preferences, thus sales were significantly higher than single-handle models.

Zendure, on the other hand, targeted the European market by launching outdoor power stations with pull handles, featuring a more delicate and elegant design that aligns with European users' aesthetics and habits, also addressing the pain point of portability for female users.

It's clear that product design needs to be deeply integrated with the stage, user positioning, brand identity, and target market. The demand stage emphasizes functionality, the pain point stage focuses on solutions, and the experience stage prioritizes aesthetics and personalization. Only by clarifying core user needs can design become a competitive advantage for the product.

Case Study Two: Bambu Lab vs Govee

Monica

Very interesting analysis. Let's move on to the next set of cases – Bambu Lab and Govee.

Joe

The core highlight of these two brands is their precise entry into the experience stage, where they not only solve pain points but also create emotional experiences that transcend functional value.

Bambu Lab's success first stems from solving a core pain point of 3D printers – printing speed. But more crucially, it built an extremely complete user community: on its official website, users can freely upload and download 3D modeling files, allowing even users who can't model to directly download files for printing.

This design created a virtuous UGC (User-Generated Content) ecosystem: users spontaneously share their modeling works, some high-quality content can be set for paid download, and both uploaders and the platform can earn revenue. This also generates social currency for the product, leading to organic spread and continuous growth of private domain traffic.

Govee's logic is similar to Bambu Lab's. Early LED strip products only needed to meet the basic demand of "emitting light." Later, functional upgrades like "eye protection, stepless color change" gradually appeared, but Govee focused on "ambient experience."

Govee LED strips boast approximately 16 million color variations, offering extremely rich colors, and they also built a dedicated user community. Users can share their lighting setup solutions for different scenarios, such as gaming, movie watching, or music synchronization, and can even program their own lighting modes and upload them.

This design stimulates users' desire to share and feel recognized – when a user's lighting scheme is adopted by others, they gain strong psychological satisfaction. This emotional value far surpasses the functional value of the LED strip itself. I heard that some users earned $15,000 in commissions by uploading programming files, further enhancing the community's activity and creativity.

The cases of Bambu Lab and Govee demonstrate that when the market enters the experience stage, product competitiveness not only comes from functionality and pain point resolution but also from the ability to meet users' emotional and social needs, enabling users to spontaneously become brand advocates.

Case Study Three: Insta360 vs yoose

Joe

Next, let's share the third set of cases – Insta360 and yoose.

Insta360's breakthrough logic was to precisely capture two core points in the GoPro-dominated action camera market: pain point resolution and experience upgrade.

One of GoPro's core pain points is that the selfie stick is exposed, affecting shooting results. Insta360 launched a panoramic action camera, completely solving this problem. At the same time, Insta360 observed the strong desire of extreme sports users to share, and optimized the sharing process – after shooting, content can be directly shared to social media with one click on a phone or device, eliminating tedious export and editing steps.

Extreme sports themselves have a strong visual impact, and quick sharing maximizes content dissemination efficiency, forming a closed loop of "shooting - sharing - spreading." Insta360, with this combination of innovations, gradually eroded GoPro's market share, and coupled with the cost advantages of China's supply chain, quickly grew into an industry benchmark.

The success of yoose shavers, on the other hand, was a precise entry into the emotional value of the experience stage. In 2020, yoose launched its first shaver product in China. Initial sales were mediocre, but it was accidentally discovered on Xiaohongshu that users were giving it as a gift to their boyfriends, with excellent feedback.

yoose's parent company is a design company, and the product itself has a strong sense of design. It happened to solve the pain point of "difficulty in finding gifts for men" – belts and similar gifts are too personal, while shavers are a daily necessity for men, and a well-designed product is more suitable as a gift.

yoose quickly adjusted its marketing strategy, focusing on its gift attribute, and priced it in the 300-400 RMB range. Its core purchasing group was not male users, but women choosing gifts for their boyfriends, and product reviews were full of positive feedback like "my girlfriend gave it to me, I love it."

In 2020, Douyin was transitioning from entertainment to commercialization and began supporting high-ticket products. yoose leveraged this traffic bonus, rapidly scaling through short video marketing. In 2021-2022, its annual revenue reached 600 million RMB, and it has now invited Wang Yibo as an endorser, becoming a leading brand in the domestic shaver market.

It should be noted that if yoose were to expand overseas, it would need to redefine its product – the characteristics of male beards differ between East and West, and overseas gift-giving culture also varies from China. Markets with a preference for elegance, such as Japan or Europe, might be more suitable for its product positioning.

Monica

A truly brilliant case analysis! I'd like to confirm, aside from product positioning and exterior design, does the yoose shaver have no significant functional differences from ordinary shavers in terms of user experience?

Joe

From a functional perspective, the core experience difference between yoose and ordinary shavers is not significant; I personally prefer manual razors. However, its key to success lies in capturing the niche scenario of gift-giving, differentiating itself through design aesthetics and emotional value. If this positioning had been clear from the beginning, it might have reduced some advertising costs, but it is undoubtedly a highly successful case of scenario entry.

Observations and Thoughts on AI Hardware Products

Monica

2025 has been a year of explosive growth for AI hardware, with many innovative forms emerging, such as emotional companionship products. Joe, how do you view this wave of hardware development? Are there any products that you find particularly interesting?

Joe

I'd like to share a few highly representative products.

First is Plaud, an ultra-thin recording patch compatible with iPhone. Its core highlight is its integration with large AI models, which can quickly convert recordings into text, greatly improving information processing efficiency.

The application of AI in hardware should primarily solve pain points or optimize experiences. Early companionship products already existed, and the addition of AI technology makes intelligent interaction smoother, pushing consumer electronics into a new cycle of updates – just as VR appeared as early as 2015-2016, but only gained new momentum when Apple entered the field in 2022-2023; AI was already sought after by capital in 2016-2018, and now, with the help of large model technology, it has achieved breakthroughs at the application layer.

However, many current AI hardware products suffer from "AI for AI's sake," fundamentally failing to clarify what problem AI is meant to solve. For example, some AI companion robots still have immature emotional interaction experiences, which may require another 2-3 years of technological iteration to reach an ideal effect.

Another notable product is an AI telescope, which solves a core pain point in outdoor observation – when spotting rare birds or other targets, by the time you take a photo, the target might have moved away. This product can automatically record observed targets, identify species information, and quickly provide feedback to the user, saving the user time spent searching and photographing.

Also, something that has impressed me greatly recently is LEGO's smart module – it didn't blindly incorporate AI, but instead returned to the user. LEGO's core users are children, and the buyers are parents. Parents want their children to enjoy the fun of building, but are concerned about screen time and data security.

LEGO's smart module doesn't have a camera and doesn't collect any user data. It only interacts via NFC chips – after users build models like ships or robots, pairing them with this module allows them to emit corresponding sound effects, preserving the core fun of building while enhancing product enjoyment through intelligence. Later, users only need to replace different NFC chips to match sound effects to different models, at a very low cost.

This product proves that not all hardware needs to be tied to AI. Returning to user needs, pain points, and experiences is what creates truly valuable products. LEGO precisely balanced children's experience with parents' concerns, making it a case study worth reflecting on for all practitioners in the AI wave.

Monica

I completely agree! AI is just an enabling tool; the "demand - pain point - experience" three-stage logic for products is the eternal underlying methodology.

I've always had a question: the exterior design of hardware products sometimes feels a bit "mystical," for example, for companion products, designing them as certain animals, colors, or whether they have limbs – these decisions seem hard to quantify. Joe, when actually designing such products, what are some entry points?

Joe

The core of design is actually very simple: it goes back to the user – first, define who you are serving, and then specifically meet their needs and aesthetics. For example, for trendy toys, the core users are anime and cosplay enthusiasts, so the design needs to align with their aesthetic preferences; for Bambu Lab, the core users are geeks, so the product design needs to emphasize a sense of technology; users of ambient lights are more concerned with emotional experience, so the appearance and lighting effects need to convey a feeling of tranquility and comfort.

The essence of a user's purchase is either for functional value or emotional value. Emotional value can manifest as IP recognition, social showing off, or a sense of companionship. For example, with Labubu trendy toys, some users see them as symbols of good luck or companions, and this emotional connection is the core of the design.

Let me share an interesting product – a "phone-locking self-discipline artifact." Users can set a locking time of 10 minutes or 1 hour, and the phone cannot be opened until the time is up. Its founder initially designed it to curb phone addiction while studying for postgraduate exams. Initial sales were poor, but it went viral after a Douyin blogger used it to tease her boyfriend. In 2021, the number of postgraduate exam takers nationwide reached 7.5 million, and this "self-discipline artifact" precisely captured the needs of test-takers. Priced at 129 RMB, although imitators later pushed prices down to 69-79 RMB, the product achieved 15 million RMB in revenue that year, becoming a phenomenal product.

Our first entrepreneurial product was a "chicken dinner artifact," a physical accessory for mobile games. At the time, similar products on the market ranged from a dozen to 39 RMB, but the experience was poor. We found that core users were hardcore gamers, who were not price-sensitive but more concerned with immersive operational experience.

This product, with an investment of only about 100,000 RMB, secured a cooperation order with Black Shark gaming phones due to its ultimate experience design. During the pandemic in 2020, demand for home gaming surged, and the product became the top seller in its category on JD.com after its launch. It later appeared at product launches for Redmi and OnePlus phones, with its unit price increasing to 79 RMB, nearly double that of similar products.

Subsequently, we also launched gaming phone cooling back clips, continuing to delve into the needs of gaming users. OnePlus products emphasize a sense of quality; we adjusted the product's curvature for nearly a month to make it more appealing and high-end. Black Shark products, on the other hand, highlight an esports feel, enhancing the immersive gaming experience through design and sound effects.

The core logic is: first serve one type of person well, then expand to a group of people. Once a brand establishes recognition within a specific demographic, then consider expanding to a second growth curve. If you try to sell to everyone from the start, you might end up serving no one well.

Product definition is multi-dimensional, involving not only the product itself but also technology, channels, promotion, pricing, and more. For example, channel selection – whether to enter Best Buy, Walmart, or Amazon, TikTok, etc. – must be based on user positioning. But at its essence, the core is always the "user" – clarify why users need you, what value stage they are in, and then tailor product design and operations accordingly.

Monica

Absolutely brilliant! The core is to focus on a specific group of people and solve their particular experiences or pain points.

Joe

Exactly. For example, products for business professionals need to be high-end, simple, and professional in design; products for young users can be cuter and more lively. The key is to accurately identify the core needs and aesthetic preferences of the target group.

Monica

Understood. Joe, which city are you usually based in?

Joe

I'm based in Shenzhen, and my work is primarily focused here.

嘉宾介绍

Monica

今天为大家隆重介绍我们的嘉宾 ——Joe 林楚乔。

他毕业于中央美术学院，现任美院客座讲师，拥有十余年工业设计经验，曾担任 OPPO 新事业部产品与设计负责人，2020 年创业创办科技类设计公司，如今还独立拆分设计板块，专注为出海工厂及初创品牌提供产品定义与设计服务。接下来有请 Joe 做详细介绍。

感谢 Monica 的介绍。正如直播预告海报所提，我深耕工业设计领域十余年，从 TP-LINK 到 OPPO，全程参与新事业部从战略规划到产品落地的全流程，积累了丰富的产品实战经验。2020 年创业后，公司在 2026 年 1 月 14 日恰好迎来六周年。

从 2025 年开始，我们将设计业务独立拆分，核心原因是发现中国供应链实力强劲，众多工厂与初创品牌纷纷布局出海，但很多团队在产品定义与品牌定位初期就存在偏差。产品前期定义失误，会导致后续量产、营销、渠道推广等环节连锁受挫，投入大量时间、人力与资金成本却收效甚微。

因此，我们在对接客户时，会先帮其梳理用户群体、渠道定位与品牌核心价值，甚至劝退过不少定义模糊的项目。在当前竞争激烈的市场环境下，如何打造优质产品并成长为品牌，是我今天想与大家深入分享的内容，希望能给正在出海或创业的伙伴带来启发。

产品问题定义方法论

Monica

谢谢 Joe 的分享。

产品问题定义一直是我关注的核心，有句话说：“若用一小时做产品，应花 55 分钟定义问题，5 分钟解决问题”。很多创业经验不足的团队，往往在问题定义阶段投入不足，而从与 Joe 的交流中发现，硬件产品的问题定义同样需要从一开始就想透彻。想请教 Joe，在与创业团队沟通产品问题定义时，你会采用哪些方法论，或引导他们如何复盘？

Joe

我对接所有产品相关合作时，都会先抛出一个核心问题：用户为什么需要你的品牌？这个问题值得团队花 10 分钟、20 分钟，甚至半年到一年去思考 —— 品牌的出现能为用户带来什么价值？这种价值既包括传统的物理、功能性价值，也涵盖当下愈发重要的情绪价值。

当前全球供应链日趋完善，产品同质化严重，用户为何选择你而非竞品？就像早期已有 GoPro 运动相机，Insta360 仍能崛起；电小二 Jackery 之后，正浩 EcoFlow 依然能在便携电源领域突围，还有有色 yoose、智岩 Govee、拓竹 Bambu 等品牌，他们的成功都源于精准抓住了用户需求的某个核心点。

在分享方法论前，大家务必先想清楚这个核心问题。明确后，我们再拆解产品定义的具体方法论，接下来我会结合 6 个案例同步分析，也会结合我们自己的创业经历 —— 如何抓住一个核心点拿下第一笔订单，实现现金流回笼并持续至今。

Monica

好的，今天我们将拆解 6 个极具代表性的产品，内容非常精彩。话不多说，我们直接进入案例分享，Joe 你觉得从哪个产品开始讲起合适？

Joe

我将 6 个案例分为三类，结合 “需求 - 痛点 - 体验” 三阶段方法论展开：第一类是便携电源品牌电小二 Jackery 与正浩 EcoFlow；第二类是智岩 Govee 灯带与拓竹 Bambu 3D 打印机；第三类是有色 yoose 剃须刀与 影石 Insta360 全景运动相机。

这些品牌中，通过不同区域、不同市场的案例对比，能让大家更清晰地理解产品定义的核心逻辑。

回到方法论本身，产品定义的核心是判断其所处的用户价值阶段：需求阶段、痛点阶段或体验阶段。任何产品（包括软件）都离不开这三个阶段，明确阶段后，产品定义的方向会更加清晰。

案例拆解一：电小二 Jackery vs 正浩 EcoFlow

Joe

首先分享第一组案例 ——电小二 Jackery 与正浩 EcoFlow。

户外电源的鼻祖是美国品牌 Goal Zero，而 Jackery 能后来居上，核心在于找准了海外市场的需求缺口。当时 Jackery 在国内充电宝市场已颇具规模，但行业竞争激烈，为寻找第二增长曲线，团队将目光投向海外。

大概2015-2016 年，Jackery 推出首款海外产品：300 瓦、0.5 度电的户外电源，精准瞄准钓鱼用户群体。这类用户需要为鱼供氧设备及其他小电器供电，传统柴油发电机噪音大、易吓跑鱼，而 Jackery 产品安静且电量充足，恰好满足了这一未被充分满足的需求，属于典型的需求阶段产品。该产品设计偏向工程化，外观虽不突出，但在 2015-2019 年期间几乎没有竞争对手，凭借精准的需求捕捉，Jackery 全球市场份额从 1 亿多美元增长至 7-8 亿美元。

2017 年成立的正浩 EcoFlow，则抓住了痛点阶段的机会。2019 年，正浩推出首款产品，核心创新是采用双向逆变技术，将充电时间从 Jackery 的 6 小时压缩至 90-100 分钟。

此时户外电源用户群体已逐渐成熟，但充电慢成为核心痛点 —— 用户临时决定外出露营或钓鱼时，需长时间等待充电，严重影响使用体验。正浩以 “快速充电” 为核心卖点，通过技术创新解决了这一痛点。

2020 年全球疫情爆发后，长途旅游受限，户外露营等短途活动频次激增，用户对户外电源的需求大幅提升；2020 年底至 2021 年，日本推出应急装备普及政策，户外电源成为必备品，多重因素叠加下，正浩销量实现爆发式增长。

随着市场发展，用户对产品外观的要求逐渐提高。Jackery 早期产品采用黑色外壳 + 橙色通风口的工程化设计，后期也推出了配色更柔和的产品，从需求阶段逐步向体验阶段过渡。

产品定义中，需求与痛点有时会并行存在。比如当前户外电源虽解决了充电速度问题，但重量过大对女性用户不友好，体积、噪音、防水性能等仍有优化空间；针对日本老龄化市场，模块化、小型化设计可能成为新的突破点。

Monica

从 Jackery 和 EcoFlow 的产品来看，外观设计多为方形盒子，想请教从产品设计角度，这类产品未来的延展空间在哪里？是否会向更轻便、更易携带的方向发展？当前方形设计是否受限于内部配置？

Joe

这个问题非常关键，设计的核心是用户思维，而用户思维需结合产品所处的需求、痛点、体验阶段动态调整。

早期 Jackery 处于需求阶段，市场属于增量市场，只要能满足核心需求，外观相对简陋也不影响用户买单。而正浩处于痛点阶段，主打 “黑科技、高端” 定位，因此采用黑色与银色为主的配色，强化科技感，与 Jackery 的 “低容量、高性价比” 形成差异化。

后续出现的德兰明海 BLUETTI，则以 “大容量、高性价比” 为卖点，进一步细分市场。当市场进入体验阶段，产品外观与细节创新成为差异化关键，比如部分品牌尝试在户外电源中加入可展开桌板，或针对女性用户设计更小巧、配色更柔和的产品。

方形设计的核心原因是内部结构限制：底部为电池，上方为逆变器和操作面板，方形结构能最大化利用空间、保证容量与性能。即便如此，不同品牌仍通过细节设计形成差异。

比如 EcoFlow 在 1500 瓦以上的大功率产品设计中，针对美国德州市场推出双把手款式。当地用户多为男性，双把手设计更能彰显力量感，契合其使用场景与审美偏好，因此销量显著高于单把手款式。

而疆海科技 Zendure 则针对欧洲市场，推出带拉杆的户外电源，设计更秀气优雅，更符合欧洲用户的审美与使用习惯，也解决了女性用户携带不便的痛点。

可见，产品设计需与阶段、用户定位、品牌调性及目标市场深度绑定。需求阶段重功能，痛点阶段重解决方案，体验阶段重审美与个性化，只有明确用户核心诉求，才能让设计成为产品竞争力的加分项。

案例拆解二：拓竹 Bambu vs 智岩 Govee

Monica

非常有意思的分析，我们继续推进下一组案例 —— 拓竹 Bambu 和智岩 Govee。

Joe

这两个品牌的核心亮点是精准切入体验阶段，在解决痛点的同时，打造了超越功能性价值的情绪体验。

拓竹 Bambu 的成功，首先源于解决了 3D 打印机的核心痛点 —— 打印速度。但更关键的是，它构建了极为完善的用户社群：官网社区中，用户可自由上传、下载 3D 建模文件，不会建模的用户也能直接下载文件进行打印。

这一设计形成了良性的 UGC 生态：用户自发分享建模作品，部分优质内容可设置付费下载，上传者与平台均可获得收益，同时也为产品带来了自发传播的社交货币，实现私域流量的持续增长。

智岩 Govee 的逻辑与拓竹相似。早期灯带产品仅需满足 “发光” 的基础需求，后续逐渐出现 “护目、无极变色” 等功能升级，而 Govee 则将核心放在 “氛围体验” 上。

Govee 灯带大概拥有 1600 万种色域，颜色丰富度极高，同时搭建了专属用户社区。用户可分享灯带的场景布置方案，比如电竞、观影、音乐适配等不同场景的灯光效果，还能自主编程灯光模式并上传。

这种设计激发了用户的分享欲与认同感 —— 用户的灯光方案被他人选用，能获得强烈的心理满足，这种情绪价值远超越灯带本身的功能性价值。好像听说有用户通过上传编程文件获得了 15,000 美元分佣，进一步强化了社区的活跃度与创造力。

拓竹与 Govee 的案例证明，当市场进入体验阶段，产品竞争力不仅源于功能与痛点解决，更在于能否满足用户的情绪需求与社交需求，让用户自发成为品牌的传播者。

案例拆解三：影石 Insta360 vs 有色yoose

Joe

接下来分享第三组案例 —— 影石 Insta360 与有色 yoose。

Insta360 的突围逻辑，是在 GoPro 占据主导的运动相机市场，精准抓住了两个核心点：痛点解决与体验升级。

GoPro 的核心痛点之一是自拍杆外露影响拍摄效果，Insta360 推出全景运动相机，彻底解决了这一问题；同时，Insta360 洞察到极限运动用户极强的分享欲，优化了分享流程 —— 拍摄完成后可直接在手机或设备上一键分享至社交媒体，无需繁琐的导出、剪辑步骤。

极限运动本身具备极强的视觉冲击力，快速分享能最大化内容传播效率，形成 “拍摄 - 分享 - 传播” 的闭环。Insta360 凭借这一组合创新，逐步蚕食 GoPro 的市场份额，再加上中国供应链的成本优势，迅速成长为行业标杆。

有色剃须刀的成功，则是精准切入体验阶段的情绪价值。2020 年，有色在国内推出首款剃须刀产品，初期销量平平，却在小红书上偶然发现用户将其作为礼物送给男朋友，反馈极佳。

有色的母公司是设计公司，产品本身具备极强的设计感，恰好解决了 “送男生礼物难” 的痛点 —— 皮带等礼物过于隐私，而剃须刀是男性日常必备品，且设计感强的产品更适合作为礼品。

有色迅速调整营销策略，聚焦礼品属性，定价在 300-400 元区间。其核心购买群体并非男性用户，而是为男朋友挑选礼物的女性，产品评论中满是 “女朋友送的，非常喜欢” 等正向反馈。

2020 年，抖音正从娱乐化向商业化转型，开始扶持高客单价产品，有色借助这一流量红利，通过短视频营销快速起量，2021-2022 年一年营收达到 6 亿元，如今已邀请王一博代言，成为国内剃须刀领域的头部品牌。

需要注意的是，有色若布局出海，需重新定义产品 —— 中西方男性胡须特征不同，且海外送礼文化与国内存在差异，日本或欧洲等审美偏优雅的市场可能更适合其产品定位。

Monica

非常精彩的案例拆解！想确认一下，有色剃须刀抛开产品定位与外观设计，在功能体验上与普通剃须刀是否没有太大差异？

Joe

从功能层面来看，有色与普通剃须刀的核心体验差异不大，我个人更偏爱手动剃须刀。但其成功的关键在于抓住了礼品这一细分场景，通过设计感与情绪价值形成差异化。如果初期就能明确这一定位，或许能减少部分广告投入成本，但无疑是一次极为成功的场景切入案例。

AI硬件产品的观察与思考分享

Monica

2025 年是 AI 硬件爆发的一年，出现了很多情绪陪伴类产品等创新形态。想请教 Joe，你如何看待这一波硬件发展浪潮？有没有让你觉得特别有意思的产品？

Joe

我想分享几个极具代表性的产品。

首先是 Plaud，它是一款适配 iPhone 的超薄录音贴片，核心亮点是结合 AI 大模型，能快速将录音转化为文案，极大提升了信息处理效率。

AI 在硬件中的应用，核心应是解决痛点或优化体验。早期陪伴类产品已存在，AI 技术的加入让智能交互更流畅，推动消费电子进入新一轮更新周期 —— 就像 VR 早在 2015-2016 年就已出现，直到 2022-2023 年苹果入局才掀起新热潮；AI 在 2016-2018 年已受资本追捧，如今借助大模型技术实现了应用层的突破。

但当前很多 AI 硬件存在 “为了 AI 而 AI” 的问题，核心是没有想清楚 AI 要解决什么问题。比如部分 AI 陪伴机器人，情绪交互体验仍不够成熟，可能还需要 2-3 年的技术迭代才能达到理想效果。

另一款值得关注的产品是有一款 AI 望远镜，它解决了户外观察的核心痛点 —— 看到珍稀鸟类等目标时，拍照过程中目标可能跑掉。该产品能自动记录观察目标，并识别物种信息，快速反馈给用户，节省了用户搜索与拍照的时间成本。

另外最近让我印象非常深刻的是乐高的智能模块 —— 它没有盲目加入 AI，而是回归用户本身。乐高的核心用户是孩子，购买者是家长，家长既希望孩子享受拼装乐趣，又担忧屏幕使用与数据安全。

乐高的智能模块没有配备摄像头，不收集任何用户数据，仅通过 NFC 芯片实现交互 —— 用户拼好船、机器人等模型后，搭配该模块就能发出对应的音效，既保留了拼装的核心乐趣，又通过智能化提升了产品趣味性。后续用户只需更换不同的 NFC 芯片，就能为不同模型匹配音效，成本极低。

这款产品证明，并非所有硬件都需要绑定 AI，回归用户需求、痛点与体验，才能打造出真正有价值的产品。乐高精准平衡了孩子的体验与家长的担忧，是 AI 浪潮下值得所有从业者反思的案例。

Monica

非常认同！AI 只是赋能工具，产品的 “需求 - 痛点 - 体验” 三阶段逻辑才是永恒的底层方法论。

一直有个疑问，硬件产品的外观设计感觉有些 “玄学”，比如陪伴型产品，设计成什么动物、颜色，是否有四肢，这些决策似乎难以量化。想请教 Joe，实际做这类产品设计时，有哪些切入点？

Joe

设计的核心其实很简单，回归用户本身 —— 先明确你要服务哪一类人，再针对性满足其需求与审美。比如潮玩产品，核心用户是二次元、cosplay 爱好者，设计需贴合其审美偏好；拓竹的核心用户是极客，产品设计需突出科技感；氛围灯的用户更关注情绪体验，外观与灯光效果需传递宁静、舒适等感觉。

用户买单的本质，要么是为功能价值，要么是为情绪价值。情绪价值的体现可能是 IP 认同、社交炫耀，或是陪伴感。比如 Labubu 潮玩，有些用户将其视为好运的象征或陪伴者，这种情绪连接正是设计的核心。

分享一个有趣的产品 —— 锁手机自律神器，用户可设置 10 分钟或 1 小时的锁定时间，不到时间无法打开。其创始人最初是为了考研时克制玩手机的冲动而设计，初期销量不佳，却因抖音博主用它调侃男朋友的视频走红。2021 年，全国考研人数达 750 万，这款 “自律神器” 精准抓住了备考人群的需求，客单价 129 元，后续虽有模仿者将价格压至 69-79 元，但该产品当年营收达到 1500 万元，成为现象级产品。

我们创业的第一款产品是 “吃鸡神器”，一款手机游戏物理辅助配件。当时市面上已有十几元到 39 元的同类产品，但体验不佳。我们发现核心用户是重度游戏玩家，对价格不敏感，更关注操作沉浸感。

这款产品仅投入 10 万元左右，却凭借极致的体验设计，获得了黑鲨游戏手机的合作订单。2020 年疫情期间，居家游戏需求激增，产品上线后成为京东该品类销量第一，后续又进入红米、一加等手机品牌的发布会，客单价提升至 79 元，较同类产品翻了近两倍。

后续我们还推出了游戏手机散热背夹，持续深耕游戏用户需求。一加的产品强调品质感，我们仅产品弧度就调整了近一个月，使其更耐看、更高端；黑鲨的产品则突出电竞感，通过设计与音效强化游戏沉浸体验。

核心逻辑就是：先服务好一类人，再拓展到一群人。当品牌在特定人群中形成认知后，再考虑拓展第二增长曲线。如果一开始就想卖给所有人，最终可能谁都服务不好。

产品定义是多维度的，不仅涉及产品本身，还与技术、渠道、推广、定价等紧密相关。比如渠道选择上，是入驻 Bset Buy、沃尔玛，还是亚马逊、TikTok 等渠道，都需基于用户定位来决定。但回归本质，核心始终是 “用户”—— 明确用户为什么需要你，处于哪个价值阶段，再针对性进行产品设计与运营。

Monica

非常精彩！核心就是围绕某一类人群，专注解决其特定的体验或痛点。

Joe

没错。比如面向商务人群的产品，设计需高端简洁专业；面向年轻用户的产品，可更可爱灵动。关键是找准目标人群的核心诉求与审美偏好。

Monica

了解。Joe 平时 base 在哪个城市？

Joe

我常驻深圳，工作重心基本都在这边。

Monica
