"Narrative Go-Global" in the AI Era

Monica

First, let's ask Yucheng to define "Narrative Go-Global," the context in which it emerged, and its importance.

Yucheng Liu

The idea of "Narrative Go-Global" has been around for a long time. Chinese enterprises going global have gone through several stages.

First was the 1.0 stage, traditional goods going global. China, as the world's factory, exported goods, but Chinese enterprises captured a thin slice of the profit, with the vast majority taken by overseas R&D or brand sales, illustrating the smile curve.

Later came the 2.0 era, where brands like Xiaomi and DJI established themselves overseas. In the US, people use DJI drones to inspect roofs and proudly mention DJI. Chinese companies have done well in brand building, but in my view, this is still a 2.0 state, better than 1.0, allowing them to benefit from brand premiums.

I believe the 3.0 stage is more abstract, more soft-powered, centered on influence, affecting the thoughts and minds of global users.

This influence is less tied to specific products or brands; it's more about influencing a person's narrative structure. It's softer, harder to grasp because it's highly non-standardized, but this is also what makes it interesting, and it's a trend we can clearly see.

Monica

So, "Narrative Go-Global," building on 1.0 and 2.0, is no longer just translating product manuals or slogans, but re-evaluating global values.

Yucheng Liu

Yes, it's about taking that kind of influence out into the world.

I'll give an interesting example of an observation. In 2024 and before, people in communities often asked whether they needed to have both a Chinese Twitter and an English Twitter account. The Chinese Twitter was like a secondary account, for interacting with friends, while the English Twitter was more formal, for communicating with overseas communities.

But this year, the situation has changed. Whether it's Twitter's platform algorithm or the emergence of AI, cultural and language barriers are easier to break down than before.

A phenomenon we observed two months ago: a Chinese Twitter user in our community developed a tool for a certain overseas open-source platform. This tool was open-sourced on GitHub and originally had a few thousand stars, but through the efforts of the Chinese Twitter community, it quickly jumped from a few thousand to tens of thousands of stars.

This was a group of people from the Chinese community who boosted an open-source project from a European country, which is a very interesting global collaboration. The Chinese community didn't create the product, but they played a significant role in amplifying its influence.

I think we'll see more and more of these things in the future.

Monica

Does Twitter's recognition logic for Chinese content differ from English content?

Yucheng Liu

The underlying logic has no fundamental difference. As a platform, Twitter's algorithm doesn't specifically suppress or favor the Chinese community. The characteristics shown by the Chinese community are more related to the behavior of user clusters.

The core logic is not different, but the recent change is mainly due to the decrease in AI costs. Twitter now has sufficient resources to translate all non-native language content into the native language. If you open the app now and your setting is English, all Japanese, Chinese, Arabic, and Spanish content will be translated into English by default. Users scrolling through tweets are oblivious to the original language.

To give another example: a while ago, I was looking at investment tweets and saw Chinese and overseas investors communicating in both Chinese and English under the same tweet. Both sides saw AI-translated content—my timeline was full of Chinese communication, and their timeline was full of English communication. This is very interesting, an observation of how humans break through language barriers. I believe this will be a long-term trend in the future.

Practical Breakdown of Twitter's Recommendation Algorithm

Monica

It certainly breaks down barriers. Starting with written communication on social media, perhaps in the future, seamless face-to-face interaction will be possible through smart wearables. Next, let's invite AppSail to talk about Twitter's algorithm, which everyone is most interested in.

AppSail.dev

Okay, I'll introduce it. Twitter's algorithm has a whole set of processes. It looks at your data, sees who you're similar to, and builds a network. By analyzing all data, including your bio, follows, interactions, likes, etc., it maps this user data and tweets into numerous interest communities. The content users see later comes from these interest communities.

First, there's a data analysis process, and then distribution is handled based on that. Twitter's algorithm recommends not only based on follow relationships but also on interest communities. Every user and creator is assigned to one or more interest communities by the algorithm, which determines who the creator's content will be pushed to. In other words, whatever interest community you belong to, the algorithm recommends you to people in that community.

The content recommended to users mainly consists of two parts:

First, content based on connections with friends you follow.

Second, content based on interest communities. For example, if I'm an AI go-global developer, my tweets will most likely be pushed to two types of people: my followers, and other users belonging to the AI developer interest community.

Monica

Is this interest community a category that Twitter automatically defines in the background?

AppSail.dev

Yes, this is the most core part of the recommendation algorithm. It maps each user's creative content, interaction relationships, etc., to a cluster. This algorithm is called Similar Cluster, and it maps each user and each tweet to an interest community.

Monica

Is this Interest Cluster the same as the user tags we commonly talk about on Douyin or Xiaohongshu?

AppSail.dev

It's somewhat similar, but not entirely the same. Twitter's algorithm has more dimensions, including bio, follows, interactions, likes, retweets, topics, and hashtags.

Twitter has built a lot around interest communities. When the algorithm was open-sourced in 2023, there were tens of thousands of interest communities. According to Grok's feedback, there should be 400,000 to 500,000 now. Interest communities can be very large, such as entertainment, music, POP, NBA; or very small, such as AI go-global developers, which is relatively smaller than entertainment news.

Monica

So the core of the algorithm is not just follow relationships, but also revolves around interest community relationships?

AppSail.dev

There are two parts: first, content based on follow relationships, and then content outside of follow relationships.

Previously, the algorithm split these two parts 50/50. This was the algorithm in 2023, so the content we saw might be half from creators we followed and half from content we didn't follow but belonged to our interest community. I don't know if the algorithm has been adjusted since then.

Monica

The content within the same interest community is vast, even for AI-related topics. How does the recommendation logic work? What content is prioritized?

AppSail.dev

This involves the subsequent scoring algorithm. The previous step essentially gathered a content pool.

Within this content pool, Twitter scores each tweet every time it's distributed, calculating the score based on data like timeliness, interaction, etc., and then sorts them from high to low. For example, it might select 35 tweets to recommend to a user.

After these 35 tweets are selected, there might be some mixing, such as evenly distributing content from different fields, inserting some ads, etc., and finally, the top 35 are recommended to each user.

Monica

Of course, in addition to purely informative content creation, go-global developers also have some lifestyle or other common issues that can be discussed.

Yucheng Liu

I'll add a few points.

First, regarding the interest community tags I just mentioned, if you're used to domestic platforms, you might feel there are similarities. But my personal feeling is that the cold start phase is different.

During a cold start, for example, below 2000 followers, whatever content you post might not be seen by anyone. This is the cold start problem.

So, generally, for accounts with under 1000 or 2000 followers, it's recommended to focus more on interaction.

The whole logic is this: Twitter gives the highest weight to interactions originating from your content, especially replies and quote tweets, because you're adding value to the original poster. For new users or those in a cold start state, the only thing you can do is add value to others.

So, during the cold start period, you must reply to others a lot. This process first builds your small cluster connections, and second, it's about giving first, bringing value to others, and in this process, meeting more people and building up your account.

Initially, it's generally not recommended to post many long, in-depth articles during a cold start. Nobody will read them, and it will be a significant blow to your morale and motivation. The initial state should be interacting with everyone, replying a lot, getting known, and keeping a relaxed mindset. Do this until you reach at least 2000 followers.

Monica

So, before 2000 followers, it's like being a good fan, interacting a lot with big influencers?

AppSail.dev

What Yucheng said is particularly important.

After you've found your positioning, you might want to find some KOLs or big accounts in your field and post high-quality comments under their posts. Don't just be overly worshipful.

Posting high-quality comments makes it easy for many people to see them, even the KOLs themselves. If they interact with you, it will bring you followers and exposure. A new account might only get dozens or hundreds of views, but a comment under a big account's post can get 1/10 of the main post's views. For example, if a big account's post goes viral with 200,000 views, the best comment below it might get 20,000 views.

During the beginner stage, generally go to the KOLs in your interest area and post high-quality comments and insights.

You are essentially establishing a connection with that interest area, and you have a relationship with high-quality accounts and content in that field. Later, when you post relevant content, it's very likely to push your content to other users in that interest community.

对，我刚才想补充，受启发一点：AI时代做内容跟做产品中间边界越来越模糊，不知道大家感受到没。刚才讲针对内容迭代思路，跟你做产品迭代思路一样。我举几个例子：不要憋大招。

我们跟创业公司或独立开发讲过无数遍，因为这是已经觉得难理解，但没过坎的人经常踩坑。他想憋大招花半年时间仔细打磨东西，指望一朝成名天下知，一下发出来满世界都知道。但真实情况往往是你吭哧吭哧每天写代码写半年，发出来100个View没人评论，整个人傻眼。这情况我们见太多。

所以不要憋大招，内容也一样。当然内容周期比产品周期快些。比如产品打磨一两个星期，打磨长文发出来30个View没人评论，也可能出现，让第一次做这事人很绝望。

在当下时代，做产品跟做内容一样：一不要憋大招，二快速试错。不要害怕写出来东西或做出来东西被人骂，或没人喜欢。一定要快速迭代，快速试错。无论你是做产品人，还是做内容人，或提供价值人，换种思路，不论做产品还是写内容，都是为大家提供价值。对于提供价值人，一定要快速试错，因为你不知道你的价值有没有人需要。很多时候你自己拍脑门觉得这价值有人需要，但其实没有。能验证这点就是快速试错，快速验证。如果验证不成功，换方向快速迭代。我觉得这对无论做什么事情逻辑或底层逻辑、方法论共通。

不存在。故事越写越有，越写越多。你在写过程中又有新故事出来，不用担心。
