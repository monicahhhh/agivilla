Founder of Eolink

Eight years of deep experience in API and AI infrastructure

Actually, an agent found us. Our product is called XPack. At the time, we were relatively early to market. The concept of MCP became quite popular this year, so we created the first MCP trading platform, and it's open source.

It just so happened that a client in Japan knew one of our partners. They approached the partner, saying they were looking for an MCP-related product because they wanted to develop an experimental product internally to see if they could sell their data this way. Since I knew that partner, they connected us. We made a special trip to Japan to have dinner with the Japanese client and discussed things offline.

They found the concept very interesting and we reached an agreement. So, to a certain extent, luck played a significant role.

If that Japanese client hadn't had that need, and hadn't known my partner, and my partner hadn't known me, then this business opportunity might not have materialized. However, we also had some prior preparation because we anticipated significant opportunities in this area in the future. So, we decided to launch this product as open source and as a first-to-market offering, and we continue to maintain it.

Monica

Did you know this partner a long time ago, or was it a targeted approach?

Jack

We had just met, but we really hit it off. I think whether you're doing business with people in Japan or elsewhere, integrity and initial trust are quite important.

The partner was initially a speaker at one of our study tours. I listened to his presentation and felt a strong resonance with his experiences, so I started chatting with him. Coincidentally, I had my baby with me, so we arranged to have dinner and spent some time together, gradually becoming familiar.

Establishing a company indicates a long-term commitment. After establishing a company, have you hired Japanese employees? If you hire Japanese employees, it shows you are serious about developing your business there. They believe that if you haven't registered a Japanese company and don't have Japanese employees, you're not a company looking for long-term development, and they will be more cautious about collaborating.

Underlying Rules of the Japanese ToB Market: Integrator-Dominated Sales System

Monica

Is your partner Japanese or a Chinese person who has been living in Japan for a long time?

Jack

A Chinese person who has been in Japan for a long time. Such partners are very important for development, very important indeed.

Because in Japan, channels are indispensable. It's almost impossible to sell directly. After looking at many examples, the feedback I received was that it's very difficult to sell directly. The reason is that their large clients are basically controlled by channels or integrators.

If you're a new company, you might not even get to meet clients, or it might be very difficult to find the person responsible for procurement on the client's side. Moreover, Japan's IT infrastructure was established much earlier than China's. Many of their systems were built many years ago.

But building early doesn't mean building well, because they were built with very old technology. That's why we see the Japanese government saying they will phase out floppy disks within a certain number of years – something I rarely saw in China even when I was in elementary school.

Although we started later, we used newer technology and worked very hard, so in many cases, our system development is better and faster. They built their systems decades ago and have been relying on integrators to maintain them.

Simply put, it's difficult for them to procure products outside of their existing integrators. They basically keep adding things on top of old systems. If you want to add something and integrate it, you have to find an integrator. So, to sell to large enterprises, you essentially need to find an integrator.

When we first went to Japan, we also hoped to first find some larger integrators to collaborate with. However, as a Chinese company, it's very difficult to get into major Japanese integrators.

At the time, we looked at which Chinese company products had already entered the market. There were very, very few, almost none, perhaps one or two occasionally.

Because we ourselves develop technology products, Japanese companies inherently distrust Chinese technology products. This is understandable, just as China now emphasizes self-reliance for foreign products. In Japan, this sentiment is also very strong. So, their trust in American products is based on a relatively stable political relationship, meaning there won't be too many risks in procurement.

Secondly, it's about product capability, because many old systems were built by American companies or directly procured from American systems, so there's continuity. These two factors combined make it difficult for Chinese companies to enter. Other factors, such as culture or product experience, have relatively less impact. The first two points are more important.

Monica

What role do integrators play in Japan?

Jack

A data point my partner shared with me is that among Japan's IT revenue, SaaS products sold directly by companies might account for less than 2%. Over 90% of the rest is sold by integrators. This means that sales channels are completely monopolized by integrators.

There are many integrators, but the few large ones are enormous and don't just focus on one area; they cover almost all sectors.

Simply put, integrators have very strong sales capabilities, with resident staff at each client site and thousands of sales teams spread across Japan and even globally, possessing decades of cooperation experience with large clients.

Secondly, integrators have many systems tied to their old clients, effectively locking them in. Thirdly, regarding client needs, Japanese companies rarely have in-house IT teams, which is quite different from China. Many Chinese companies have R&D teams, whereas Japanese IT teams primarily handle equipment maintenance, requirement gathering, or project management, rarely engaging in R&D.

R&D is basically outsourced to integrators, who then subcontract to remote R&D teams, which used to be in China and are now perhaps in Vietnam, Cambodia, or other places, to assist with development. Their main tasks are project management, requirements collection, and design.

Products and Strategies: How to Make Chinese Products Accepted in the Japanese Market

Monica

In the Japanese ToB market, is it fair to say that instead of selling to ToB clients, it's more about winning over integrators?

Jack

Exactly. I've talked to integrators, and they say if you can win us over, you don't have to worry about selling.

But the problem is, winning them over is very difficult, perhaps even harder than winning over clients. Because winning over clients only requires meeting one client's demands, but integrators need to consider meeting the needs of many clients if they include your product in their portfolio. So, your product needs to be very mature to even be considered by an integrator. For a startup, this is quite challenging. Regarding product maturity, I feel startups shouldn't even consider this path:

First, integrators are unlikely to consider products from startups. They are like Fortune 500 companies that only like to work with other Fortune 500 companies; they want to sell products to clients that are fully validated and very stable.

Secondly, from the perspective of Japanese industry, the best-selling products are not the sophisticated AI or virtualization products we see in China. Instead, they are financial management systems, human resources management systems, HR outsourcing systems, and even a business card management product that ranks fourth in SaaS revenue, earning tens of billions of yen annually. It simply helps manage physical business cards.

This kind of thing doesn't exist in China because in daily interactions, they receive many physical business cards. They collect them and then use phones or other tools to input the information. Later, when they need to find people or business opportunities, they search through this database. This is a need that Chinese companies would hardly think of, making it difficult for them to do better than the Japanese.

Secondly, China's most advantageous software products are difficult to sell in Japan because they don't have this demand.

Thirdly, even if there is demand, they are reluctant to purchase Chinese products, especially now that relations are strained. Even when relations were relatively better, procurement was rare. They mostly procure from Europe and America.

For example, I heard a statistic that a very large Japanese enterprise subscribes to Salesforce products for $50 million a year. This is extremely rare in China; you rarely hear of subscriptions selling for $50 million. Trust is something that's hard to break on your own, and it's hard to say that your product is so outstanding that it can break through. There's a very long time window involved; it might take one to two years to build good relationships with integrators, gain trust, and even secure the first order. Many startups might not be able to wait that long.

So, if you have a particularly strong product, don't go to Japan; go directly to the US or create a global product. Going to Japan is only advisable if the product is suitable or if there's a specific opportunity.

Many suggest that if you want to enter the Japanese market, it's best to extract the strongest feature from your product and create a very small, standalone product. This is actually a better market entry strategy because when Japanese clients choose products, they won't immediately adopt an all-in-one large system to solve all their problems. Their systems were built many years ago.

If you introduce a super powerful system, it's equivalent to replacing many of their existing components. System replacement is unacceptable in many Japanese companies. They don't replace things; they layer on top.

This is different from software in China and the US. Chinese clients often say their old system was bad and ask you to help migrate data, moving everything to the new system. But Japan won't do this, or at least it's very rare.

If you layer on top, it means the added functionality must adapt to their original system. So, it's best to extract the most powerful feature and make it a small plugin. This makes it easiest for them to accept. A higher price is okay, but if a small tool can solve a problem, they are willing to pay for it, as long as it doesn't conflict with their existing workflows.

Trust, Cost, and Long-Termism: "Can You Last" in Japan

Audience

Will the current China-Japan relationship affect future market sales?

Jack

Absolutely. There was already a lack of trust, and now it's even worse.

Monica

Are the legal requirements for company setup significantly different from China?

Jack

We have already registered a company in Japan. The difference is that it's extremely slow, and there are many procedures. The fastest it takes is 3 months.

In China, when you set up a company, they often help you open a bank account at the same time. The system is developed, and administrative efficiency is high, backed by strong IT infrastructure. Japan also has these things, but there are a lot of forms to fill out, many of which are paper-based, requiring many procedures that cannot be avoided.

It's relatively difficult for foreigners to handle it themselves. If you use an intermediary, you need to prepare many contracts. For example, to register a company, you need to prepare a business plan, which is not required in China. You need to explain what the company does, how you plan to conduct business, who your potential clients are, and whether you have any current deals. The requirements for company compliance and sustainability are relatively high, unlike in China where you can register and deregister quite easily.

Monica

What's the situation like in Japan regarding finance-related aspects, such as accounting and taxation?

Jack

It's actually quite similar to China. In China, when a company is small, you can find an agency to handle bookkeeping and tax filing. It's similar there; many small companies and even family workshops don't handle their own finances. They need to prepare a lot of paper documents every month.

The government also sends a lot of paper documents, and sometimes there are phone calls or visits to inquire. So, people generally hire agents. The prices are similar to China. In China, a cheaper one might be 2,000-3,000 RMB per year, while there it's about 4,000-5,000 RMB per year.

Monica

Do you currently have an office and local employees in Japan?

Jack

Yes, because we need to ensure the company is seen as operating legitimately.

In recent years, many Web3 companies have gone to Japan because Japan was the first country to legalize Bitcoin trading. So, starting this year, opening a bank account in Japan has become very difficult. Companies are often required to have a Japanese person specifically manage the bank account.

This person must have a Japanese address and phone number. We, as foreign investors, are not eligible. Some people apply for a highly skilled professional visa, which has even higher requirements. Previously, a registered capital of 5 million JPY (equivalent to 250,000 RMB) was sufficient. Now, it requires 30 million JPY (1.5 million RMB in cash) paid-in capital, and you must stay in Japan for at least 180 days per year.

Monica

How do Japanese employee salaries and requirements, including labor laws, compare to China?

Jack

Overall, the cost is much higher. The higher cost is due to higher compliance requirements. For example, if we want to develop business in Japan, and we won't be living there permanently, we would first find someone like a partner or regional head.

This person's annual salary before tax would be around 700,000-800,000 RMB. At the company level, you'd multiply that by 1.5, so annual expenditure on this one person would be around 1.1-1.2 million RMB. If we hire others, the minimum setup usually includes a regional head (often also handling sales), and possibly a pre-sales or customer support person. These roles also cost 300,000-400,000 RMB annually.

Hiring an employee in Tokyo, a minimum annual salary of 3-4 million JPY (equivalent to 200,000 RMB) is considered poor; life is very difficult. Moreover, Japanese people don't like Chinese companies, making it difficult to recruit them. They feel Chinese companies don't comply with labor laws, exploit employees, have problematic corporate cultures, and there are political factors. So, generally, higher salaries are offered to attract them to switch jobs, sometimes double, sometimes a 30%-40% increase. Overall, salaries are higher.

Therefore, if you plan to enter the Japanese market, it's highly recommended to start with a small product, secure some clients and revenue first, and then consider opening a company and hiring people there. For SaaS or small tool products, it's not always necessary to register a Japanese company; even an American company might suffice.

Monica

How did your C-side product achieve such high revenue in its first month?

Jack

Because we did a joint promotion with a cloud vendor. When entering a local market, you definitely need to leverage big players.

We are not a company that has always focused on global products. We started researching last year and only truly put effort into product development this year. So, our strategy is to leverage big players, find large companies or well-funded companies, see what they want to do, and identify what we can complement for them.

For example, AI is very popular now, and large model vendors want to support good products in various regions to sell their tokens. We essentially act as a wrapper between them and users, making the product more suitable for end-users.

Moreover, the product usage experience of Japanese users is very different from that in China and Europe/America. Chinese products tend to lean towards European/American styles, not so much Japanese.

Many Japanese products resemble Chinese websites from 20 years ago, with a lot of dense text, high information density, and numerous anime elements. Some are very exaggerated, some look very outdated, but they have been accustomed to this for a long time. This is because their company systems have been like this for decades; IT development has always been slow, and their aesthetics have remained the same. So, there's a polarization: some designs are particularly trendy and beautiful, while others are very rustic. You need to cater to their usage habits, and a lot of localization work is required.

The most time we spent wasn't on developing features, but on making the product conform to Japanese usage habits, making it visually appealing and enjoyable for them to use. We did a lot of work with our partners in this regard.

How do you make locals think a product looks good?

Mainly by observing the layout of Japanese competitors or commonly used similar websites and simply replicating it, without too much innovation.

Secondly, incorporate some cartoon elements, anime elements, or IP characters into the product interface. They really respond to this. Even for ToB software, sometimes cute anime girl characters pop up. On trains and street advertisements, there are also many such cute characters. Their resistance to children and cute things is weaker than that of Chinese people, perhaps due to long-term aging and seeing them less often. In manga stores, it's normal to see people in their sixties and seventies reading manga, but in China, cartoon characters are rarely seen in PPTs for the government or large enterprises.

I've looked into it, and after comparing, I felt the Singapore market was a bit small, so we ultimately chose Japan.

However, this depends on the product. From a foreigner's perspective, a Singaporean company is now equivalent to a Chinese company. If you're doing business in Japan, you'll still be seen as a Chinese company. If you're doing business in Singapore, for legitimate operations, the market isn't that big. For example, Web3 is okay in Singapore, but not considered legitimate business in China, so the definition is different. If you're doing ToB software, the customer unit price in Singapore might be higher, and consumption habits are better, for instance, POCs are paid. But the number of customers is too small, and people are expensive.

Audience

Are you familiar with the Korean market?

Jack

I have a basic understanding. The ToB market depends on whether there's local customer demand for the product and the relevant conditions for entering the ToB space.

ToB sales are essentially about identifying needs, finding key people, influencing them, and facilitating deals. Every market is similar. Compared to Japan, the Korean market's pursuit of informatization is similar; neither is as aggressive as China because they don't rely on it for their economic growth. China hopes to surpass the US in IT, informatization, and AI, but Japan and Korea are not in a hurry. Japan knows its software isn't that good, but it doesn't care. For example, Sony camera apps are poorly made, updating only once a year, because they don't care; their mindset is different.

Audience

I'd like to know if the Chinese community in Japan has needs similar to those of Foody (Fantuan) delivery in North America?

Jack

My personal feeling is that there isn't a specific demand solely for the Japanese Chinese community. For example, when Europeans and Americans travel or reside in China, we rarely consider their needs separately because their needs tend to assimilate.

After Chinese people arrive in Japan, they often live according to Japanese customs, whether it's for work, life, education, or healthcare. Everything generally becomes Japanized. Moreover, this group is very small.

Japan has a population of 140 million, with about 4 million foreign residents, of whom about 1 million are Chinese. Creating a product for a user base of only 1 million is too small. Furthermore, Chinese people in Japan are often reluctant to reveal their Chinese nationality due to political sensitivities.

I even heard a story about a Chinese person who worked in Japan for ten years and only then found out that their colleague next door was also Chinese. For those ten years, they had always spoken Japanese, and only when the colleague left did they reveal their Chinese identity. So, it's very difficult to carve out a separate demand for this group.

Monica

If a Japanese company has loan and financing needs, can they get loans from local banks?

Jack

They can, but the requirements are very high. If you're Japanese, it's easier, as people want to support their own. I also wanted to find new financing opportunities in Japan, but I learned that raising 1 million RMB in Japan is already considered a relatively large amount of financing. In China, you wouldn't even dare to call that financing. So, it's not a particularly good financing channel there; the US might be better. For loans, if you're in manufacturing or offline business, you'd generally find a Japanese partner, and it would be easier to get a loan from their perspective.

I think the local innovation and incubation environment in Japan is good. The Japanese government promotes many activities, making it difficult for companies to fail, but also relatively difficult to grow large. The Japanese market and the American market are somewhat connected; American companies entering the Japanese market are like entering their own backyard, but Chinese companies entering are like going from their own home to someone else's. The competitive difficulty is different.

I've asked locals, and very few IT and internet startups in Japan manage to grow large. People are more inclined to engage in traditional industries. This is understandable. Entrepreneurship is essentially a competition of national strength. China excels in manufacturing, trade, and e-commerce, possessing a very strong infrastructure and manufacturing gene. Japan, however, lacks such genes. Their genes lie in high-end manufacturing, high-end design, service industries, and cultural industries, none of which are related to IT. So, they don't focus on IT.

However, the Japanese government is indeed supporting IT modernization (which they call DX). Opportunities in this sector are highly likely to remain with Japanese local companies or American companies. Their concept of IT modernization is different from ours. We focus on everything moving to the cloud, digitalization, and intelligence. They are converting paper-based documents into Excel and Word files on computers, which is essentially paperless operations. This is currently the largest part of their market.

Audience

Are venture capital and equity financing institutions active?

Jack

They are, relatively active, but the sources of funding are somewhat different. In China, a lot of private equity comes from wealthy individuals or large companies. There, much of it comes from government funds, bank funds, and more often, industrial funds. For example, companies like Toyota and Honda invest in specialized companies that serve their upstream and downstream. So, to get investment in Japan, it generally needs to be related to their industry. They won't simply invest because something is profitable, though that does happen. But the more mainstream approach is industrial investment.

Monica

What do you mean by "deep SEO" as you mentioned earlier?

Jack

Japanese users have a long decision-making process. Before choosing a product, they conduct extensive research, finding all available information online, and they do it very thoroughly. So, when doing product in Japan, SEO is definitely necessary, even more detailed than in China.

There are also some small tricks for AI now. Many Japanese product official websites or company websites are written in great detail, including company history, founders, office address, bank registration information, etc. Blogs and customer case studies are also very detailed. Each customer case study will include photos of the client's storefront, photos of partners, and in-depth interviews. I feel that for SEO in Japan, there is a very strong demand for deep content because their products have always been done this way.

Throughout the session, I feel there's an important point I haven't covered: their workplace culture or characteristics.

Many older Japanese companies have lifetime employment and seniority-based systems. Once an employee joins, as long as the company doesn't go bankrupt, they can work there for life. So, when they choose a product, it's like they're staking their own credibility. They will meticulously search for information, and once a product is introduced, it won't be easily replaced. Because they've invested so much time and personal credibility, if it's replaced after a few years, it implies the decision made at the time might have been wrong, and that person could lose promotion opportunities.

This is less of a concern in China because employees change jobs frequently, leaving after two or three years, so the impact of past decisions is minimal. In Japan, talent mobility is slow, and companies are older, placing particularly high demands on them. Therefore, for SEO, whether it's company branding, basic introduction, philosophy, product information, or customer case studies, everything needs to be explained very clearly. Many SEOs in China will claim to be the best in the industry, but that doesn't work in Japan. You need to clearly explain why you're the best, with plenty of supporting evidence, which greatly influences the final deal.

Monica

Besides SEO, what other ways can strengthen trust endorsement and accumulation?

Jack

Meeting in person. You have to spend time with the client, similar to China, but the cycle is longer. They might have a meeting or meet with you once a week, but the meeting content can be covered quickly; the pace isn't that fast.

For example, when discussing a solution with a client, they might say, "Let's meet next week." The first meeting is a kickoff, bringing everyone together. The second meeting starts with gathering requirements. The third meeting discusses the requirements. It's very slow. So, for ToB products in Japan, you need to spend time with the client, you must hire local people, or move to Japan yourself. The latter is not feasible for many founders, so hiring is a better option.

Audience

Are you considered a technical consulting firm?

Jack

No, we primarily provide products, but our current contracts with Japanese companies do include consulting services.

Monica

Are there any leading companies in this field in Japan right now?

Jack

Mainly various integrators. The first thing integrators do is consulting. They create very polished PPT proposals for clients. Clients will only start working after the proposal has been thoroughly discussed and approved. Then they continue with the next steps.

Japan has many small consulting firms, with very specialized categories. The leading consulting firms are those top integrators, such as Dentsu, NTT, NEC, Fujitsu, etc. The top five integrators probably account for 80-90% of the market. They charge for the PPT phase; they charge for producing proposals because they put in labor. POCs also have a fee, and sometimes the POC fee might even exceed the product's own cost.

Relatively speaking, it's a healthier business environment. Even if they don't win the bid in the end, they still earn money from the POC. They will ask many companies in the market to submit proposals because once a product is introduced, the cost of replacement is very high.

How many local employees have you hired in Japan so far?

Jack

Currently, we've hired one local head. We're just starting, and it's mainly a collaboration between him and me. There are some things in Japan that we can't easily do ourselves. For example, visiting clients—I don't speak Japanese, so I can't communicate well with them. Pre-sales and after-sales also require Japanese communication, and it's definitely smoother for Japanese people to communicate among themselves.

Monica

How did you find such a person?

Jack

Through a friend's introduction. Open recruitment is also an option, but it's more difficult because Japanese people are generally reluctant to join Chinese companies. Compared to American, Japanese, and Chinese companies, they would definitely prioritize the first two.

Monica

What primarily attracts suitable people?

Jack

Similar to China, they look at how long this venture can last, and whether you are truly committed to entering this market.

If the product is exceptionally good and popular, even a Chinese company can attract many people. But we are not in that category yet, so we need to prove that the product is good. Finding people from your user base is also a good option because they are already using your product.

Audience

What is the entire decision-making process for enterprises purchasing products?

Jack

It's similar to PPT, POC, and then moving forward. This is all learned from Europe and America. This set of processes in China and Japan basically originates from Europe and America.

Costs are discussed from the beginning, such as how long the consultation will last, how many meetings will be held, how much each meeting costs, and how it's calculated. For example, if a meeting with one person costs 2,000 yuan for consultation, they will quote based on the number of meetings. These amounts are small change to them.

Monica

What kind of industries and products, or what kind of AI entrepreneurs, do you think are suitable for entering the Japanese market? Or how can one determine if they are suitable for the Japanese market?

Jack

To determine suitability, the Japanese market isn't vastly different from the European and American markets. Just as the hottest products in the US now also have a large market in China, it's just that some demands need to be adapted to local conditions.

If you have a deep understanding of local needs and tailor your efforts to them, that's fine. For example, for popular restaurant reservation services overseas, Japan's reservation services aren't that good. There are many tourists, restaurants won't answer so many phone calls, and waiters don't speak English or Chinese, only Japanese. Many people want a product that solves the problem of making reservations, and some have done it, and quite well. From a broad demand perspective, there isn't much regional difference.

If you really want to start a business in the Japanese market, just look at the most profitable products and sectors in the industry. If you want to do something smaller, more refined, and less competitive, as I just mentioned, find a small local need. It's best to stay in Japan for two months, experience the local industry, and you'll discover many opportunities.

政府也会寄很多纸质材料，有时候还会有电话上门询问，所以一般都会请代理，价格跟国内差不多，国内便宜点的2000~3000块人民币一年，那边大概4000~5000块人民币一年。

Monica

你现在有在日本设立 office、招当地员工吗？

Jack

有的，因为要保证公司在他们看来是正儿八经运营的。

最近几年很多搞 Web3 的去日本，因为日本是第一个比特币合法化可交易的国家，所以从今年开始在日本开设银行账户变得非常困难，会要求公司最好有一个日本人专门管银行账户。

这个人要有日本的住址和电话，我们作为外部投资者不行，有些人会申请高级经营人才签证，那要求更高，以前注册资本 500 万日元（相当于 25 万元人民币）就可以，现在要 3000 万日元（150 万人民币现金）实缴，而且一年至少要在日本待 180 天以上。

Monica

日本员工的薪酬和要求，包括劳动法相关的情况跟中国相比怎么样？

Jack

总体成本贵很多，贵的前提是合规要求高，比如我们要发展日本业务，又不会一直住在那里，就会先找一个类似于合伙人或区域负责人。

这个人的年薪税前大概七八十万人民币，公司层面还要再乘以 1.5，一年花在这一个人身上大概 110-120 万左右，如果招其他人，一般最低配置是区域负责人（往往兼销售），可能还有售前或客户支持，这些岗位每年也要三四十万。

在东京招员工，最低年薪 300-400 万日元（相当于 20 万人民币）算是穷人，生活非常艰难，而且日本人不喜欢中国公司，很难招到，因为他们觉得中国公司不遵守劳动法、压榨员工、企业文化有问题，还有政治关系等因素，所以一般会给到比较高的薪酬吸引跳槽，有翻倍的，也有涨 30%-40%的，总体薪酬就高了。

所以比较建议如果要做日本市场，先挑一个小的产品，先拿到一些客户和收入，再考虑在那边开公司招人，如果是做 SaaS 类、小工具类的，其实不一定需要注册日本公司，甚至开一个美国公司也差不多。

Monica

你的 C 端产品是通过什么方式第一个月就达到这么高的营收？

Jack

因为我们跟云厂商做了联合推广，初入本地市场的时候肯定要抱大腿。

我们不是一直做出海产品的公司，去年开始研究，今年才正儿八经有精力做产品，所以策略就是抱大腿，找到大厂或有钱的公司，看他们想做什么，我们能帮他们补足什么。

比如现在 AI 很火，大模型厂商希望在各个区域扶持好的产品，把他们的 Token 卖出去，我们相当于在他们和用户之间做一层包装，让产品更适合终端用户。

而且日本用户的产品使用体验跟国内、欧美很不一样，中国的产品有点偏欧美，不太偏日本。

日本的产品很多像 20 年前的中国网站，字非常多、密密麻麻，信息量很大，还有大量动漫元素，有些很浮夸，有些看起来很土，但他们长期以来已经习惯了，这是因为他们几十年来公司的系统都是这样子的，IT 建设一直比较慢，审美也保持那样，所以会有两极分化，有特别潮流好看的设计，也有特别土的，要迎合他们的使用习惯，本地化需要做很多工作。

我们当时花最多时间的不是开发功能，而是让产品符合日本的使用习惯，让他们看起来顺眼、喜欢用，在这方面我们跟合作伙伴做了很多事情。

如何让当地人觉得产品是好看的？

主要是看日本的竞品或常用同类型网站的布局，照搬就行，不要做太多创新；

第二个是在产品界面里融合一些卡通元素、二次元动漫元素或 IP 形象，他们很吃这一套，就算是 ToB 软件，有时候也会有可爱的二次元女生形象跳出来，电车上、街边广告也有很多这种可爱的形象，他们对小孩和可爱的东西抵抗力比中国人弱一些，可能因为长期老龄化，见的少，漫画店里面有很多六七十岁的人在看漫画，这很正常，但在国内，给政府或大型企业的 PPT 里很少有卡通人物形象。

观众

有关注过新加坡市场吗？

Jack

有看过，对比之后觉得新加坡市场有点小，所以最后选择了日本。

不过这要看做什么产品，现在外国人看来新加坡公司等于中国公司，做日本市场的话还是会被认为是中国公司，做新加坡市场的话，如果是正经业务，市场没有那么大，比如 Web3 在新加坡是 OK 的，在国内不算正经业务，定性不同，如果是做 ToB 软件，新加坡的客单价可能比较高，消费习惯也比较好，比如 POC 会给钱，但客户数量太少，人也贵。

观众

韩国市场了解吗？

Jack

简单了解，ToB 市场要看产品在当地有没有客户需求，以及进入 ToB 的相关情况。

ToB 销售无非就是需求、找关键人、影响他们、促进成交，每个市场都类似，韩国市场跟日本市场相比，对信息化的追求都差不多，没有中国那么激进，因为他们不靠这个发家，中国希望在 IT、信息化、AI 这块超过美国，但日本、韩国不着急，日本知道自己的软件做得不是那么好，但根本不在乎，比如索尼相机的 APP 做的很差，一年更新一个版本，因为他们不在乎，心态不同。

观众

想了解一下日本的华人群体会不会有一些像北美的饭团外卖这样的需求？

Jack

我个人感觉不存在专门的日本华人需求，比如欧美人到中国旅游或居住，我们很少会单独考虑他们的需求，因为需求会被同化。

华人到日本之后，很多时候也是按日本的方式生活，不管是工作、生活、教育、医疗等，基本上都偏日化，而且这个群体很小。

日本 1.4 亿人口，外籍常住人口大概 400 万，其中中国人大概 100 万，做一个只有 100 万用户群体的需求太小了，而且在日本的中国人其实不太愿意暴露自己的中国国籍，因为政治上相对敏感。

我还听过一个故事，有个中国人在日本上班十年，才知道隔壁同事也是中国人，这十年他们一直讲日语，直到同事离职才说自己是中国人，所以很难单独切这块需求出来。

Monica

如果日本公司有贷款和融资需求，有办法从当地银行拿到贷款吗？

Jack

当地有，但要求很高，如果是日本人还好，大家希望扶持本民族的人，我之前也想在日本找新的融资机会，但了解到在日本融 100 万人民币已经算是比较大额的融资了，放在国内都不好意思说融资了，所以那边不是特别好的融资渠道，美国那边可能更好，贷款的话如果是做实业、线下生意，基本上会找日本的合伙人，从他们的角度去贷款更容易一点。

日本当地的创新孵化环境我觉得还是好的，日本政府会推很多活动，让公司比较难死掉，但也相对难做大，日本市场跟美国市场可以说是通的，美国公司进入日本市场就像进自己家后花园一样，但中国公司进去就相当于从自己家去别人家，竞争难度不一样。

我问过当地的人，在日本做 IT、互联网创业能够做大的公司很少很少，大家更倾向于做实业，这也可以理解，创业其实是在拼国力，中国做制造业、贸易出海、电商这块最牛逼，有非常强的基建和制造基因，但日本没有这样的基因，他们的基因是尖端制造、尖端设计、服务业和文化产业，这些都跟 IT 没什么关系，所以他们不会想着搞 IT。

不过日本政府确实在扶持信息化改造（他们叫 DX），这块产业的机会大概率还是留给日本本土公司或美国公司，他们的信息化改造跟我们的概念不一样，我们是所有东西上云、数字化智能化，他们是把以前纸质的东西变成电脑里的 Excel、Word，也就是无纸化，这是他们现在市场最大的一部分。

观众

风险投资和股权融资这种风投机构活跃吗？

Jack

其实有的，相对活跃，但资金来源不太一样，国内很多私募资金来自有钱人或大公司，那边很多是政府的钱、银行的钱，更多的是产业基金的钱，比如丰田、本田等公司会投资一些专门为他们上下游服务的公司，所以在日本拿投资，基本上要跟他们的产业相关，不会单纯因为看到一个东西赚钱就投，当然也有这种情况，但更主流的是产业投资。

Monica

你之前提到的深度 SEO 是一个什么概念？

Jack

日本用户的决策链路比较长，在选择产品之前会做大量调研，把网上能找到的资料全部找一遍，做得非常细，所以在日本做产品，SEO 肯定是要做的，甚至比国内做的更细。

现在还有针对 AI 的一些小手段，日本很多产品官网或公司官网会写得非常详细，比如公司历史、创始人、办公地址、银行注册信息等，博客和客户案例也写得非常细，每个客户案例都会有跟客户的门面照片、合作伙伴的照片，还有深度访谈，我感觉在日本做 SEO，对深度内容的需求非常强烈，因为他们以前的产品都是这样做的。

整场下来，我觉得有个重要的点没讲，就是他们的职场文化或职场特点。

很多老一点的日本企业是终身雇佣制、年功序列制，员工一旦入职，只要公司不倒闭就可以干一辈子，所以他们选择产品的时候，相当于用自己的信用做背书，会非常详细地找资料，而且引入产品之后不会随便替换，因为花了很多时间用个人信用背书，如果用了几年之后换掉，说明当时决策可能做错了，这个人可能就失去晋升机会。

这在中国就没有那么多顾虑，因为员工跳槽频繁，两三年就走了，当时的决策影响不大，在日本，人才流动慢，公司年龄长，对公司的要求就特别高，所以 SEO 不管是公司包装、基本介绍、理念、产品信息，还是客户案例，都要讲得很清楚，国内很多 SEO 会说自己是行业最牛逼的，但在日本不行，你要讲清楚为什么最牛逼，有很多佐证才行，这对最终成交影响很大。

Monica

除了 SEO 之外，还有什么方式能够加强信任背书和信任积累？

Jack

见面，要泡在客户那边，跟国内差不多，但周期更长，他们可能一周跟你开一次会、见一次面，但会议内容很快就能讲完，节奏不会那么快。

比如跟客户聊方案，他们说下周见面聊，第一次见面是开动员会，把大家叫在一起，第二次会议开始整理需求，第三次会议针对需求做讨论，非常慢，所以在日本做 ToB 产品，要泡在客户那边，必须在当地招人，或者自己搬到日本住，对很多创始人来讲后者不可行，所以招人会更好一点。

观众

你们算是技术咨询类吗？

Jack

不算，我们本身提供产品，但目前日本公司跟我们签的合同里面有咨询服务。

Monica

现在日本这类里面有比较头部的公司吗？

Jack

主要是各种集成商，集成商做的第一件事就是咨询，他们会给客户做非常漂亮的 PPT 方案，客户觉得方案经过层层研讨之后才会开始动手，然后继续往下做。

日本有很多小的咨询公司，门类分得很细，头部的咨询公司就是那些头部集成商，比如电通、NTT、NEC、富士通等，前五的头部集成商大概占了八九成的市场，他们在 PPT 阶段是付费的，出方案要收费，因为付出了劳动力，POC 也收费，有时候 POC 的费用可能高过产品本身的费用。

相对来讲是比较健康的商业环境，就算最后没中标，POC 的钱也能赚到，他们会找市面上很多家出方案，因为一旦引入产品之后替换成本很高。

Monica

你现在在日本招了几位当地的员工？

Jack

目前先招了一个当地的负责人，我们刚开始，主要是我跟他配合，日本有些事情我们不好做，比如拜访客户，我不会日语，没办法跟客户好好交流，售前售后也需要日语沟通，日本人之间交流肯定更顺畅。

Monica

这种人是通过什么渠道找到的？

Jack

是朋友介绍的。公开招聘也有，但比较难，因为日本人不太愿意去中国公司，对比美国公司、日本公司和中国公司，他们肯定优先选择前两者。

Monica

吸引合适的人主要靠什么？

Jack

跟国内一样，他们会看这个事情能做多久，你是不是真的想进入这个市场。

如果产品特别牛逼、特别火，即使是中国公司也能吸引很多人，但我们现在还不是这类，所以要向他们证明产品不错，从用户里面找也是不错的选择，因为他们本身用你的产品。

观众

企业购买产品整个决策流程是怎么样的？

Jack

类似 PPT、POC 然后推进，这都是学的欧美的，中国和日本的这套流程基本上都是从欧美传过来的。

费用的事情一开始就会聊，比如咨询做多久、开多少次会、每次会多少钱、怎么算，比如每次开会一个人，咨询费用 2000 块钱，就按开会次数报价，这些钱对他们来讲都是小钱。

Monica

你觉得什么样的赛道和产品，或者什么样的 AI 创业者适合进入日本市场，或者怎么判断自己适不适合进入日本市场？

Jack

判断的话，日本市场跟欧美市场没有太大区别，就像美国现在最火的产品在国内也有大量市场一样，只不过有些需求要结合当地情况。

如果很了解当地需求，针对当地需求去做是 OK 的，比如海外一些热门餐厅订餐服务，日本的订餐服务做得不是那么好，游客多，餐厅不会接那么多电话，服务员也不会英语或中文，只能日语，很多人希望有解决订餐问题的产品，就有人做，而且做得还行，从大需求来讲没有太大地域差异。

真要创业做日本市场，看行业里最赚钱的产品和赛道就行，如果想做小一点、精细化一点、竞争没那么激烈的，就像刚才讲的，找到当地的小需求，最好在日本待两个月，把当地业态走一遍，会发现很多机会。

而且日本的 IT 系统和智能化普及程度没有那么高，老龄化严重，很多养老院希望升级改造，变得更智能，比如用 AI 指导老人运动，他们要求量身定做，不是给养老院，而是给每个老人，根据老人的具体情况提供运动指导，不是通用动画，这种需求非常细分、垂直、个性化，里面有很多 AI 可以做的事情，都是比较小的机会。

中国做市场需要做一个大的、刚需的、别人绕不开的产品才能收钱，但日本不一样，做一个很小的事情大家也愿意给钱，因为收入到了一定程度，对个性化有要求，可能界面不一样、体验有点不同，也愿意花溢价购买。

观众

国内的 AI 初创公司想根植 ToC 产品，想扎根日本，拿日本的风投是否现实？

Jack

现实，但要结合日本的产业，做针对日本特性的产品，拿日本的钱会比较容易。

日本公司在国内每年会搞一些创业比赛，针对他们的产业问题，让中国公司参赛，觉得好的话就会考虑投资，在日本，IT 互联网的风投占比并不是特别高，更多的是传统实业的投资，相当于放贷。

因为日本的线下商业非常发达，一条街 100 个店，各个店都不一样，而国内一条街可能只有 10 个店，七八家是连锁店，业态不同导致他们的线下投资比国内好，更稳定，所以日本的传统投资机构没必要投高风险的事情，就算投高风险的，也更愿意投美国公司，因为有更好的退出路径，所以最好的方式是做产业投资，配合他们的产业做事。

观众

公司雇佣自己在日本工作，前期怎么样估算自己在日本的创业成本，如何估算未来的收益？

Jack

这位朋友可能是本身在那边打工然后想创业，从后半句来看，自己创业的话，首先看创什么业，互联网还是线下？

线下就不讲了，互联网的话，刨开注册公司的费用，成本主要是自己，现在日本有很多数字游民、做独立产品的，如果雇佣一个日本人加自己，自己的成本算在国内公司，日本人的成本从海外收入里扣减，日本员工如果是区域负责人，税前七八十万人民币一年，公司层面乘以 1.5。

在日本很难省成本，尤其是招人，成本很高，补充一下，日本招人很多靠猎头，不管高中低端，猎头收费按年薪（含奖金）的 30%-50%，非常高，所以日本上市公司排第四的是做名片的，前三都是搞人力、招聘、劳务派遣的，日本几乎是全民就业状态，就业率接近 100%，还有很多岗位空缺。

最近几年有很多中国人或东南亚人去那边打工，所以做人力招聘可能也有机会，很多人在那边做猎头。

观众

日本员工容易“背刺”老板吗？

Jack

应该是指劳务纠纷，日本对员工的劳动保护做得很好，但有点矫枉过正。

比如很难裁掉员工，即使表现不好，首先要证明员工表现不好，然后要帮他改进，证明用尽一切办法都无法让他表现好，才有可能辞退。

而且辞退后员工告公司，大概率员工胜诉，胜诉后还能回公司，所以很多公司宁可不辞退，把员工放在那里，日本招聘的面试很多，最少 5 轮，多的七八轮、九轮，因为招人的试错成本很高。

观众

日本的中国人和日本人会靠外貌分辨对方吗？
