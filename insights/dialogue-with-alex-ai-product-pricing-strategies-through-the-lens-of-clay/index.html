Three Major Pricing Misconceptions: The Most Common Pitfalls for Entrepreneurs

Monica

In your many years of pricing consulting, what do you think are the most common pricing misconceptions?

Alex

If we talk about entrepreneurs, the first profound realization is: don't sell your product too cheaply.

When starting a business, everyone knows pricing is important, but they often treat it as an offensive weapon, using price to win orders. There's nothing wrong with that. However, investment institutions look at the other side: can the company consistently generate profits and sustain itself through its pricing or sales capabilities? When I spoke with Americans, they said Warren Buffett looks at several dimensions when evaluating a company, and one of them is pricing power, especially the company's moat. Since the capital tide receded and everyone started paying more attention to self-sustaining capabilities, the importance of pricing has become even more prominent.

So, the first misconception is that people's understanding of pricing should shift slightly from an offensive weapon – it's a double-edged sword that can hurt others, or protect oneself.

The second misconception is pricing too low. For a company, two values are crucial:

One is cost, and the other is willingness to pay. Any price between these two ranges is feasible. If the price is a bit higher, the customer profits less, and I profit more; if it's in the middle, both share the profit. But the prerequisite is that both willingness to pay and cost must be clearly calculated. However, for a startup, it's often difficult to calculate clearly.

First, what is our own cost? Especially for startups, what exactly is the cost? People often don't have a clear concept. Some companies can calculate explicit costs, such as how much money was invested, office space, personnel, and investment in development tools and platforms.

I once asked a founder: Do you know your own costs? He said he didn't cost anything. I told him his own time should also be counted as a company expense.

Second is willingness to pay. When we do business in China, we rarely try to understand how much customers are willing to pay for something. When both these values are difficult to determine, discussing pricing, even with a good methodology – setting it between the two values – becomes uncertain because both values are uncertain, the middle value is uncertain, and everything is uncertain, turning into what we commonly call "shooting from the hip."

The last misconception is that many companies or startups come to me and say, "Could you take a look at our pricing?" And it always boils down to one question: How much should this thing sell for? Is it 99 yuan, 199 yuan, or 299 yuan? From a methodological and systematic perspective, pricing is not just about how much to sell for, but rather, based on case studies, what dimensions to use for selling. We'll talk about an AI product later.

A company everyone is familiar with is Michelin. Michelin used to sell tires individually. Later, they realized the Chinese market was difficult because tire prices were very low. What to do? They conducted a survey, and the story is quite interesting: Who are the customers? They are commercial vehicle logistics companies that buy tires. They asked customers: Why do you buy my tires?

Customers said: Your tires are durable, require less frequent changes, and I want my vehicles to run longer. This is your value.

During one customer interview, they didn't even ask how much customers were willing to pay, but based on this, they gained important information: the product value customers recognized was "longer running time." So, if you look up this case, you'll find that Michelin's current pricing method is based on mileage. Of course, tires can still be sold individually, but customers are willing to pay more for longer running time. They linked the pricing dimension to the value point recognized by customers, making sales much smoother.

So, when discussing pricing, don't limit the strategic dimensions to a few small points, such as "how much should it sell for," just because it's a strategy. Open up your thinking; there are many dimensions, and small adjustments in different dimensions can lead to differentiation.

Monica

This is particularly interesting. In the AI stage, AI products need an even more open mindset because many product forms and value points are new species.

Before the live stream, we discussed that the cost of AI products decreases incrementally. I was thinking, besides a price increase strategy, there might also be a price reduction strategy. When should prices be reduced, and by how much?

A thought just flashed through my mind: very few companies ask me about price reduction strategies; they usually only ask about how to increase prices.

Generally, people think price reduction is simple, right? The lowest is free. I wonder why Monica would ask this question? We can start with an analogy. Besides SaaS, I've also worked on some pricing projects. An established company approached me for a pricing project. I asked him how he priced his products. He said it was cost-plus: something sells for 100 yuan, earns 10 yuan profit, so it sells for 110 yuan. Pricing was handled by the finance department.

Later, I asked him: Do you know what the team next to you is doing? They are racking their brains to cut costs, reducing them by three or five percentage points for suppliers every year, or working hard themselves. I said your company invested, let's say, one or two million to cut costs, and reduced them by three yuan annually. How do you apply your cost-plus method then? He said according to the new cost.

Doesn't that mean the team worked hard for nothing, just to benefit the users?

I said if you're willing to do that, it's fine; that's the company's vision.

But if you look at it from a financial perspective, how much money was earned? It doesn't seem to have changed much.

Regarding Monica's question, I thought of a point: today, the customer buys for 110 yuan. Tomorrow, if it's still 110 yuan, the customer doesn't care about your costs, nor do they know what your costs are; they only care if they're willing to continue buying. If others in the industry haven't dropped the price by three yuan, you don't need to either. If there's no such necessity, can we think differently?

From the perspective of my model just now, the customer's willingness to pay at 110 yuan has already been validated.

It's just that the market situation might have changed, and competitors are forcing you to lower prices, so you do. If not, do you not need to? Because you already have this anchor point of willingness to pay. He said that's right.

I said actually, you could try. If you want to give up some, it's not 110, maybe 109. You save three yuan, take two yuan profit for yourself, and give one yuan to the consumer; that's not impossible.

Returning to Monica's question, for AI, perhaps the token price drop is not like that company where customers have information asymmetry. It's likely common knowledge that tokens are becoming cheaper. So, for startups building AI models, when token prices drop, everyone will definitely ask you how prices should be adjusted.

I think this question can be understood this way: because everyone knows. Based on our past experience,

If we start from the user's perspective – regardless of whether the company makes or loses money, and ignoring costs for a moment – let's say the product improves user efficiency by 10%. If they usually earn 100 yuan, today they can earn an extra ten yuan, and they are willing to pay me an extra five yuan. In the future, even if the number of tokens decreases, it essentially doesn't affect their work efficiency. Theoretically, they should still pay me five yuan, because it's just my own efficiency improving.

This might be a good starting point for thinking about this issue: from the user's perspective, how much value does the product actually generate, which determines how high the product can be priced. This might be a value point that can be discussed independently of cost. However, I don't rule out that in the future, consumers will ask: your tokens have gone down, so why is it still so expensive?

Clay Achieved 10x Growth Through Pricing Strategy

Monica

Next, we're going to talk about the Clay case. One very interesting aspect of its pricing is the concept of "credits." This is quite insightful.

Alex, could you briefly introduce Clay, its market niche, and its general pricing strategy?

Alex

I noticed this product after OpenAI came out, and it became very popular.

What did Clay do before? The founder initially created a productivity tool. He realized that every time he wanted to reach a customer, he had to scrape customer information from LinkedIn, put it into an Excel spreadsheet, and then process the data, which was very troublesome. So, when he started his business in 2018, he created a data enrichment plugin that scraped information from LinkedIn or similar platforms to help customers with prospecting.

Later, he released a desktop application, which essentially still did data enrichment, not limited to LinkedIn, integrating company phone numbers and emails. By the end of 2022, after OpenAI emerged, it gave the company a huge boost. He essentially created a spreadsheet, similar to Feishu's multi-dimensional table, where users could give it a list of target company names, and AI would extract relevant data for each row, such as CEO, founding information, contact details, etc.

To enrich the information, it allows users to add columns to the multi-dimensional table. For example, adding a column: "I want to know what the recent user reviews for this company are like on social media." It calls the Google API to check reviews, or Facebook for reviews on the official website. Using OpenAI's capabilities, it scores these reviews as positive or negative feedback, telling sales personnel: this company has very low positive feedback and very high negative feedback, and these are the keywords. This is beneficial for sales personnel, improving their work efficiency. They know which companies are target customers, and if they need to make ten calls this week, these ten are essential.

Because it's particularly suitable for our product, it's essentially a tool for personal efficiency improvement.

I've studied its pricing strategy. When he first developed the data enrichment application, there was no OpenAI yet. The founder's pricing anchor point was around two to three hundred US dollars. Looking at Clay's pricing today, after the free version, it immediately goes to 150 US dollars a month, which includes some credits.

My understanding is that the initial MVP gave him some price anchor points: customers had a willingness to pay around two to three hundred US dollars for the efficiency improvement in reaching potential customers.

Initially, it served individual users. Later, when developing the AI product, it first used a PLG (Product-Led Growth) approach, and then used sales for enterprise customers. In his pricing strategy, he first divided it into five tiers: from the simplest free version, to Premium, to enterprise customization. Prices range from over one hundred US dollars to over three hundred US dollars, with the enterprise version being custom.

The reason is that he initially served small individual users paying two or three hundred US dollars, so the initial niche market targeted this segment. The five pricing packages can be understood as five matching stages in a company's growth process. He bundled the products, gradually packaging them to meet the efficiency needs of lead acquisition at each stage of a company's growth. This is the first interesting point.

Second, the product has many features. There are five levels, and each level has different features. For example, the free version has no AI features, and data enrichment can only enrich 1-2 data sources. It gradually stacks up, with data sources increasing from 10, 20, to 100, which can be scraped from Google, as well as some paid data. AI capabilities range from no AI, to simple AI, to customizable AI workflows that can perform more complex logical judgments. The last tier is integration: individual users don't need integration, but enterprise users need integration with Gmail, Email, and it can even automatically write emails for you, so you just copy and paste.

With so many product features, he initially thought that pricing each feature separately would be particularly complex because the product was growing, and data sources were increasing. Every new data source would require pricing, and the user's cognitive cost would also be very high. So, he implemented a credit mechanism, a points or token mechanism. It's worth noting that when he did this, the traditional AI product points mechanism had not yet appeared.

This points mechanism, firstly, allows the value generated by the user to be linked to consumption and payment – the more data sources enriched, the more points consumed.

Secondly, it effectively avoids the complexity of pricing every new feature during product iteration. It makes user conversion very easy to understand, payment simple, and linked to value.

I read an article at the time, written by the founder himself, which sounds particularly reasonable now. He said, why not use Notion's seat-based pricing? Because Notion is a collaborative office tool, it wants more people to collaborate, so it can sell seat-based pricing; theoretically, the more people, the more it can scale. But we are actually helping individual users improve efficiency; there's no situation where ten people become a hundred people, it should be ten people becoming one person. Our scenario is that users only have one spreadsheet, but that spreadsheet becomes very complex, adding many data points and columns. So, we should link product pricing to such value, not seats. Seats might not be the best fit for users because it's essentially an efficiency tool. It still sounds reasonable now, but I think it was quite brilliant and thorough for the founder to think that way at the time.

Monica

Let me explain: Clay is a sales data middle office, and its core function is to replace the SDR role.

In overseas B2B companies, there's a division of labor: SDRs (Sales Development Representatives) are responsible for collecting leads, which they then pass to AEs (Account Executives), who can be understood as more senior sales personnel. The AEs then contact these leads. Clay precisely targets improving SDR efficiency, and may even replace the SDR role.

Its ROI is quite easy to measure: after using the product, what is the cost per lead? What was the approximate cost when an SDR did it? Data previously showed that it could help users reduce the CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost) per lead by 75%.

Because it's relatively easy to measure, users are more likely to make a payment decision.

For SDRs in the US, I've helped friends recruit similar roles before, and their monthly salary is roughly two to three thousand, or three to four thousand US dollars. So, from this perspective, it's easy to measure the cost per lead. Tools that improve the efficiency of a specific role like this sound like great entrepreneurial opportunities. Clay's pricing strategy is very instructive.

Alex

Thank you, Monica, for explaining it so clearly. There's another point that startups can learn from: Clay had already launched its AI-powered product in 2022, but there was no billing on the website at that time.

Billing started in early 2023, and growth from 2022 to 2023 was tenfold, and from 2023 to 2024, it was another tenfold growth, which is very impressive. Of course, the base might have been small before. But going back to what we just said, for pricing, you don't need to put a structured price on the website right away. Referring to Clay, it's necessary to use some angel customers or MVPs for testing.

Later, he encountered another interesting point. We talked about five service packages, ranging from over one hundred US dollars to over three hundred US dollars. Later, Clay contacted several enterprise customers, some paying thousands of US dollars, some paying 100,000 US dollars.

When he first started with enterprise customers, he asked himself: Do I need two sets of pricing strategies? He used to do PLG (Product-led Growth) and small, self-service products. Did he need two sets for enterprise customers? At the time, they decided not to complicate things and use one pricing strategy. But he went through some detours in enterprise pricing.

The first detour: the initial enterprise orders were tens of thousands of yuan, which was already very high compared to a few hundred US dollars. But after taking them, he found a problem: it conflicted with existing channel partners. Because when Clay first started, he found 30 influencers, who specialized in sales, to be Clay's influencers. The product wasn't that easy to use and intuitive at first, so he had these 30 partners teach customers how to use Clay. He was only responsible for PLG.

When taking orders of sixty or eighty thousand, he realized that enterprise customers wanted him to do everything from start to finish. He said this pricing strategy wouldn't work because it harmed the interests of the existing 30 partners; they would lose business. They are our partners, and we must protect them. So what to do?

He said we must show that enterprises are different from general individual users, but still maintain consistency with the original pricing strategy. Later, they came up with a strategy called a base fee: in the bundled strategy, enterprise customers had to first buy a base fee, and then additional features would be charged based on demand. At least this showed that general users didn't have a base fee, while enterprise users did, thus differentiating them.

But during the sales process, he found that he couldn't sell many enterprise orders because others would always challenge him: What is this base fee? What exactly does it serve? What does it do?

Monica

It seems like a strange existence.

Alex

Right, this was their experience during their startup phase. In order to keep the overall pricing strategy unchanged, but to differentiate small users from enterprise users and earn tens of thousands of dollars in profit from enterprises, while small users paid hundreds of dollars, he had to explain something. So he set a base fee for himself. However, the order conversion rate was particularly low, and he was challenged by procurement every time: what exactly are these things? How do they improve our efficiency? Why do we have this fee?

Conversely, there's another point, as the founder himself said in his sharing, this base fee was also difficult to increase in the second year. If the base fee is 100 US dollars today, and 105 US dollars tomorrow, how do you explain it to the customer?

So the story ultimately comes back to what we just discussed: it has a subscription model, but the subscription model is not a base fee concept; it's a subscription model that gives you points. Enterprise customers pay a higher subscription fee and get more points. For large enterprise customers, it's negotiated on a case-by-case basis, with the last column on the website being "enterprise customer," and prices are customized and not publicly displayed. This is the pricing challenge he encountered when transitioning from small and medium-sized enterprise customers, from PLG, to wanting to do SLG (Sales-led Growth) for large enterprise and key account customers. I find it very interesting.

Monica

This is very typical. Many SaaS products initially take the PLG route, and after gaining strength, they all want to do SLG and target large customers. This is when they face pricing strategy issues. But I think Clay's credit concept is very smart; it helped him resolve many related conflicts.

Alex

Making that conversion, sales also become very simple. We include so many features, and there are these points; based on your usage, you can decide which ones you want. Sometimes it can even lead to upselling, because customers need more points and must pay a higher subscription fee.

Monica

It's like he created his own economic system, with a basic currency that can circulate within the product system.

Token Price Drop, How AI Products Reprice

Monica

Let's discuss pricing strategies further, starting from the Clay case.

We just talked about the Clay case. What's good about it is that its ROI and value points are very clear; you know how much labor cost is replaced by using 200 US dollars. However, many current AI startup products have unclear value points, such as those related to emotional value or personal knowledge bases, where the value points are very vague. In such cases, there's a significant discrepancy in value perception between the founding team and the customers/users. How do you approach pricing in this situation?

Alex

The idea that good wine needs no bush is debatable, or rather, not quite right, from a pricing perspective.

Because we, as product developers, always feel that our product is very valuable and can bring this and that value to customers. But when observing customer usage, either they don't know how to use it, or they don't perceive the value during the initial demo.

What is pricing based on? It's not based on the value we perceive, but on the value customers recognize or perceive. This is also why when selling a product, onboarding customers is crucial from the start, to deliver value immediately. Or in sales pitches and marketing, to make customers feel the value. So, perceived value is quite important.

The solutions are nothing more than traditional marketing methods:

content marketing, success stories, finding influencers to solve the problem.

To increase perception, perhaps they didn't feel it, but after being told, the feeling is deeper, and the perceived value is higher. Another situation is that they don't know how to use it, for example, they haven't discovered a certain feature of the product, and they feel a large chunk of value is missing, asking why it's so expensive? In this case, many AI products are now doing use cases. The homepage tells you what others' use cases are, or on YouTube, Bilibili, Xiaohongshu, many people share how to use a certain AI tool to write PPTs, etc. At least when some scenarios emerge, people feel that it can indeed solve some problems. This is about perceived value.

So, often, pricing is one thing, but it's inseparable from product, marketing, and sales; many things are interconnected. Perceived value is very important, because if there's no perceived value, it's very difficult to get money from customers' pockets. If customers don't feel the value, what then? I think 30-40% of it might be on the PMF side, so they haven't felt the value.

Let me use a PMF thermometer we used before. To achieve PMF, first, the product and market must be adapted, and it must be scalable. Many times, what we call PMF, I personally think is problem-solution fit: customers have a problem, and I can solve it. But whether they are willing to pay is another matter. So, many times, what we do is this fit.

In fact, after problem-solution fit, there is also product-model fit, which is the adaptation of the product and the business model: not only solving customer problems, but also customers being willing to pay for this problem. This is the next level. The final level is PMF: not only can they pay, but I can find such customers in large numbers repeatedly, replicate them, and achieve economies of scale.

So, I personally think that to solve Monica's question, when conducting PMF user interviews, it is first necessary to clarify what kind of problem it solves for them: is it an optional problem, or a very urgent problem? The willingness to pay will definitely be different.

Second, we often have this problem when developing products: I help you solve problems, we are very good, so you should pay. I would suggest, when developing products, we say the product helps you solve such problems, or uses technical means to solve them, how much are you willing to pay for this problem? This question or awareness should be present in early communication with customers. Whether it's developing new products or managing product requirements, I personally think it should be there.

Because if we ask customers if they need such a feature, they will definitely say yes. But if we ask customers if they are willing to pay an extra ten or twenty yuan for this feature, the answer might be different. So, when doing PM, don't forget to incorporate pricing, turning it into the concept of PPMF.

Monica

Pricing PMF – pricing also needs to find PMF.

Product plus price truly constitutes the product.

As you just said, to ask how much people are willing to pay for such a product, based on your experience, how many users do you think you need to ask before you dare to set a price?

Alex

If it's in the early stage, first, ensure the user persona is very precise and homogeneous. If they are heterogeneous, a larger data volume will be required. If it's within the same niche, about 20 to 25 users will provide some convergent insights or a sense for product pricing. We talk about how this product helps customers solve this problem, what value it brings, and how much this value is worth in RMB.

YC Model: Pricing, an Underestimated Growth Lever

Monica

I'd like to ask Alex to break down the YC model for us.

In our regular operational level, optimizing customer acquisition efficiency and user retention by 1% only brings 3.3% and 6.7% revenue growth, respectively. However, optimizing price by 1% can bring 12.7% revenue growth. So, from this logic, we see that price optimization is much higher than improving retention and customer acquisition efficiency.

How do you view this model?

Alex

First of all, it's an impossible triangle: it's difficult to achieve customer acquisition, retention, and monetization simultaneously. Depending on the company's development stage, it may need to focus on certain points. If a company is currently focused on profit and not on user growth, from a profit perspective, pricing has the most direct impact on profit.

We can create a simple mathematical model: if a company sells a product for 100 yuan, with fixed costs, it earns a profit. Optimizing each dimension that affects profit, for example, selling for five yuan more or saving 5% on costs. Overall, the conclusion is that pricing has the most direct impact on profit optimization. Because once the price is set, looking at the top line of the income statement, revenue, it will invariably flow down to the bottom line, profit, without any intermediate impact. So, pricing directly affects profit. Conversely, the impact of cost is indirect, especially variable costs.

The last time I heard a YC sharing, it was very similar to our theoretical model calculations. YC said that in the impossible triangle, if a company is currently focused on profit, optimizing each dimension – for example, spending 1% more on customer acquisition, doing 1% more on retention, or selling for 1% more – the impact on profit, according to their statistics, is that a 1% optimization in monetization can lead to a 12.7% increase in profit. This is easy to understand: a 1% increase in customer acquisition definitely requires cost investment, so the profit impact is not that high. If pricing is done well, as just mentioned, an extra dollar directly translates to profit.

The next question is what Monica asked: When does increasing prices lead to so much customer churn? This is actually also a mathematical formula. Every time prices are increased or decreased, users will definitely change. Company revenue can be calculated, and elasticity can be calculated: how much profit does the company make if the price is increased by 1%? When the marginal profit growth becomes lower and lower, that might be the point to watch out for. YC has an empirical formula that I was told, which you can refer to: stop increasing prices when customer churn reaches 20%.

Monica

From this perspective, YC strongly encourages price increases.

But as you just said, price is set between cost and the value recognized by the user. Would you encourage AI products to price at the very top?

Alex

I wouldn't really encourage pricing at the very top.

But there must be one point of view: in the US or abroad, Clay's model works because their SDR costs are high, as you mentioned, three to four thousand US dollars is quite expensive. Compared to domestic labor, this is a natural external condition. Essentially, a lot of work involves spreadsheets, and we just replaced the spreadsheet work, so it works.

From a domestic perspective, especially for startups, I personally think it depends on different stages. When a company is just starting, it must be user-centric, and pricing too high is not conducive to customer acquisition. So Monica's question can be explained this way: everyone can consider what pricing endpoint (highest value) should be the ultimate goal. Based on this endpoint, what should the price be today? And how should each step in the future achieve that price point? For example, if the endpoint is 100 yuan, today it's set at 20 yuan because of customer acquisition, or it's free. But to reach 100 yuan in the future, what kind of product planning should be done, what value should be achieved, and how many steps should be taken to increase the price?

Compared to price reductions, price increases are more difficult. It's easy to drop the price by ten yuan, but it's harder to raise it back. In particular, I strongly advise against dropping a psychological price point. For example, if a product is 219 yuan, dropping it to 199 yuan or 189 yuan, it will be very difficult to raise the price again because it crosses the 200 yuan psychological price point.

Sequoia Capital: Three PMF Paradigms to Re-understand Product and Market

Monica

Sequoia this year proposed three major PMF paradigms.

The first stage is called "Hair on Fire," which is the most urgent need stage.

The second stage is "Hard Fact."

The third stage is "Future Vision."

I think AI products fit these three needs very well. Some AI products, like Clay, solve customer acquisition problems, which is a very urgent need. Some might be based on facts, replacing traditional SOPs. The future vision might be selling what it can bring to the enterprise in the future.

Alex, how do you view the different stages, and how do the pricing strategies for products evolve and adjust across these three stages?

Alex

It's actually a reverse distillation and summary of the AI products seen this year.

The first one is urgent needs, hair on fire.

The second is called Hard Fact. What is Hard Fact? It means that someone is already doing this, but in layman's terms, can you do it better? What is the future vision? It means customers don't even realize this thing will come out, or that they might become your potential customers. That is, they don't even know it themselves, and then you tell them, pitch them, and influence them to move in this direction. This is called future vision.

So, for the most part, I see that all of these can be categorized into these three types of PMF.

But why did I specifically look at this Sequoia article and bring it up? Because I think everyone has been talking about PMF.

In fact, everyone focuses a lot on the "M" part, the market. For example, is this urgent need something customers are actively seeking? Are they eager to pay? Are there no suppliers, and so on?

However, we haven't paid enough attention to the "P" part, the product side. And PMF talks about the fit between product and market. So, Sequoia's three PMFs each provide a few questions to structure the product side.

I think everyone can try to answer the three questions within each PMF.

For example, for urgent needs, customers are indeed actively seeking such solutions. We say the market is like this, but the questions it asks are all very good. First, you are different from others. Customers are comparing solutions. Are you different from others, and can customers immediately understand this difference? Can they recognize or perceive this differentiation, as Monica said? Finally, is your product faster than others? In fact, when we do PMF, if we focus too much on the "M" and always think there's a market, but we don't look at our "P," then PMF might not be done well, or only half done.

That's why this matter is relatively complex.

To answer Monica's question just now, I think we also need to focus on the product side. Regarding pricing strategy, I'll use the first example. Before the broadcast, Monica and I were discussing, and a few days ago, there was also a situation where we had a product, there was a market, and there were competitors. And our strategy might have been cost-plus, as mentioned earlier. Another method is to look at competitors' prices and then set our price.

Let's take the "urgent need" PMF as an example. If we can tell customers the differentiation of our product, regardless of the specific point, and this differentiation can generate value, then we might be able to command a higher willingness to pay and a premium for it.

For example, how to understand this. Today, a good friend asked me: their company makes an AI product, and foreign companies have a global pricing, and China also has pricing. But it seems that Chinese competitors are all free, so how to deal with this? I said I couldn't help him solve it without more in-depth understanding, but you can look at it this way: everyone knows there's free water, there's Nongfu Spring, but some people still drink Evian and more expensive VOSS, right? They are all water, and their functions are the same.

三大定价误区：创业者最常犯的坑

Monica

在你多年定价咨询中，你觉得最常见的定价误区是什么？

Alex

如果拿创业者来讲，第一个深刻感受是：不要把你的东西卖得太便宜。

创业时，大家知道价格重要，但常把它当作进攻型武器，通过价格拿订单，这没错。但对投资机构，他们看另一面：企业是否能持续通过定价或销售能力获得利润、持续造血。我和美国人聊时，他们说巴菲特看公司有几个维度，其中一个就是定价权，特别是公司的护城河。自从资本潮退去，大家越来越关注自我造血能力后，定价的重要性就更凸显。

所以第一个误区是，大家对定价的理解应从进攻武器稍微转变意识——它是双刃剑，既可伤别人，也可能伤自己或保护自己。

第二个误区是定价太低。对于企业，两个值很关键：

一是成本，二是支付意愿。只要价格在这两个区间之间都可行。定价高一点，客户获利少一点，我获利多一点；定在中间，双方均分利润。但前提是支付意愿和成本都要核算清楚。可对创业公司，往往很难算清。

第一，我们自己的成本是多少？尤其对于初创企业，成本到底是多少？大家常没明确概念。有些企业能算清显性成本，比如投了多少钱、办公室面积、人员、开发工具平台的投入。

我后来问一个创始人：你知道你自己的成本吗？他说自己不要钱。我说你自己的时间也应该算在公司开销里。

第二是支付意愿。我们在中国做业务时，很少去了解客户愿意为这东西付多少钱。当这两个值都难确定时，再去讨论定价，方法论虽好——定在两个值之间——但就会因为两个值都不确定，中间值也不确定，所有东西都很不确定，变成俗话说的拍脑袋。

最后一个误区是，很多企业或初创企业找我时说：“定价这事儿，你帮我们看看呗。”最后就落在一个点：这东西到底该卖多少钱？到底是99元、199元还是299元。从方法论和体系讲，定价不单单是卖多少钱这一件事，而是结合案例，以什么维度去卖。待会儿我们会讲一个 AI 产品。

大家比较熟悉的公司叫米其林。米其林以前卖轮胎可以一条条卖。后来发觉中国市场很难做，因为中国轮胎价格很低，怎么办？他们做了一个调研，故事挺有意思：客户是谁？是那些买轮胎的商用车物流公司。他们问客户：你为什么会买我的轮胎？

客户说：你的轮胎耐用，换轮胎时间少，我希望车跑得更久。这是你的价值。

一次客户访谈，还没问客户愿意付多少，但基于此，他们获得一个重要信息：客户认可的产品价值是“开得久”。所以大家查这个案例会发现，米其林现在的定价方法是按公里数。当然轮胎仍可以单个卖，但客户愿意为开得更久付更多钱。他们把定价维度和客户认可的价值点结合挂钩，卖起来就顺畅很多。

所以讨论定价时，不要因为它是策略，就把策略维度锁定在几个很小的点上，比如“应该卖多少钱”。把思路打开，维度其实很多，在不同维度上稍作调整，可能会有差异化出来。

回到 Monica 这个问题，对 AI 来讲，可能 Token 下降不像那家公司，客户有信息差。Token 变得更便宜可能是大家都知道的事。所以创业公司做 AI 模型，Token 下降，大家肯定会来问你价格应该怎么调？

我觉得这个问题可以这样理解：因为大家都知道。结合我们之前经验，

如果我们从用户视角出发——不管公司赚钱多还是亏钱多，先不管成本，先说产品帮助用户提高了 10% 的效率。他平时赚 100 块钱，今天能多赚十块钱，愿意多付我五块钱。未来 Token 数量下降，本质对他工作效率没影响，他理论上还应继续付我五块钱，因为只不过是我自身效率提升的情况。

这可能是思考这个问题比较好的切入点：从用户角度去看，产品到底产生多大价值，决定产品能定多高的价格。这可能是能脱离成本讨论的一个价值点。但我不排除未来消费者会探讨：你的 Token 下降了，为什么还那么贵？

Clay 靠定价策略做到 10 倍增长

Monica

我们接下来要聊 Clay 这个案例。它的定价里很有趣的一点是有 credit 的概念。这蛮有启发的。

Alex 先帮大家简要介绍 Clay 产品、它的市场生态位和大致定价策略。

Alex

我注意到这个产品是在 OpenAI 出来后，它变得非常火。

Clay 之前做什么？创始人最早做了一个生产力工具。他发觉每次触达客户，都要去领英上把客户信息抓下来，填在 Excel 表格里，再做数据处理，特别麻烦。所以 18 年创业时，他做了一个数据增强插件，在领英或类似平台上把信息抓下来，帮助客户做拓展。

后来他出了一款桌面应用，本质还是在做数据增强，不限于领英，把企业的电话号码、邮件都整合进来。到 2022 年底，OpenAI 出来后，对公司是个非常大的助推。他本质做了一个电子表格，类似飞书的多维表格，用户可以扔给他一串目标企业的名字，通过 AI 在每一行抓取企业相关的 CEO、成立信息、联系方式等数据。

为了让信息更丰富，它允许用户在多维表格里增加列。比如增加一列：想知道这家公司最近在社交媒体上的用户评论怎么样。调用 Google API 看评论，或 Facebook 对官网的评论。通过 OpenAI 能力，把这些评论做正负反馈打分，告诉销售人员：这家公司正面反馈很低，负面反馈很高，关键词是什么。这对销售人员是利好，提升了工作效率。他知道哪些企业是目标客群，这周要打十个电话，这十个是必须要打的。

因为它特别适合我们的产品，所以本质是做个人效率提升的工具。

它的定价策略我研究过，他最早做数据增强应用时，还没有 OpenAI，创始人收费的价格锚点在两三百美金左右。看 Clay 今天的定价，免费版本后，马上到 150 美金一个月送一些 credit。

我的理解是，最早那款 MVP 给了他一些价格锚点：客户为实现触达潜客的效率提升，大概有两三百美金的付费意愿。

一开始做个人用户，后来做 AI 产品时，先用 PLG（Product-Led Growth） 形式驱动，后边用销售做大客户。在他的定价策略里，首先分了五档：从最简单的免费，到 Premium，到企业定制。价格从一百多美金到三百多美金，企业版是定制。

原因在于，他一开始服务的是那种两三百美金的小个体用户，所以一开始的细分市场就打这个。五个价格包，可以理解成一家企业成长过程中五个匹配的阶段。他把产品打包，面对企业成长各阶段所要获取线索的效率，慢慢进行打包。这是第一点有意思的。

第二，产品有很多功能。五个级别，每个级别功能不一样。比如免费版没有 AI 功能，数据增强只能增强

1-2 个数据源。慢慢叠加上去，数据源从10个、20个到100个，可以从谷歌上抓，还有一些付费数据。AI 能力从没有 AI，到简单 AI，到可自定义工作流的 AI，可以做更复杂的逻辑判断。最后一档是集成：个人用户不需要集成，企业用户需要与 Gmail、Email 集成，还能帮你自动写好 Email，你只要复制粘贴。

这么多产品功能下，他一开始觉得每个功能分别定价会特别复杂，因为产品在增长，数据源在增加。每次新数据源都要定价，用户认知成本也很高。所以他做了一个 credit 机制，点数或积分机制。要知道，他做的时候传统 AI 产品的点数机制还没出现。

这种点数机制，第一能让用户产生的价值与消费、付费挂钩——增强的数据源越多，消耗点数越多。

第二能有效规避产品迭代中，每新增功能都需定价的复杂性。让用户转化时变得非常易懂，付费变得简单，且与价值挂钩了。

当时看过一篇文章，创始人自己说的，现在听起来特别有道理。他说为什么不用 Notion 的坐席制去卖？因为 Notion 是协同办公工具，希望越来越多人进来协同，所以能卖坐席制，理论上人越多越能 scale。而我们其实是帮单个用户提高效率的，不存在十个人变成一百个人，应该是十个人变成一个人。我们的场景是用户只有一张表格，但那张表格会变得非常复杂，会增加很多数据点和列。所以我们应该把产品定价与这样的价值挂钩，而不是坐席。坐席对用户可能不是最好的契合点，因为它本质是效率工具。现在听起来大家还觉得有道理，但创始人当时能想成这样，我觉得还是蛮厉害、蛮透彻的。

Monica

我帮大家解释一下：Clay 做的是销售数据中台，核心取代的岗位是 SDR。

海外 to B 企业里有这样的分工：SDR 专门收集 leads，给到 AE（Account Executive），可理解为较 senior 的 sales，他就可以拿着这个例子去跟客户联系。Clay 精准切中了提升 SDR 效率，甚至可能取代 SDR 的岗位。

Alex

酒香不怕巷子深这件事情从定价角度看有待商榷，或不太正确。

因为我们做产品的人，一直觉得产品很有价值，能为客户带来这样那样的价值。但看客户使用时，要不然他不会用，要不然没有上手 demo 时他感知不到价值。

定价基于什么？不是基于我们认为的价值，是基于客户认可或感知的价值。这也是为什么卖产品时，一上来 onboarding 客户很重要，要第一时间释放价值。或在销售话术、做 marketing 时，要让客户感受到价值。所以感知价值比较重要。

能解决的方法无外乎传统营销方法：

要提高感知，可能他没感觉到，但告诉他后，感觉更深刻了，感知价值更高。还有是他不会用，比如没发觉产品某个功能，就觉得价值缺了一大块，为什么你那么贵？这种情况下，就是很多 AI 产品现在在做 use case。首页告诉你别人的 use case 是什么，或在 YouTube、B站、小红书上，大量人分享怎么使用某个 AI 工具写 PPT 等。至少有些场景出来后，让人觉得的确能解决部分问题。这就是感知价值的事情。

所以很多时候定价虽是一件事，但离不开产品、营销、销售，很多东西都联动。感知价值挺重要，因为没感知到价值，从客户口袋里要钱非常困难。如果客户没感觉到价值，那怎么办？我觉得百分之三四十可能在 PMF 这边，所以他没感觉到价值。

我拿我们之前做过的一个 PMF 温度计来讲。要做到 PMF，首先产品和市场要有适配，且能 scale、能规模化。很多时候我们做的所谓 PMF，我个人觉得叫 problem-solution fit，即解决方案和问题的适配：客户有问题，我能解决。但他愿不愿意付费是另一码事。所以很多时候做的是这个 fit。

其实在 problem-solution fit 之后，还有一个 product-model fit，即产品和盈利模式的适配：不但帮客户解决问题，且客户愿为这问题付钱。这是再往上一层。最后一层才是 PMF，不仅能付钱，且这样的客户我能大量重复找到，能复制，实现规模化效益。

所以我个人觉得，解决 Monica 刚才问的问题，是在做 PMF 用户访谈时，首先要明确到底解决了他什么样的问题：是可有可无的问题，还是非常紧急的问题？支付意愿肯定不一样。

第二个，我们做产品时经常有这问题：我帮你解决问题，我们特别牛，所以你应该付钱。我反倒建议，在做产品时，我们说产品帮你解决这样的问题，或用技术手段解决，你愿意为这问题付多少钱？这个提问或意识要在前期与客户沟通时有。无论是做新产品，还是做产品需求管理时，我个人觉得都要有。

因为我们问客户需不需要这样的需求，客户肯定说需要。但问客户愿不愿意为这需求多花十元、二十元，很可能有不一样的答案。所以做 PM 时，别忘了把 pricing 放进去，变成 PPMF 的概念。

YC 模型：定价，是被低估的增长杠杆

Monica

我想请Alex来帮我们拆解一下YC的模型。

在我们的常规的运营层面， 获客效率和用户留存的优化1%，它只能带来3.3%和6.7%的收益，但价格优化1%能带来12.7%的收益增长。所以其实从这个逻辑上我们看到这个价格优化远远比提升留存和获客优化效率来得高很多。

怎么样去看这个模型？

Alex

首先它是一个不可能三角：同时做获客、留存、变现，这三件事很难同时做到。根据企业发展阶段不同，可能需要关注其中某几个点。如果企业当下关注利润，不关注用户数量增长，从利润角度讲，定价对利润的作用最直接。

可以做一个简单数学模型：如果一家企业卖 100 元价格，有固定成本，得到一个利润。在每个影响利润的维度上做浮动优化，比如多卖五块钱或节约 5% 成本。总的来说结论是，价格对利润的优化最直接。因为价格定完后，看财务报表最上面一行收入，会无一例外落到最底下利润，中间不受任何影响。所以价格对利润影响最直接。相反成本影响是间接的，尤其是可变成本。

上次听 YC 分享，跟我们的理论模型测算非常类似。YC 说在不可能三角里，如果企业当下注重利润，在每个维度上，比如获客多花 1%，留存多做 1%，或多卖 1% 价格，对利润的影响，根据他们统计，变现优化 1% 能做到 12.7% 的利润提升。这很好理解：获客多 1% 肯定要投入成本，所以利润影响没那么高。定价做好，就像刚才讲的，多一块钱就直接是利润。

下面一个问题就是 Monica 问的：提价提到什么时候客户都流失了？这其实也是一个数学公式。每次提价或降价，用户肯定会变。公司收益可以算出来，可以算弹性：多提 1% 价格，公司利润多少。当边际利润增长越来越低时，可能就是要注意的点。YC 有个经验公式我告诉大家，是听来的，大家可以参考：提价提到客户流失 20% 的时候，应该停。

Monica

从这个维度讲，YC 非常鼓励大家去提价。

但像刚才说的，价格是在成本和用户认可的价值中间去定。你会比较鼓励 AI 产品顶格去定吗？

Alex

但必须有一个观点：在美国或国外，Clay 的模式能成，是因为他们的 SDR 成本高，就像你讲的，三四千美金挺贵的。相比国内人工，是一个自然的外部条件。本质是大量工作做表格，只不过我们把做表格的事代替了，所以能成。

从国内来讲，尤其对初创企业，我个人觉得取决于不同阶段。企业刚起步时，肯定以用户为基础，定价太高不利于获客。所以 Monica 这个问题可以这样解释：大家可以思考，以什么样的定价终点（最高值）作为终点。基于这个终点，今天应该是什么价格？未来每一步应该怎样实现价格点。比如终点是 100 元，今天定 20 元，因为要获客，或是免费。但未来要做到 100 元，应该做怎样的产品规划，实现怎样的价值，分几步去提价。

相比于降价，提价会更难一点。降十块钱很容易，但涨回来比较难。尤其我非常不建议大家去降一个心理价位点，比如产品 219 元，降到 199 元或 189 元，再提价会非常困难，因为它会跨越 200 元这个心理价位点。

所以就算市场里有很多竞争对手，但只要它是紧迫需求，且产品有足够差异化，就能形成溢价。在水里可能是情感类溢价，或功能性溢价，或品牌型溢价。这时再看竞争对手定价，不是不可以，而是如果竞争对手价值能被量化，做到 100 分，我能做到 110 分，那我合理比他多十块钱或五块钱，是合理的。否则我们做得更好，价格更低，说不过去。

问题好专业，是做运营的同学吧。

之前一场闭门会分享，我正好讲了，是请了之前公司做定价的同学一起分享的，他非常有见解。我简单总结不展开：他当时有个任务，要给公司提价保证利润，用户又不能流失太多，所以去后台看用户行为数据。但提问题的同学可能他们公司比较全。

他当时看数据时发觉没有数据，又反过去跟产品做了很多埋点。这就是为什么定价和产品要联动。

埋完点后再做用户数据分析。数据分析要看每个企业、产品不一样，不太好一概而论。他们当时是给一个小企业做远程登录。看的维度有使用频次、协同人数等，定义一些维度，在这些维度上找到有潜力的高 LTV 客户。每个产品不一样，比较笼统。

观众

这套定价的方法和逻辑是否适用于所有行业，还是有行业偏好？

Alex

行业偏好肯定有。如果拿 to B 和 to C 来讲，to B 和 to C 就有偏好。刚才 Monica 讲，很多 to B 产品，尤其 Clay，特别适用于 to B。但那套方法论对 to C 可能有些制肘，就算是 to 小 B，也可能拿到情感、陪伴里，那是另一套逻辑。

但什么是不变的？最底层的核心观点不变：无论怎么做定价，一定要去挖掘产品的价值，这不会变。即使是情感陪伴，也产生情感价值，只不过我们不知道。第二，把这些价值进行量化，这件事不变。方法论也不变。比如情感价值，我举个例子，怎么为安全量化。

最典型的例子是自动驾驶。我们不讨论自动驾驶是不是安全，相对来讲，之前我看过一个数据：特斯拉 FSD 在北美数据显示，两次事故之间的行驶里程，FSD 是人类的六倍。也就是说它行驶六倍里程后才发生一次事故，人类驾驶员可能 100 公里，FSD 是 600 公里。

反过来讲，FSD 在北美是 99 美金包月。马斯克调过很多次价，假设它是 99 美金。问题就变成：你愿不愿意为六倍更安全付 99 美金？

回到情感陪伴也一样：要找到那个价值，然后要量化，量化后可定价。如果关于怎么量化，有一本书以前我读过，讲万物皆可量化。比如怎么量化婚姻是否幸福。

最重要的有两件事：第一要有一群样本，第二要有定义这事的维度。然后在这些维度上跟踪样本，做前后对比统计。我觉得这些应该是不变的。

Monica

你觉得哪些 AI 场景和产品比较适合以结果为导向去做定价或以效果定价，哪些不太适合？

Alex

先讲不太适合的。

第一，产品功能如果 to B 为主，用户一时半会搞不明白，需要一个团队支持他把功能全实现，或教他所有功能，有 onboarding 过程，实现第一价值后才有价值体现，这不太适合做结果导向。不可能一上来放那么多人服务每个客户。这种情况下，要大量服务的话，不适合做结果导向，因为价值不太能被体现，一旦被体现就要铺大量人力，除非是大客户另说。

还有哪些不太适合？经验之谈：基准线很难定的，不太适合。哪个基准线之上属于我的，哪个属于你的，哪个属于客户自己的，哪个属于产品带来的，不太好定。基准线难定，未来收费就很麻烦。

还有，基准线能定，但价值量化很难，不是那么好量化，不像商业利润就是利润，收益就是收益。定个基准线，你每月财务报表对我开放，但大家有不公平开放、不给我看，这种就比较难。比如情感陪伴类，每个月访谈你一次，看看满意度，这很难，没有公允值。还有一种，相对于 to B 的 sales SaaS 工具或 AI 工具，客户的采购不太愿意按效果付费。

Monica

Alex 你认为未来三年 AI 公司在定价上最大的变量是什么？是模型、算力、用户心智，还是物种变化？

Alex

我个人判断是，整个市场迭代太快，使得我们对整个价格体系可能一时半会要固化下来，形成一套非常体系化的方法论或体系，会比较困难。这可能是最大的变量：技术迭代太快，无论是模型还是 token。

Monica

最后再给 Alex 一个问题：对现在直播间的创业者，如果你对大家的定价优化策略只给一个建议，你的建议是什么？

Alex

我非常建议，因为定价还是我们刚才讲的，是集团军作战的概念。我相信大家也没有专门的人负责定价。我的一个策略是：大家在做 PMF 的时候，可以适当融入一些定价，至少能看清楚我们带来的价值有多大。

Monica
